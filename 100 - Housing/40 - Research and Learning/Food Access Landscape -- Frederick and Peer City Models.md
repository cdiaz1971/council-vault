# Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models

## Purpose

This note builds out the food domain of the Affordability Agenda tiering framework. It establishes the cost burden baseline from 2024 ALICE data, maps Frederick's existing food access conditions and gaps using USDA tract-level data, inventories the asset and program landscape, and documents peer city models available for adaptation. The note is structured to support both internal briefing and eventual legislative or budget action.

---

## The ALICE food burden baseline

Food is the third-largest ALICE Household Survival Budget line item for individuals and a major cost for families. Maryland 2024 Household Survival Budget figures:

| Household type | Monthly food cost |
|---|---|
| Single adult | $513 |
| Single senior | $473 |
| One adult, one school-age child | $868 |
| One adult, one childcare-age child | $779 |
| Two adults, two school-age children | $1,578 |
| Two adults, two childcare-age children | $1,392 |

These figures use the USDA Thrifty Food Plan with county-level variation from Feeding America. The full Maryland 2024 single adult survival budget totals $4,239/month ($50,868/year, requiring $25.43/hour). The under-65 ALICE threshold for Frederick County in 2024 is $88,330/year. The 65+ threshold is $73,428/year.

The structural problem food access shares with all ALICE burden categories is the eligibility gap. SNAP eligibility cuts off at 130% of the federal poverty level. A single adult earning $35,000 a year is well above the federal poverty line but still $15,000 short of the Frederick County ALICE threshold and ineligible for SNAP. This is the population for whom city-level food infrastructure matters most and for whom there is currently the least systematic coverage.

Federal policy context as of 2026: H.R. 1 cuts SNAP funding by $279 billion through 2034 and eliminates SNAP-Ed nutrition education funding beginning October 2025. These cuts push more households into the ALICE gap and increase demand on local food infrastructure already operating at or near capacity.

## Frederick-specific conditions

### 2024 ALICE data -- county and city

Source: United For ALICE 2026 Maryland Data Sheet (2024 ACS estimates).

**Frederick County (2024, 1-year ACS estimates):**
- Total households: 108,330
- Poverty households: 6,193 (5.7%)
- ALICE households: 28,272 (26.1%)
- Below ALICE threshold: 34,465 (31.8%)
- Median household income: $122,059 (state average: $102,905)
- ALICE threshold, under 65: $88,330
- ALICE threshold, 65+: $73,428

**City of Frederick (2024, 5-year ACS estimates):**
- Total households: 33,584
- Poverty households: 3,207 (9.5%)
- ALICE households: 11,136 (33.2%)
- Below ALICE threshold: 14,343 (42.7%)

The city-county gap is the most important number for council work. At 42.7% below threshold, the city runs eleven points higher than the county and reflects the city's higher concentration of renters, immigrants, single-parent households, and lower-wage workers. More than four in ten city households cannot reliably meet basic needs including food.

### ZIP code hardship distribution

The 2024 data shows significant variation across ZIP codes covering Frederick:

| ZIP | Total households | % below ALICE threshold |
|---|---|---|
| 21701 (downtown/east Frederick) | 18,651 | 37% |
| 21702 (west Frederick/Golden Mile) | 18,525 | 40% |
| 21703 (southwest Frederick) | 15,517 | 40% |
| 21704 (east/suburban Frederick) | 6,136 | 20% |

ZIPs 21702 and 21703 -- covering the Golden Mile corridor and much of District 2 -- sit at 40% below threshold, confirming the geographic concentration of hardship in the city's western corridor. ZIP 21704 at 20% reflects the wealthier suburban portions of the county.

### Maryland financial hardship by household type (2024)

The financial hardship data shows how unevenly food burden is distributed across household types in Maryland:

| Household type | % below threshold |
|---|---|
| Single-female-headed (with children) | 75% |
| Single-male-headed (with children) | 51% |
| Adults under 25 | 51% |
| Adults 65 and over | 44% |
| Adults 45-64 | 28% |
| Single or cohabiting (no children) | 29% |
| Adults 25-44 | 27% |
| Married (with children) | 13% |

Single mothers represent by far the highest-risk household type. Any food access intervention at the city level should be designed with that population as a primary target.

### USDA Food Access Research Atlas -- tract-level confirmation (2019 data)

**All 8 census tracts within Frederick city qualify as Low-Income and Low-Access at the 1/2-mile urban threshold.** Not one qualifies at the 1-mile threshold. This means the access barrier is close-in -- people are cut off within walking distance, not just across town. This is the most stringent USDA food desert classification available for urban areas.

Source: USDA FARA 2019 tract-level data files for Frederick County tracts within city boundaries.

**Aggregate across all 8 tracts:**
- Total population covered: 33,618
- Population low-access at 1/2 mile: 14,294 (42.5% of total)
- Low-income population low-access at 1/2 mile: 4,632
- Hispanic/Latino residents low-access at 1/2 mile: 2,104
- Black/African American residents low-access at 1/2 mile: 3,024
- Children (0-17) low-access at 1/2 mile: 3,292
- Seniors (65+) low-access at 1/2 mile: 1,539
- SNAP households across all tracts: 1,872
- Households without a vehicle: 1,478

Two tracts carry a compounded hardship flag -- formally designated as both low-income/low-access AND low vehicle access: tracts 750801 and 751003. Households in these tracts are simultaneously unable to afford groceries, too far to walk to a supermarket, and without reliable transportation.

**Policy implication:** This data closes the data gap identified at note creation. The city now has federal, tract-level confirmation that it qualifies to apply to DHCD for a Food Desert Area designation under the Fresh Food Financing Initiative. The designation application should reference these 8 tracts by FIPS code and the 1/2-mile low-access classification. NourishMD eligibility should be checked for specific Golden Mile addresses against the Maryland Community Business Compass Fresh Food Gap Score, but the tract data strongly suggests eligibility.

## Existing asset and program inventory

Frederick has meaningful but fragmented food assistance infrastructure. The common pattern across all programs is emergency response and crisis-mode provision. None of them address the underlying structural gap: ALICE households who are not in crisis but cannot consistently afford groceries and are ineligible for federal programs.

**City of Frederick Food Bank** (operated by Frederick Community Action Agency): provides a 3-5 day food supply, two visits per month per household. Two locations. Crisis-oriented, not a substitute for regular grocery access.

**Frederick Rescue Mission Food Distribution Center:** distributed 2.5 million pounds of food in the most recent year, sourcing from the Maryland Food Bank, local grocery stores, restaurants, and farmers. Serves Frederick County broadly. No income verification required.

**Frederick Farm to School Veggie Vans:** received Maryland Food and Agricultural Resiliency Mechanism grant funding to bring fresh local food to two low-income communities in Frederick. Mobile model, limited frequency.

**United Way Ride United Network:** 47% of rides since July 2024 were for healthcare appointments, 19% for veterans. Transportation to food sources is not a named use but is available -- directly relevant given 1,478 car-free households in food desert tracts and two tracts with formal low-vehicle-access flags.

**Frederick County FEED Grant Program:** launched November 2025 by County Executive Fitzwater following federal SNAP disruptions. Awarded $500,000 to local organizations including Maryland Food Bank, Seton Center, YMCA produce market (fresh fruits and vegetables), Safe Ride Foundation (transportation to food resources), and others. One-time emergency funding; a second round is anticipated in 2026. The YMCA community produce market is the closest current analog to a sustained fresh food access intervention and worth tracking as a candidate for city budget support.

## City authority by tier

### Tier 1 -- Direct city action

Zoning is the highest-leverage Tier 1 tool. Commercial zoning decisions about what can be built and where in the Golden Mile corridor are direct food access policy, not just land use administration. The LMC reform process is the near-term vehicle for making food access an explicit criterion in zoning decisions.

Additional Tier 1 instruments:
- **DHCD Food Desert Area designation application** -- the USDA tract data now confirms eligibility. This is the highest-priority near-term action. It unlocks the Fresh Food Financing Initiative for food retail businesses in the designated area.
- Permit fee waivers for new food retail opening in the designated food desert area
- City-owned land donation or lease to a nonprofit food operator
- Naming food access as an explicit criterion in LMC reform deliberations -- every density and mixed-use decision in the Golden Mile should be evaluated against food access impact

### Tier 2 -- City as facilitator or partner

- Sustained city budget line for FCAA food programs and YMCA produce market, framed as infrastructure not emergency response
- Partnership with a Goodr-style operator using a city-owned or city-adjacent facility
- Farmers market fee waivers and SNAP/WIC acceptance expansion
- Convening conversation with County Executive Fitzwater's office on whether a library-embedded grocery model is viable for a Frederick branch

### Tier 3 -- Advocacy only

- SNAP eligibility thresholds and benefit levels (federal)
- WIC program funding (federal)
- Federal nutrition program cuts under H.R. 1
- SNAP-Ed elimination

Federal policy shifts make Tier 1 and 2 action more urgent. The gap widens as federal programs contract.

## Maryland state tools

### Fresh Food Financing Initiative

Maryland's Fresh Food Financing Initiative (HB 451, 2014) provides flexible financing through DHCD's Neighborhood BusinessWorks program for businesses and nonprofits in designated food desert areas or Sustainable Communities. Local governments must apply to DHCD to designate a neighborhood as a food desert area. The USDA tract data now confirms that all 8 city tracts qualify at the 1/2-mile threshold. The city can submit a Food Desert Area designation application referencing these tracts and unlock state financing for food retail businesses in the corridor.

### NourishMD Grant Program

Maryland launched the NourishMD grant program in January 2026, providing $25,000-$150,000 grants to food retailers in areas with a Fresh Food Gap Score of 60 or higher on the Maryland Community Business Compass. Eligibility requires checking the Compass tool for specific addresses. The USDA tract data strongly suggests Golden Mile addresses will qualify. If confirmed, the city can actively recruit food retail applicants and assist with the application process.

## Peer city models

### Prince George's County / Goodr -- library-embedded free grocery (Maryland peer)

In May 2026, Prince George's County opened the Fairmount Five Market inside the Fairmount Heights Branch Library through a partnership with Goodr, an Atlanta-based social enterprise with 34 similar stores nationally since 2021. The model serves 200+ families per month. It is funded through a county grant and stocked weekly. Residents apply in advance and shop -- they are not given food boxes. There are no visible markers of charity.

The Goodr model specifically targets households above SNAP eligibility but below the ability to afford groceries reliably -- the ALICE gap population. A second PG County location is planned. Maryland's First Lady attended the opening and tied the model to the state's Food is Medicine initiative, which includes a Medically Tailored Meals program reaching 3,000+ Marylanders.

The structural elements of the model: a public institution as host site, a county grant as the funding mechanism, and a private social enterprise as the operator. Frederick City does not operate the library system, so direct replication of the library-embedded format requires county partnership. However, a city-owned or city-adjacent facility -- community center, city hall annex, a donated commercial space -- could host a similar model without county involvement.

Key design principle: dignity-first framing matters both practically and politically. Programs that feel like infrastructure produce higher participation and broader coalition support than programs that feel like charity. Participants shop, they do not receive. This is the same principle that should govern how the council frames all ALICE-targeted programming.

### Local Progress municipal grocery ownership framework

Local Progress hosted a two-session series on municipal grocery models in early 2026, identifying four ownership structures cities have used to create grocery access in food deserts:

**Full municipal operation:** city owns and runs the store. Highest control, highest operational and political risk. Not recommended for Frederick's scale and political context.

**Nonprofit operator with city anchor support:** city provides space, subsidy, or land; nonprofit runs operations. Most tested model in mid-size cities. Best fit for Frederick near-term.

**Cooperative:** community-owned, member-governed. Strong equity alignment, slower to scale, requires organizing capacity. Longer-term option consistent with the democratic economic infrastructure thread in the broader affordability agenda.

**Hybrid arrangements:** mixed ownership and operator structures. Most flexible, most complex governance.

Peer examples from the series: Atlanta's Azalea Market (city-backed nonprofit operator model) and Illinois grocery development (state-backed under Gov. Pritzker). Key resources: Rural Grocery Initiative guide for city leaders (ruralgrocery.org/learn/publications/guides/Grocery_Store_Considerations_for_City_Leaders.pdf) and Economic Security Project policy background (economicsecurityproject.org/resource/opening-the-door-to-public-grocery-stores/).

The nonprofit operator model is the closest analog to what Frederick's FCAA and Rescue Mission already do in adjacent spaces. The gap is permanent infrastructure rather than crisis distribution.

### Seattle -- executive order on food access (peer city framing model)

In 2025, Seattle Mayor Harrell issued Executive Order 2025-10 directing the city to explore actions to incentivize grocery distribution, including through eminent domain, a stakeholder workgroup, and state law advocacy. The order names specific food desert neighborhoods and commits to a Food Action Plan. The eminent domain framing is more aggressive than Frederick's political context warrants, but the executive order structure -- committing the city to a specific food access agenda as a formal action -- is a viable analog for a Council Communication of Intent or resolution that names food access as a priority and directs staff to return with options.

### Federal context: H.R. 1 and local mitigation

The NLC has documented that H.R. 1's SNAP cuts and SNAP-Ed elimination will increase food insecurity and shift the burden to local programs. Pierce County (WA) allocated $400,000 in emergency supplemental funding when SNAP disruptions caused a 40% increase in food bank visits in late 2025. Frederick County's FEED grant ($500,000) follows the same pattern. The question for the city is whether emergency response is the only available posture, or whether permanent infrastructure investments can absorb the structural gap before the next federal disruption.

## Legislative sequencing implications

**Near-term, no new authority required:**
- Submit DHCD Food Desert Area designation application for the 8 confirmed city tracts. This is now the highest-priority action -- the data is in hand, the eligibility is confirmed, and the application unlocks state financing tools immediately.
- Check Golden Mile addresses against Maryland Community Business Compass Fresh Food Gap Score for NourishMD eligibility confirmation.
- Track YMCA community produce market outcomes from the FEED grant cycle as a candidate for sustained city budget support.

**Near-term, through the budget cycle:**
- Sustained city budget line for FCAA food programs and YMCA produce market, framed as infrastructure.
- Explore city-owned facility availability for a Goodr-model partnership with a nonprofit operator -- particularly relevant given the two low-vehicle-access tracts (750801, 751003) where a walkable facility would have the highest impact.

**Longer-term, through the LMC process:**
- Name food access as an explicit criterion in LMC reform deliberations. Commercial zoning in the Golden Mile must be treated as food access policy.
- Once DHCD designation is secured, actively recruit food retail businesses and assist NourishMD applicants in the corridor.
