# ALICE Report -- Frederick County 2025 (2023 Data)

## Summary

The United Way of Frederick County's 2025 ALICE Report uses 2023 American Community Survey data to document the scale and distribution of financial hardship in Frederick County and City. ALICE stands for Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed -- households earning above the federal poverty level but below the actual cost of living. The report uses a locally calibrated Household Survival Budget rather than the federal poverty line, making it a far more accurate instrument for policy analysis than federal metrics.

This note is the foundational reference for all non-housing affordability work. The six burden categories the ALICE Survival Budget tracks -- housing, childcare, transportation, food, healthcare, and technology -- map directly to the domains where the City Council has potential leverage.

Note: Frederick County-specific line-item budget figures require requesting the county data sheet directly from United Way of Frederick County. The Maryland state average figures below are from the 2025 state report PDF. Frederick County costs will be higher than state averages in most categories, particularly housing.

---

## Key Data Points

### Frederick County vs City of Frederick

The city-county gap is the most important number for council work. Frederick County overall sits at 33% of households below the ALICE Threshold (35,557 households). The City of Frederick is at 43% (13,837 of 32,182 households, 2019 to 2023 five-year data), ten points higher than the county. Howard County, the state's lowest, was at 30%. The 45% figure sometimes cited for the City is Frederick County Election District 2, a county subdivision larger than the City, not City Council District 2. This gap reflects the city's higher proportion of renters, immigrants, single-parent households, and lower-wage workers relative to the suburban county.

Maryland statewide: 39% below threshold (934,715 households). 10% in poverty, 29% ALICE. Frederick County ranks among the better-performing counties in the state, but that figure obscures the city's structural concentration of hardship.

### The ALICE Survival Budget -- Maryland State Average, 2023

The following are statewide averages from the 2025 Maryland state report. Frederick County figures will differ; the county data sheet is the source for localized numbers.

Monthly costs, Maryland state average:

| Category | Single Adult (18-64) | Single Adult (65+) | Family of Four |
|---|---|---|---|
| Housing | $1,251 | $1,251 | $1,615 |
| Child Care | $0 | $0 | $1,538 |
| Food | $514 | $473 | $1,394 |
| Transportation | $414 | $349 | $910 |
| Health Care | $218 | $580 | $850 |
| Technology | $86 | $86 | $116 |
| Miscellaneous | $248 | $274 | $642 |
| Taxes | $565 | $643 | $1,113 |
| Monthly Total | $3,296 | $3,656 | $8,178 |
| Annual Total | $39,552 | $43,872 | $98,136 |
| Hourly wage needed | $19.78 | $21.94 | $49.07 |

For comparison, the federal poverty level in 2023 was $14,580 for a single adult and $30,000 for a family of four. The gap between FPL and the ALICE threshold -- roughly $25,000 for a single adult at the state average -- is the "ALICE gap." Most federal program eligibility (SNAP, housing vouchers, LIHEAP) is keyed to FPL or small multiples of it. A household earning $35,000 a year is ineligible for most programs and still cannot cover basic needs. This is the policy gap the council should be naming explicitly.

Frederick County-specific figures (from WYPR reporting, Ken Oldham interview, 2025):
- Single adult threshold: $49,380/year ($24.69/hour)
- Family of four threshold: $111,588/year ($55.79/hour)

These are higher than the Maryland state average, consistent with Frederick's above-median cost of living relative to the state.

### Methodology Note

Housing costs use 110% of HUD Fair Market Rent, adjusted using Small Area FMR in metro areas. Child care uses Maryland State Department of Education registered Family Child Care Home rates. Food uses the USDA Thrifty Food Plan with county variation from Feeding America. Transportation uses AAA operating cost data. Technology is defined as basic broadband plus a smartphone plan per adult. Miscellaneous is 10% of budget excluding taxes.

## Burden Categories -- What Each Domain Actually Costs

**Housing** ($1,251-$1,615/month at state average) is the largest single budget line and the most documented burden. Rents increased more than 30% during the COVID pandemic. In 2023, 73% of all renter households below the ALICE Threshold in Maryland were rent burdened (paying 30% or more of income on housing), up from 68% in 2021. For homeowners below the threshold, 59% were housing burdened, up from 51% in 2021. This is the domain with the deepest vault coverage and the most active legislative work (Real Rent Ordinance, housing production target, LMC reform, MPDU rate).

**Child Care** ($1,538/month for a family with infant and preschooler) is the second largest line item for families, just behind housing at the state average ($1,615). In Frederick County the 2023 figures were $1,802 for child care and $2,107 for housing. A 2023 study found 80% of Frederick County families relied on childcare for employment. Of those, 38% said childcare added more than 30 minutes to their commute, and 46% spent more than $1,200 per month on services. The council has limited direct instruments but real leverage through zoning (facility siting), partnerships with the county, and advocacy to the state on subsidy rates.

**Food** ($514/month single adult; $1,394/month family of four) represents roughly 16% of the family survival budget at state average. Frederick-specific food access gap data is not well documented in the public record. The ALICE gap dynamic is most acute here: households earning too much for SNAP but unable to cover groceries represent a significant population with no current programmatic answer at the city level. Food equity is explicitly flagged in the ALICE report as varying by gender, race/ethnicity, and immigration status.

**Transportation** ($414/month single adult; $910/month family) is the third major household cost line. The primary driver is car ownership costs. For car-free households the burden shifts to ride-share dependency, concentrated among those outside city centers. United Way's Ride United Network (RUN) provides free and discounted rides; 47% of RUN rides since July 2024 were for healthcare appointments and 19% were used by veterans. The council has direct leverage through TransIT advocacy, parking policy, and land use decisions that affect walkability and transit access.

**Health Care** ($218/month single adult; $850/month family; $580/month for adults 65+) uses employer-sponsored plan premiums plus out-of-pocket costs. The 65+ figure is notably higher because it incorporates Medicare Parts A and B plus average out-of-pocket spending for the top five chronic diseases. Healthcare is largely outside the city's direct jurisdiction, but nearly half of RUN rides being for healthcare appointments shows how healthcare access cost presents as a transportation burden at the local level.

**Technology** ($86/month per household) covers basic broadband and a smartphone plan per adult. United Way distributed 5,500 Chromebooks to ALICE families in Frederick County and found 80% of recipient families had no computer at home prior to receiving one. The digital divide is a direct barrier to job access, telemedicine, and educational attainment. The city has leverage through franchise agreements, right-of-way policy, and advocacy for affordable broadband access.

## Demographic Distribution

### Maryland Statewide (from state report, as context for Frederick)

By age: under 25 at 73% below threshold; 25-44 at 37%; 45-64 at 32%; 65 and over at 48%.

By race/ethnicity: Black at 49%; Hispanic at 47%; American Indian/Alaska Native at 53%; White non-Hispanic at 33%; Asian at 32%.

By household type: single-female-headed with children at 70%; single-male-headed with children at 56%; married with children at 18%; single or cohabiting under 65 no children at 38%.

### Frederick County Specific (from WYPR and United Way Frederick reporting)

- Single mothers: 76% below threshold
- Single fathers: 49% below threshold
- Adults under 25: 85% below threshold (2,393 households in this cohort)
- Adults 65+: 43% below threshold
- Black veterans: 35% below threshold (vs 17% white veterans)
- Hispanic veterans: 30% below threshold
- Total veterans: 20% of 15,750 Frederick County veterans below threshold

The Frederick veteran racial gap is significant and underused in public framing. Black veterans in Frederick experience financial hardship at more than twice the rate of white veterans, exceeding the statewide veteran racial disparity.

## Labor Force Context

Of the 20 most common occupations in Maryland in 2023, 10 paid less than $20 per hour. Of all workers in those 20 occupations, 28% lived in households below the ALICE Threshold. Occupations with the highest concentration of ALICE workers: personal care aides (56%), cooks (52%), janitors and building cleaners (50%), cashiers (47%), laborers and material movers (43%), waiters and waitresses (43%).

These are Frederick's workforce -- warehouse workers, retail, food service, healthcare support, custodial. Only 25% of Maryland workers had the security of a full-time salaried job in 2023. Nearly two-thirds of those in the labor force were paid hourly or worked part time, meaning income is variable and benefits are less likely.

Wages have increased since 2018 but have not kept pace with costs. A retail salesperson earning the 2023 Maryland median wage of $16.06/hour ($33,410 annually full-time) is still $21,730 short of the one-adult, one-school-age-child survival budget -- actually slightly further behind than the same worker was in 2010, despite a 62% nominal wage increase over that period. Wage growth has not closed the ALICE gap; it has at best slowed its widening.

## Savings and Asset Fragility

Below-threshold households in the South Atlantic Census Division (which includes Maryland) in 2023: only 32% had savings sufficient to cover three months of expenses (vs 71% above threshold). Only 38% had any retirement assets (vs 89% above threshold). Only 12% of non-retirees below threshold reported their retirement savings were on track (vs 34% above threshold).

This is the material basis for why one-time crises -- a car repair, a medical bill, a missed paycheck -- cascade into housing instability or debt spirals for ALICE households. Emergency relief spending at the city level addresses symptoms of this fragility. Structural interventions that reduce the cost side of the six budget categories address the underlying condition.

## Trend Direction

Maryland: Between 2010 and 2023, total households increased 11%, ALICE households increased 16%, poverty households increased 22%. The share below threshold has been persistently elevated -- 37% in 2010, 39% in 2023, with only a brief dip to 37% in 2019. The pandemic did not substantially change the structural rate; the post-pandemic expiration of safety nets worsened it.

Frederick County: Between 2022 and 2023 the number of ALICE households increased by 2,017 (to 27% of county households). Poverty-level households increased by 402 (to 6%). Combined 33% of county households below threshold; 43% of city households. Trend is worsening.

## The ALICE Gap as Policy Frame

The core framing utility of this report is the gap it names between federal program eligibility thresholds and actual cost of living. The federal poverty level is designed for a national average that does not exist in Frederick. A household earning $45,000 a year here is above the poverty line, above most program thresholds, and still cannot simultaneously cover rent, childcare, transportation, and food. Federal programs do not reach them. City programs rarely reach them either.

Bringing all Maryland households to the ALICE Threshold would have required $26.6 billion in additional income in 2023. That is not a local government number. But the council's role is not to close the full gap -- it is to reduce the cost side of the equation in the domains where it has authority, and to advocate aggressively on the domains where it does not.

## City of Frederick Leverage by Domain

**Direct city action possible:** Utility shutoff protections (water shutoff prevention program exists; expand to energy), rental fee regulation (Real Rent Ordinance), zoning for affordable housing production, parking policy reform, fee waivers for low-income residents accessing city services, Municipal ID (connects to banking access and check-cashing cost reduction), public broadband franchise and right-of-way policy.

**City can partner or facilitate:** Childcare facility siting (zoning and land use), transportation subsidy coordination with United Way RUN and TransIT, food access through commercial zoning and land donation to food-serving nonprofits, financial empowerment programming through city-funded community organizations, VITA (free tax preparation) support and promotion.

**City can advocate but not directly control:** SNAP and LIHEAP eligibility thresholds, state childcare subsidy rates and provider reimbursement, BGE/Pepco rate structures (PSC jurisdiction), federal housing voucher funding levels, state minimum wage.
