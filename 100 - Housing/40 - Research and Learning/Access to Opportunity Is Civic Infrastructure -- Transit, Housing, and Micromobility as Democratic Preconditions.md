# Access to Opportunity Is Civic Infrastructure -- Transit, Housing, and Micromobility as Democratic Preconditions

## Summary

Transit frequency, housing production, and micromobility access are not separate affordability line items -- they are the physical infrastructure through which civic participation becomes possible or is systematically denied, connecting César's EPMD ordinance, Housing Production Target, and Affordability Beyond Housing work to the Infrastructure of Democratic Survival framework.

## The Connection

Denis Agar's argument against fare-free transit, taken seriously, is not really about transit. It is about what it means to be systematically excluded from participation in society. His language is precise: without frequent transit, nondrivers are excluded from "employment, education, democratic processes and all of the things that constitute society." Some are no longer invited to events because everyone assumes they cannot get there. The exclusion is so complete it becomes invisible -- not discrimination, just logistics.

This is Layer 3 of the Infrastructure of Democratic Survival framework ([[The Infrastructure of Democratic Survival -- Arendt Nakagawa and the Through-Line in Cesars Council Work]]): civic infrastructure as the architecture through which political presence and expressive particularity become possible at all. Arendt's point is that the public realm requires physical conditions of access. You cannot appear before others, cannot act politically, cannot be witnessed as a citizen, if you cannot get to where the public is.

Transit frequency is not a transit problem. It is an infrastructure-of-appearance problem.

## Three Parallel Arguments

### 1. Transit frequency as civic access

Agar's Brampton data makes the case empirically: investing in frequency, not price, drives ridership among the people who most need transit. The one-third of Americans and Canadians without driver's licenses are not primarily blocked by cost -- cost was a barrier for 3% of surveyed nondrivers in Washington State; lack of service was a barrier for 65%. Making a bad bus free does not make it useful. Making a frequent bus free does.

The equity case for frequency is identical in structure to the equity case for the EPMD ordinance. Class 1 and Class 2 e-bikes on shared paths are not a recreational amenity. They are the mobility layer for residents who cannot afford cars, cannot drive, or cannot access fixed-route transit that runs once an hour. The argument in [[EPMD Ordinance -- Equity Argument, Class 1 and 2 Path Access]] is the same argument Agar is making: access to the network is the intervention, not access to a subsidized price on a network that doesn't reach you.

### 2. Housing production as access infrastructure

Housing is where this synthesis gains the most political weight. The Housing Production Target Resolution (1,400 units/year through 2035) is not fundamentally a supply argument. It is an access argument. When workers are priced out of Frederick, they cannot live near the civic institutions, employers, schools, and social networks that constitute participation in Frederick's public life. When rents consume 40-50% of income, the margin required to show up -- to attend a meeting, run for office, organize, vote, advocate -- shrinks or disappears.

[[Inequality Is the Housing Crisis -- Synthesis]] frames this explicitly: housing unaffordability is not a market malfunction but a power arrangement that concentrates access and excludes those without capital. [[Affordability Is a Power Problem Not a Price Problem -- Synthesis]] extends it: affordability policy is economic democracy policy. A city that cannot house its workforce is not just expensive; it is contracting its own civic base.

The Maryland supply data now in the vault reinforces the urgency. Statewide inventory is down 16.4% year-over-year, new listings down 22.1%, prices up 4.7% -- and this is happening despite state legislative action. Frederick's production target is not ahead of the problem; it is catching up to it.

### 3. Micromobility as last-mile civic infrastructure

The EPMD ordinance is the smallest-scale intervention in this synthesis, and in some ways the most legible. A Class 1 or 2 e-bike on a shared path is worth nothing if the path does not connect to anywhere. But when it does -- to a bus stop, a grocery store, a school, a polling place, a community meeting -- it extends the mobility radius of someone who cannot drive or cannot afford to. Frederick's car-dependent infrastructure makes this the most actionable near-term layer: path access policy is local, it is cheap, and its equity implications are immediate.

## The Unified Frame

The political case César can make -- and which this synthesis is designed to support -- is this: housing, transit, and micromobility are not three items on an affordability checklist. They are three layers of the same infrastructure. Deny someone housing within a reasonable commute, and transit cannot reach them. Give them transit access but run it once an hour, and it cannot reliably get them to work, school, or civic life. Give them frequent transit and housing but no safe, low-cost last-mile connection, and the last gap remains. All three are required for the infrastructure to function as civic infrastructure rather than civic decoration.

This frame connects to [[Civic Infrastructure as Democratic Immune System]] and the three-layer model in [[The Infrastructure of Democratic Survival -- Arendt Nakagawa and the Through-Line in Cesars Council Work]]. Layer 3 is not abstract. It is a bus that comes every 15 minutes. It is a unit a teacher can afford to rent. It is a path a 60-year-old can use on a bike that assists their effort.

## Implications for César's Active Work

**EPMD ordinance:** The equity argument for Class 1 and 2 path access is not just about fairness to cyclists. It is about completing the last-mile layer of civic infrastructure. Frame it that way in remarks if the opportunity arises.

**Housing Production Target Resolution:** When introducing, connect the production goal explicitly to civic participation, not just economic growth. Workers who cannot live in Frederick cannot be citizens of Frederick in the full sense.

**Affordability Beyond Housing agenda:** Transit access is currently listed as a Tier 2-3 domain (limited city authority). But the frequency argument reframes what advocacy looks like at the city level -- it is not about running city buses, it is about making the case to TransIT and the state that frequency investment is equity investment, and putting that on the record.
