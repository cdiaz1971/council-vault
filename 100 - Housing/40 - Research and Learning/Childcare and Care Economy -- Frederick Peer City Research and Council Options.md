# Childcare and Care Economy -- Frederick Peer City Research and Council Options

## Why This Note Exists

Childcare is the largest ALICE budget line for families with young children. In the Frederick County survival budget, a family with two children in child care pays $2,500 per month, up $698 in one year and more than housing ($2,250). The Maryland state average is $2,164. Children under five are the most commonly evicted demographic in the U.S., partly because childcare costs consume income that would otherwise cover rent.

The [[Affordability Agenda -- Council Authority Tiering by Domain]] correctly identifies childcare as a Tier 2 domain: the city does not set rates, license providers, or administer subsidy programs. Those levers are at the state (Maryland State Department of Education Division of Early Childhood) and county (Frederick County) levels. But Tier 2 does not mean nothing. Cities have meaningfully expanded childcare access through zoning reform, facility partnerships, employer coordination, and budget investment -- without controlling rates.

The [[Worker Power as Care System Infrastructure -- Demos Explainer 2026]] adds a dimension the tiering note does not fully capture: care worker wages and working conditions are themselves an affordability issue. Care workers in Frederick are disproportionately women and workers of color, and they often cannot afford to live in the city they serve. If care worker wages collapse, the care system fails regardless of how many new facilities are built. This note incorporates that frame.

## The Frederick Baseline

### What We Know

From the [[ALICE Report -- Frederick County 2025 (2023 Data) -- Reference Note]]:

- A Frederick County family of four with two children in child care pays $2,500/month in the ALICE survival budget ($2,164 at the Maryland state average).
- 80% of Frederick County families rely on childcare for employment.
- Of those, 38% reported childcare adds more than 30 minutes to their commute -- a signal that families are reaching beyond their neighborhood for available care.
- 46% of Frederick County families spend more than $1,200 per month on childcare services.
- 72% of children in rental units are ALICE. The overlap between rental households and childcare-dependent families is high.

From [[Childcare as Housing Stability Issue Universal Childcare Momentum]]:

- Children under five are the most evicted demographic in the U.S.
- New Mexico launched universal childcare in November 2025 as the first state to do so, with estimated household savings of $12,000 per year.
- Four states and DC offer universal pre-K.

### What We Don't Know

- The number of licensed childcare providers in Frederick City limits vs. unmet demand.
- Where childcare deserts are concentrated within the city -- which neighborhoods have no licensed provider within reasonable distance.
- The current waitlist data for Frederick County's subsidized childcare slots (Child Care Scholarship Program).
- How many providers in Frederick City have closed in the last five years, and why.
- Whether the city's zoning code contains specific barriers to home-based childcare providers or childcare facility siting in residential or mixed-use zones.

These are the primary research gaps. Most of them are answerable through MSDE provider licensing data, the Frederick County childcare resource and referral network (Maryland Family Network), and a zoning code review.

## Peer City Models

### New Mexico -- Statewide Universal Childcare (2025)

New Mexico became the first state to launch universal childcare in November 2025, funded through oil and gas revenue dedicated to early childhood programs. Families with children under six pay nothing or a sliding-scale copay based on income. Estimated savings: $12,000 per year per eligible family. This is a state-level intervention, not replicable at the city level, but it establishes what the policy ambition looks like and provides an advocacy benchmark. Frederick City can pass a resolution urging Maryland to model a similar program.

### Cambridge, MA -- Citizenship-Neutral Civic Infrastructure Approach

Cambridge's model is most relevant for the participation angle: their PB12 program, which is open to non-citizens, and their long track record of inclusive civic programming demonstrate that a city can build participation infrastructure that reaches immigrant residents across multiple domains simultaneously. Childcare access and civic access are both preconditions for full participation. Cambridge has not launched a standalone childcare program, but its integrated equity infrastructure is the model for what Frederick's approach could look like.

### Austin, TX -- Childcare as Economic Development

Austin operates a childcare access program through its economic development office, framing subsidized childcare slots for low-income workers as workforce infrastructure rather than social services. The city contracts with childcare providers to reserve a set number of subsidized slots for qualifying families, funded through the city budget. Austin's approach demonstrates that a city can purchase childcare access as a procurement act -- directly funding slots rather than building or operating facilities. This is within city budget authority and does not require state permission.

Austin's program targets workers in low-wage industries, particularly hospitality and service workers. It is means-tested, not universal. Program size: roughly 1,000 subsidized slots at launch, with waitlist data used to guide expansion.

This is the most directly replicable peer model for Frederick. A similar program at Frederick's scale -- starting with 100-200 reserved slots for ALICE-threshold families -- would cost roughly $1.2M-$2.4M annually at the state average rate. Funding could come from the city's general fund, federal CDBG dollars, or a dedicated allocation in the FY2028 budget.

### San Jose, CA -- Zoning Reform for Childcare Supply

San Jose conducted a systematic audit of its zoning code to identify barriers to childcare facility siting. Findings: childcare centers were prohibited in many commercial zones where demand was highest; home-based providers faced permitting requirements more burdensome than comparable home businesses; parking minimums applied to childcare facilities were designed for retail, not family drop-off patterns.

San Jose amended its zoning code to: permit childcare as a by-right use in all commercial and mixed-use zones; reduce parking requirements for childcare facilities to reflect actual traffic patterns; streamline the permitting process for home-based providers to match the process for comparable home occupations.

The supply impact: new provider openings increased in the two years following the amendments. This is Tier 1 action for Frederick. A zoning code audit for childcare-specific barriers is low-cost and the staff work required is minimal relative to the policy impact.

### Chicago -- Employer Roundtable and Childcare Benefit Coordination

Chicago's city government convened a childcare benefit roundtable with major employers in 2023, bringing together HR directors, childcare providers, and city staff to map gaps in employer-sponsored childcare benefits and identify opportunities for employer investment. The city did not fund the program; it served as a convener and facilitator.

Outcomes: three major employers committed to childcare benefit expansions for hourly workers; two childcare providers expanded capacity on the strength of employer partnership commitments; the roundtable produced a publicly available employer guide on childcare benefit design.

This is a no-cost Tier 2 action for Frederick. The Council President is well-positioned to convene it. The hospital system, major employers in the Westview area, and large retail employers in the district would be the core invitees.

### Michigan, California, Minnesota -- Care Worker Standards

From [[Worker Power as Care System Infrastructure -- Demos Explainer 2026]]: these three states have enacted labor policies that combine public investment in care with care worker wage and quality standards. The mechanism: state funding for care programs is conditioned on providers meeting minimum wage and benefit standards for workers. This prevents the race to the bottom in which public subsidy goes to the lowest-cost (lowest-wage) providers.

Maryland has not enacted this type of linked standard. A Frederick City resolution calling on the state to link childcare subsidy rates to provider wage standards would be within the council's advocacy authority and would complement any direct city budget investment.

## Frederick-Specific Options

### Tier 1 -- What the Council Can Do Directly

**Zoning code audit and reform.** Commission a staff review of the city's zoning code for barriers to childcare facility siting in commercial, mixed-use, and residential zones. Home-based provider permitting requirements should be compared to comparable home occupation permits. If barriers are found, a targeted LMC amendment is the instrument. Timeline: staff audit within 60 days; amendment introduced within a budget cycle.

**Permit fee waiver for new childcare facility openings.** The city charges permit and licensing fees to new businesses. A specific waiver or reduction for new childcare facility openings would lower the startup cost for prospective providers in underserved neighborhoods. This requires a budget ordinance or administrative directive. Cost: minimal; the fee revenue foregone is small relative to the supply impact.

**City employee childcare benefit review.** The city as employer can examine its own childcare benefit structure and consider what, if anything, it provides to hourly and part-time city employees. Limited direct impact but visible as a signal to other employers. Staff action; no ordinance required.

### Tier 2 -- What the Council Can Facilitate or Fund

**Reserved slot program (Austin model).** A city budget allocation to purchase reserved childcare slots from licensed providers for ALICE-threshold families. Starting scale: 50-100 slots at roughly $12,000-$18,000 per year per slot, depending on age group. Estimated cost of a pilot: $600,000-$1.8M annually. Funding candidates: CDBG allocation, general fund, or a dedicated line in the FY2028 budget request.

Before proposing this, the vacancy and waitlist landscape needs to be confirmed. If licensed providers already have unfilled capacity, the city may be able to purchase slots immediately. If providers are at or near capacity, a slot purchase program will need to be paired with the supply-side zoning work.

**City facility partnership for childcare siting.** If the city owns underutilized community facility space -- recreation centers, community centers, city-adjacent properties -- a partnership with a nonprofit childcare provider for subsidized siting is within administrative authority. The city would provide space at below-market rent; the provider would maintain licensed operations with a set-aside of slots for ALICE-threshold families.

**Employer roundtable (Chicago model).** Convene major Frederick employers -- hospital system, county government, major retail and hospitality employers -- to examine childcare benefit gaps and coordinate investment. No budget required. Produces a public record of employer commitments and a guide for smaller employers.

**Frederick County coordination.** The county administers the Child Care Scholarship Program, which provides subsidized childcare for qualifying families. The city can advocate for increased county allocation, support outreach to eligible families who are not enrolled, and coordinate city-funded nonprofit contracts to include childcare navigation as a service.

**VITA-model benefits navigation for childcare subsidies.** Many Frederick families eligible for the Child Care Scholarship Program are not enrolled. A city-facilitated benefits navigation effort -- modeled on VITA free tax preparation outreach -- could connect eligible families to existing subsidies without new program spending. Partnership with Maryland Family Network (the county childcare resource and referral network) is the implementation path.

### Tier 3 -- What the Council Can Advocate For

- Maryland universal pre-K expansion funding
- State childcare subsidy rate increases for providers (current rates are below market, limiting provider participation in the subsidy program)
- Federal Child Care and Development Block Grant funding levels
- State legislation linking childcare subsidy funding to provider wage standards (Michigan/California/Minnesota model)
- Federal paid family leave (directly reduces the period during which childcare is needed for very young infants)

## The Care Worker Frame

Demos frames this clearly: childcare access cannot be fixed without fixing childcare jobs. A Frederick childcare expansion that fills slots but pays providers poverty wages will produce high turnover, low quality, and a care workforce that cannot afford to live in the city. This creates an internal contradiction in any affordability agenda that addresses housing costs for residents without addressing wage conditions for care workers.

The Frederick-specific version: care workers in District 2 neighborhoods are disproportionately women of color, many of them immigrants. They face the same ALICE threshold squeeze as the families they serve. A childcare policy that creates access for families but not stability for workers is incomplete.

The practical implication for Frederick: any city-funded reserved slot program or facility partnership should include provider wage standards as a contract condition. This is not enforceable on providers citywide, but the city as a funder can require it as a condition of city dollars. This gives Frederick a model for what a state-level linked-standard policy would look like.

## Recommended Near-Term Actions

1. Commission a staff zoning code audit for childcare facility barriers. Low cost, Tier 1, can produce an LMC amendment within a budget cycle.
2. Request from Frederick County: current Child Care Scholarship Program waitlist count and current slot capacity in Frederick City limits. This data determines whether the problem is supply (not enough providers) or access (providers exist but families can't get in or can't afford it).
3. Convene an employer roundtable. No budget required. Produces a public record and may generate private investment.
4. Design a reserved slot pilot for FY2028 budget request, contingent on the waitlist and capacity data.
5. Pass a resolution supporting Maryland universal pre-K expansion and state childcare subsidy rate increases. Tier 3 advocacy, low cost, generates a public record and press opportunity.
