# Prosperous Cities Can Mask Structural Fiscal Weakness

## Summary

Coby Lefkowitz at Strong Towns argues that good urbanism requires genuine numeracy: the ability to look past surface-level budget health and see the structural vulnerabilities underneath. Using New York as the primary case, the piece shows how cities can appear flush while depending on temporary federal aid, unsustainable tax receipt records, and accounting tricks that smooth current-year numbers at the cost of future fiscal stress.

## Key Points

New York's Department of Education budget more than doubled from 2010 to 2026 while enrollment dropped by nearly 150,000 students. The per-student spending number looks generous but masks a shrinking and aging institution.

Federal aid to New York hit $16.6 billion in 2021 (16% of the total budget) as Covid relief, then dropped by nearly $5 billion by 2026. Cities that structured programs around that temporary aid are now facing structural shortfalls.

NYC's Comptroller projects the city will brush against its 15% debt affordability ceiling by 2033, with annual budget shortfalls of $5-6 billion projected from 2027 through 2029 -- and those projections assume robust tax base growth. If conditions worsen, the gap deepens.

"Prepayments" are used to smooth current-year budgets, an accounting move that makes near-term numbers look better than the structural reality.

The broader argument: urban advocates who argue for more social spending and denser cities need to also argue for the fiscal discipline and realistic forecasting that makes those programs durable. Optimism about urbanism is not a substitute for the math.

## Relevance

A useful frame for Frederick's budget conversations, particularly around housing and infrastructure. If Frederick pursues ambitious housing production targets and affordability programs, the fiscal modeling needs to be honest about long-term operating costs, subsidy dependence, and what happens when state or federal funding shifts. The Strong Towns critique of surface-level budget optimism applies at the municipal scale too.
