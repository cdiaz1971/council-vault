# Institutions Plan for Communities Without Them -- A Pattern Across Housing Research and Zoning

## The Pattern

Two articles arrived in the same inbox batch, from entirely different domains. One is about urban planning and the unglamorous logistics infrastructure that makes cities work. The other is about extractive research and the communities of color that institutions claim to serve. They are describing the same thing.

Ryan Puzycki (City of Yes) argues that cities fail at infrastructure planning because the political system is structured to let neighborhoods say "not here" without anyone asking "then where?" The result: unglamorous but necessary uses get pushed to wherever resistance is weakest -- which is usually lower-income areas -- while planners spend their time adjudicating setbacks for triplexes. No one is planning for the city as a whole, because the system isn't built to do that.

Dr. Brittany Lewis (Next City) argues that institutions fail at community partnership because the research system is structured to extract data from communities rather than co-produce knowledge with them. The result: policies get designed by outside experts based on predetermined questions, announced to communities rather than built with them, and generate backlash or indifference rather than change. No one is actually listening, because the system isn't built to do that either.

The shared structure is this: an institution with power makes decisions that affect a community without that community being genuinely included in defining the problem or shaping the solution. The institution defends this as rational, expert-driven, or efficient. The community experiences it as something being done to them. The outcome falls short of what either side wanted.

## Why This Matters for Frederick

Frederick is running this pattern simultaneously on multiple tracks.

In housing and zoning, the LMC review is a parcel-level political fight that keeps deferring the system-level question: what kind of city does Frederick want to be, and what does it need to function? The pressure to convert every available site to housing, without a plan for the logistics and infrastructure layer, is exactly the "sanctuary suburb" dynamic Puzycki describes -- wanting the benefits of density without planning for what density requires.

In community engagement, the Municipal ID program and City Values Act depend on trust relationships with immigrant communities that city government cannot build through standard outreach. Every survey, public comment period, and community meeting that asks for input after the policy has already been shaped is operating in the extractive research mode Lewis describes. The organizations -- Centro Hispano, SSCM, AACF -- that have genuine ground-level relationships are the alternative. The question is whether they are being consulted or actually co-designing.

In data center permitting, the community benefit agreement framework is an attempt to solve the "where does it go?" problem that Puzycki raises. But if those agreements are designed by city staff and presented to communities for sign-off rather than negotiated with communities as co-designers, Lewis would say the form of partnership is being used without the substance.

## The Synthesis

The common failure mode is institutional efficiency mistaken for good governance. Expert-designed solutions are faster to produce than co-designed ones. Parcel-level zoning decisions are easier to manage than city-scale infrastructure planning. Survey data is cheaper to collect than long-term community relationships. In each case, the shortcut produces a result that looks like a decision but functions like a deferral.

The alternative, in both cases, requires accepting that the process is part of the outcome. A zoning framework that was built with communities produces different decisions than one imposed on them. A research process that positions community members as co-producers generates different questions than one that arrives with the questions already set. The city that plans for its unglamorous needs alongside its aspirational ones is more functional than the one that pushes those needs out of sight.

For César, the practical implication is a question worth asking of every active policy track: who defined the problem, and who is being asked to ratify a solution?

- The data center CBA framework could be evaluated against Lewis's extractive vs. co-design distinction. Is the community being invited to negotiate, or to respond?
- Puzycki's argument about infrastructure planning at the system level vs. the parcel level is an argument for a comprehensive plan with teeth -- something Frederick's current process may not support.
