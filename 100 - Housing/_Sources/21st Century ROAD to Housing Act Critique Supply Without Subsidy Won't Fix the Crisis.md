## Summary

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law in July 2026 -- the first substantial federal housing bill in decades. Quigley and co-host Jack (End Poverty Now podcast) give it the Gen-Z verdict: "mid." The law improves financing and construction pathways for market-rate housing, which Quigley acknowledges is not useless. But it leaves untouched the structural problem at the bottom of the market: 7 million households with incomes below the poverty line pay more than half their income in rent, with a recommended monthly housing cost of just $224. No construction boom produces housing at that price point without deep subsidy.

## Key Points

- **What the law does**: Streamlines financing and construction of new housing. Useful for middle-income supply. The National Low-Income Housing Coalition has a detailed guide.
- **What it doesn't do**: Address the needs of eviction court clients, SSI recipients, Social Security seniors, low-wage parents, or anyone whose income cannot support market-rate rent -- regardless of how much supply is added.
- **The $224 floor**: Households at or below the poverty line can afford roughly $224/month in rent. No for-profit landlord will agree to that, no matter how many units get built. Alan Mallach (Center for Community Progress) documented this in 2019: "Rents Will Only Go So Low, No Matter How Much We Build."
- **The policy gap**: Only 1 in 4 eligible households currently receives housing vouchers. The Center on Budget and Policy Priorities has argued directly that supply alone is insufficient -- rental assistance must be part of the response.
- **The National Housing Law Project response**: "This is progress but it is no silver bullet... Until there is a whole-of-government approach to save our homes and protect the people who live in them, the crisis will continue."
- **What real reform looks like**: The Green New Deal for Public Housing Act is cited as the standard to hold out for -- high-quality public housing at scale.

## Relevance

This note is directly relevant to the Maryland Housing Certainty Act synthesis thread. The ROAD to Housing Act critique maps precisely onto the tension in Maryland between supply-side reformers (who push permitting streamlining, ADUs, LMC reform) and tenant protection advocates (who want rent stabilization, eviction protection, voucher expansion). The Quigley/Mallach argument -- supply helps middle-income renters but not the bottom of the market -- is the strongest rebuttal to supply-only framing and should inform how Frederick positions its tenant protection package alongside any housing production agenda.

The 7 million households / $224/month figure is a useful anchor statistic for council remarks and public communications on why production and protection must move together.
