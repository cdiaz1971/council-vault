# Housing -- Faith-Owned Land as Infill Housing Opportunity

## Summary

A piece by Eli Smith of the Southern Urbanism Substack argues that faith-owned land can accommodate significantly more housing than congregations typically assume. Using site studies from the Kronberg Urbanists and Architects Homelessness and Incremental Housing Toolkit, Smith demonstrates that 5 units can fit on a 0.15-acre single lot, and 11 units on a 0.31-acre double lot, while still creating usable common space. The key design principles are courtyard orientation, compact unit sizes, and attention to beauty.

## Key Points

The central claim is that people systematically underestimate how much housing small lots can hold. A 50' x 135' lot -- smaller than most church side yards or parking overflow areas -- can accommodate 5 units arranged to minimize the sense of crowding. Doubling the lot to 0.31 acres opens up a central green courtyard that makes the density feel livable rather than cramped.

Smith focuses on faith-based organizations specifically because they often hold underutilized land adjacent to sanctuaries -- excess parking, vacant fields, unused side lots -- that could be converted without disrupting the congregation's core use of its property.

The design argument connects directly to a community acceptance argument: beautiful, well-designed small-scale infill is harder to oppose than large-scale development, and it creates residents who have a stake in caring for the place. The example used is a cottage court in Decatur, Georgia.

Smith notes that congregations can adapt the unit mix to their mission -- smaller studios for transitional housing, larger units for families -- without fundamentally changing the site planning approach. The flexibility is in the unit types, not the land efficiency principles.

## Why This Matters for César

This is directly relevant to Frederick's housing production goals and the Housing Production Target Resolution. Faith institutions hold significant land in Frederick that is not on the city's radar as a housing site. A targeted outreach strategy to congregations -- framed around their own mission and existing land rather than rezoning or subsidy -- could unlock supply that bypasses the LMC review debate entirely.

It also offers a counter-narrative to the claim that density requires sacrificing neighborhood character. Cottage courts and courtyard infill can be genuinely beautiful and community-building, not just tolerated.
