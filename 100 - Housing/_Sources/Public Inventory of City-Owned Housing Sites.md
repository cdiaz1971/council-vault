# Public Inventory of City-Owned Housing Sites

## Summary

New York City's LIFT Tracker publicly maps more than 100 city-owned sites with capacity for over 50,000 homes. It is a model for pairing the city-owned land RFP and the housing dashboard recommendations in César's housing resolution.

## Key Points

In July 2026 the Mamdani administration launched the LIFT Tracker, a public inventory of more than 100 city-owned sites that together could deliver more than 50,000 new homes. It sits under the administration's Block-by-Block Housing Plan, and individual projects like 100 Gold Street move through it into public review.

The tool does two jobs at once. Internally, it forces the city to identify what it owns and assess each site's housing potential before any disposition decision. Externally, it makes that pipeline visible to residents, developers, and advocates, so progress or stalling on public land is a matter of public record rather than something buried in agency files.

## Relevance

The Resolution on Affordable Housing Action includes both a city-owned land RFP and a housing dashboard among its nine priority recommendations. The LIFT Tracker shows those two items work better as one. An RFP needs an inventory of candidate sites first, and a dashboard that tracks production on public land is the accountability mechanism for whether the RFP actually delivers.

Frederick already has much of the raw material. The City's public ArcGIS server at spires.cityoffrederick.com exposes parcel data as queryable feature services (see [[Frederick City GIS Server -- Query Reference]]), so a first-pass inventory of City-owned parcels could likely be pulled without new software. The harder and more political step is the housing-capacity assessment for each site, which would need Planning Director Collard's office. [[GIS for Affordable Housing Community Buy-In -- Fairfax County Case]] shows how a map like this can also do public persuasion work, not just internal planning.

A public inventory also changes the politics. Once sites are listed with their housing potential, leaving a parcel idle becomes a visible choice the administration has to defend, which gives the Council leverage on an issue where Mayor O'Connor has been resistant.
