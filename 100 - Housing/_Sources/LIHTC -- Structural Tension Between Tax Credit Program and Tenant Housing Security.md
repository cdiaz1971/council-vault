# LIHTC -- Structural Tension Between Tax Credit Program and Tenant Housing Security

## Summary

A 2026 academic article in Housing Policy Debate identifies a fundamental design conflict within the Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) program: the financial math required to make LIHTC projects viable for developers systematically works against stable, affordable rents for the lowest-income tenants the program is meant to serve.

## Key Points

LIHTC is the federal government's primary tool for affordable housing production, costing approximately $13.5 billion annually. But its design produces an internal contradiction.

LIHTC rents are calculated as a percentage of Area Median Income (AMI) -- typically set for households at 50-60% AMI -- not based on tenants' actual incomes. Meanwhile, nearly half of all LIHTC tenants have incomes at or below 30% of AMI (extremely low-income). This mismatch means roughly 37.5% of LIHTC tenants are rent-burdened even in units that qualify as "affordable."

The AMI-based rent formula also creates a rent-increase mechanism that is disconnected from tenant incomes. When AMI rises sharply due to inflation -- as in 2022 -- LIHTC rents can jump double digits in a single year. Many tenants were displaced. The program lacks the rent increase caps and tenant protections common in other federal affordable housing programs.

Advocacy organizations (National Housing Law Project, NLIHC) have pushed the IRS and Treasury to impose rent increase limits in the program. Eleven state housing finance agencies have imposed their own caps, ranging from 2% (NYC, South Carolina) to 10% (Nevada, California). Federal rules do not require this.

The structural issue: developers need to maximize rents to make project financing pencil out. That pressure is built into the tax credit model. Any reform that meaningfully caps rents affects developer returns and therefore project feasibility -- the "math problem" named in the article title.

A related study of California LIHTC projects (2011-2023) found that only 15% of units were priced affordable to extremely low-income households, despite ELI households comprising the majority of LIHTC tenants.

## Relevance

This is directly relevant to Frederick's LMC review and any consideration of inclusionary zoning or tax credit financing for affordable housing. If Frederick relies on LIHTC as a production tool -- as most jurisdictions do -- it inherits this tension. Units may be built and counted as "affordable" while remaining inaccessible or unstable for the lowest-income residents.

The rent increase mechanism is also relevant to any Frederick discussion of rent stabilization: LIHTC units are typically exempt from local rent control, which compounds the problem.
