# A 20 Percent Rent Drop Cuts Poverty as Much as the Expanded Child Tax Credit

## Summary

After zoning and permitting reforms, Austin and Minneapolis saw inflation-adjusted rents fall about 20 percent. Parolin estimates a drop that size would cut poverty 18 to 26 percent and child poverty 21 to 38 percent in high-cost states, on par with the 2021 child tax credit and at no public cost.

## Key Points

Austin and Minneapolis updated zoning and permitting rules to allow more homes, especially apartments and townhouses. In both, inflation-adjusted rents have since fallen roughly 20 percent, and renters save more than $4,000 a year compared with paying average national rent growth.

Parolin modeled what a 20 percent rent decline would do in the ten high-cost states, Maryland among them. Overall poverty would fall 18 to 26 percent, and child poverty would fall 21 to 38 percent. For families with children, the poverty threshold would drop by $3,905 to $5,550 depending on the state, comparable to the per-child value of the temporary 2021 child tax credit expansion, which produced a record drop in child poverty while it lasted.

The mechanism is supply. Separate research cited by Pew finds new housing sets off moving chains, where households moving into new units free up older ones, and that older, cheaper apartments and lower-income neighborhoods see the largest rent declines when new supply arrives.

Pew is explicit about the limit. The lowest-income households will still need vouchers and LIHTC-financed units. Supply lowers the floor for everyone else but does not replace subsidy for those at the bottom.

## Relevance

This is the direct answer to the claim that new market-rate housing only helps affluent households, a line that comes up from Citizens for Responsible Growth and others in Frederick's ADU and zoning fights. The research says the opposite: enough new supply to push down rents reduces poverty, with the largest gains for children.

It also strengthens the case behind the Resolution on Affordable Housing Action and the broader production push. DHCD's 5,194-unit benchmark for the City and the resolution's 4,000-unit floor can be framed not only as housing targets but as anti-poverty policy, and the subsidy caveat fits the resolution's structure, which pairs production reforms with MPDU, Housing Fund, and CDFI tools.

Useful framing line for public use: a rent decline from added supply delivers a benefit the size of the expanded child tax credit without a dollar of public spending.
