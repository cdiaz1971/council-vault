# Food Access and SNAP

## Frederick

- **The burden.** Food is a major line in the ALICE Household Survival Budget. The Maryland 2024 averages are $513 a month for a single adult and $1,392–1,578 for two adults with two children. Frederick County's 2024 ALICE threshold for households under 65 is $88,330.
- **The eligibility gap.** SNAP eligibility ends at 130% of the federal poverty level. A single adult earning $35,000 is far above the poverty line, ineligible for SNAP, and still well short of the ALICE threshold. That's the group city-level food infrastructure matters most for.

  ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]])
- **Who's below the threshold (2024 data).**
  - City of Frederick: 42.7% of households (14,343 of 33,584).
  - Frederick County: 31.8%.
  - By ZIP: 21702 (west Frederick and the Golden Mile) and 21703 (southwest) are at 40%, 21701 at 37%, and 21704 at 20%.
  - In Maryland, 75% of single-mother households are below the threshold.

  ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]]) Earlier 2023 figures are on [[Housing Affordability and Inequality]].
- **Food deserts.** Eight census tracts in the City, home to about 33,600 people, qualify as low-income and low-access at USDA's half-mile urban threshold (2019 data). None qualifies at one mile, so the barrier is within walking distance.
  - About 14,300 residents in these tracts live more than half a mile from a supermarket, including 3,292 children and 1,539 seniors.
  - 1,478 households have no vehicle.
  - Tracts 750801 and 751003 are also flagged for low vehicle access.
  - The lowest-income tracts (772200, 750505, 750504) have poverty rates above 15%.

  ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]])
- **A Golden Mile address.** 1003 W Patrick Street sits in tract 750504. The Maryland Community Business Compass places it in the "City of Frederick – Patrick Street Corridor" Sustainable Community, the City Enterprise Zone, and a New Markets Tax Credit eligible tract (15% poverty). The tract's median household income is $89,815, against a county median of $127,188 (Maryland Community Business Compass, checked 2026-09-25).
- **Existing assets.** Frederick's food assistance is real but mostly crisis-oriented:
  - the City of Frederick Food Bank, run by Frederick Community Action Agency (3–5 day supplies, twice a month)
  - the Frederick Rescue Mission (2.5 million pounds a year)
  - Farm to School Veggie Vans in two low-income communities
  - United Way's Ride United transportation
  - Frederick County's $500,000 FEED grants (November 2025), which fund the Maryland Food Bank, Seton Center, a YMCA produce market, and rides to food

  ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]])
- **What the City can do.** César's tiering of City authority:
  - **Direct:** commercial zoning (Golden Mile zoning is food policy), a DHCD food desert designation, permit-fee waivers for food retail, and City land for food operators.
  - **Partner:** sustained funding for FCAA and the YMCA market, a City facility for a nonprofit grocery operator, and farmers-market SNAP/WIC acceptance.
  - **Advocacy:** SNAP, WIC, and SNAP-Ed.

  ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]])
- **Planning-stage food items** are tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].

## Maryland financing tools

- **Fresh Food Financing Initiative.** HB 451 (2014) provides flexible financing through DHCD's Neighborhood BusinessWorks for businesses and nonprofits in designated food desert areas or Sustainable Communities. Local governments apply to DHCD for a food desert designation ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]]).
- **NourishMD.** Launched in January 2026, it gives food retailers $25,000–150,000 grants in areas scoring 60 or higher on the Compass's Fresh Food Gap Score ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]]).

## Federal SNAP cuts

- **What changed.** H.R. 1, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act:
  - expanded work requirements and ended exemptions for older adults, veterans, homeless people, former foster youth, and some rural residents
  - removed state waiver flexibility
  - eliminated SNAP-Ed

  ([[H.R. 1 -- SNAP Work Requirements and State Impact]]; [[Federal SNAP Cuts Are Overwhelming Local Food Systems]]) César's landscape note puts the SNAP cut at $279 billion through 2034 ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]]).
- **People losing benefits.** Stateline reports more than 4 million people lost SNAP between February 2025 and February 2026. The average benefit is about $188 a month. Pantries are rationing protein, and the New York Fed found food insecurity above its pandemic peak ([[Federal SNAP Cuts Are Overwhelming Local Food Systems]]). CBPP's SNAP Tracker counts 3.5 million people pushed off, including hundreds of thousands of children ([[SNAP Tracker -- 3.5 Million Pushed Off Food Assistance Under Republican Megabill]]).
- **States now share the cost.** Beginning in federal fiscal 2028 (October 2027), states with payment error rates above 6% must pay 5%, 10%, or 15% of benefit costs. The national error rate was 10.62% in FY2025 ([[SNAP Payment Error Rate Compliance Cliff Federal Cost-Sharing Penalty]]). Error rates measure administrative mistakes, not fraud ([[Maryland SNAP Penalty Trap -- Improving Error Rates Triggers Federal Cost-Sharing Cliff]]). 210 bipartisan mayors, including Baltimore's, asked the Senate Agriculture Committee to reverse the cuts, and experts project state costs above $9 billion ([[200 Mayors Call on Senate to Reverse SNAP Cuts]]).
- **Maryland's trap.**
  - Maryland cut its error rate from 35.56% (2022) to 13.64% (FY2024) to 13.08% (FY2025).
  - The law gives a one-year delay only to states whose rate times 1.5 is at least 20. Maryland's FY2024 rate would have qualified (20.46), but its improved FY2025 rate doesn't (19.62).
  - So the state faces at least $240 million in cost-sharing in the 15% tier.
  - The state is continuing to lower its rate anyway.

  ([[Maryland SNAP Penalty Trap -- Improving Error Rates Triggers Federal Cost-Sharing Cliff]])
- **Error-rate technology.** Nevada, at 6.22% with nearly $50 million of exposure, uses a SAS platform to rank cases by error risk for caseworkers to review first. In its first months it saved about $330,000 across 3,000 reviews. It flags problems such as rent entries far off ZIP-code averages. The tool assists caseworkers rather than deciding cases ([[AI-Assisted SNAP Case Triage Nevada SAS Platform]]).

## Local models

- **Dignity-first free groceries.** Prince George's County opened the Fairmount Five Market inside a library branch with Goodr, which has 34 stores nationally. It's funded by a county grant and serves 200+ households a month, mostly seniors above SNAP limits. Participants shop on a schedule with a reusable bag, with no markers of charity ([[Library-Embedded Free Grocery Store as Food Access Infrastructure]]). Goodr's founder distinguishes "access to meals" (a box of donations) from "access to food" (choosing what you cook). Programs designed as infrastructure rather than charity draw more participation and broader support ([[Dignity-First Design in Free Food Programs]]).
- **Municipal grocery ownership.** A Local Progress series laid out four models:
  - **full municipal operation:** highest control and risk
  - **nonprofit operator with City space or subsidy:** the most tested in mid-size cities
  - **cooperative:** strong on equity, slower to scale
  - **hybrid**

  Examples include Atlanta's city-backed Azalea Market and state-backed grocery development in Illinois ([[Municipal Grocery Ownership Models -- From Vision to Operation]]).
- **Grocery benefits above SNAP limits.** Montgomery County's MC Groceries Program gives families with children who aren't SNAP-eligible $100 per child a month, up to $400, in online grocery benefits ([Montgomery County Office of Food Systems Resilience](https://www.montgomerycountymd.gov/office-food-systems-resilience/our-initiatives/mc-groceries-program)).
- **Peer framing.** Seattle's Executive Order 2025-10 committed the city to a food access agenda, including a workgroup and a Food Action Plan. Pierce County, Washington added $400,000 when food bank visits rose 40% during late-2025 SNAP disruptions ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]]).
- **Community food systems.** After Hurricane Maria in Puerto Rico, which imports about 85% of its food and where a third of residents were food-insecure, mutual aid kitchens, cooperatives, and school farms became lasting infrastructure. Federal cuts to FEMA grants and farm programs now threaten them ([[Puerto Rico -- Community-Led Food Sovereignty After Disaster]]).
