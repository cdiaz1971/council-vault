# Dallas Parking Reform Produced Concrete Results -- Peer City Evidence

## Summary

Dallas stripped parking minimums in 2025 and the early results are concrete: one senior housing project avoided adding 200 parking spaces it would not have needed, preserving two trees and reducing project cost. The NLC has framed workforce housing shortages as no longer a big-city problem.

## Key Points

Dallas approved parking and building code changes in 2025. One documented early outcome: a senior housing project in the city was permitted to expand without adding 200 parking spaces the old rules would have required. That freed capital, reduced land consumption, and preserved two trees on site. The project's planner framed it plainly: parking reform is about adding and preserving more humanity, not just producing more housing.

The American Planning Association, presenting at an NLC panel in Salt Lake City, stated that the housing crisis is no longer concentrated in high-cost coastal metros. Workforce housing shortages are now showing up in smaller regions. The implication for Frederick is direct: the supply gap is not someone else's problem.

Dallas already issues permits for and sells more new homes than most other US cities -- yet 72 percent of renters with mid-range incomes were cost-burdened in 2022, and fewer than 20 percent of renters could afford a median-priced home. The deficit of rental homes affordable at 50 percent AMI was nearly 40,000 units as of 2022, projected to grow past 70,000 by 2033 without intervention. High permitting volume alone does not solve the affordability problem.

Smart Cities Dive's related coverage includes a note that Frederick County, Maryland is "plotting a more active approach to housing shortages" -- regional context is developing around the same supply conversation Frederick City is navigating.

## Relevance

Frederick's zoning code ties parking requirements to land use in ways that may constrain infill development and affordable projects near transit corridors and mixed-use zones. The Dallas data point -- removing a parking requirement freed capital and preserved community assets -- translates well in council discussion because it is concrete, not theoretical.

The NLC framing validates the urgency of Frederick's housing work. Pre-approved architectural plans for ADUs or small multifamily buildings is a specific tool worth exploring with Planning and Permitting -- see [[Pre-Approved Architectural Plans and Electronic Permitting as Supply-Side Tools]].
