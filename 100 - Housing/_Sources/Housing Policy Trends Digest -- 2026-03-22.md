# Housing Policy Trends Digest — March 22, 2026

**To:** Frederick City Council  
**From:** Council President's Office  
**Date:** March 22, 2026  
**Re:** Housing Policy Trends — National and Maryland, 2023–2026  
**Coverage:** 36 months ending March 22, 2026

---

## Overview

The national housing landscape is characterized by persistent undersupply, deteriorating affordability, and an expanding wave of state-level zoning preemption that is compressing the range of tools available to local governments. Median home prices nationally have continued to rise above what the majority of households can afford, while construction of entry-level and workforce housing has remained structurally inadequate in most metro areas. The dynamic that defined high-cost West Coast and Northeast markets a decade ago — restrictive zoning, limited supply response to demand, accelerating cost burden — has spread to mid-size cities in the South and Mid-Atlantic. Frederick fits squarely within the affected tier.

In Maryland, the framing has shifted from voluntary supply targets to active preemption pressure. Governor Moore's three-bill 2026 housing package — the Starter and Silver Homes Act (HB 239/SB 36), the Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act (HB 894/SB 389), and the Housing Certainty Act (HB 548/SB 325) — represents the most coordinated state-level housing push in decades. The Maryland Association of Counties has countered with its own vehicle, the BAMBY Act. As of the date of this digest, the Housing Certainty Act has passed the House with overwhelming bipartisan support (123-8) and has been referred to the Senate Economic Matters Committee; it is the bill most likely to be enacted in this session.[^1] The Starter and Silver Homes Act and Transit and Housing Opportunity Act have been heard in committee but no floor votes have occurred. All bills carry an effective date of October 1, 2026 if enacted. Frederick must treat the remainder of this session and the period between session end and October 1 as the window for analysis and code preparation.

## I. Zoning Reform and Missing Middle Housing

The national trajectory over the past three years is consistent: states and cities are moving to legalize missing middle typologies by right, reduce or eliminate parking minimums, and streamline permitting, with or without local cooperation. Oregon was the first state to mandate duplexes by right statewide (2019), and the model has diffused rapidly. Montana enacted sweeping upzones in 2023. California extended its ADU and small multifamily legalization. Florida, Texas, and Arizona have all seen legislative pressure to preempt local single-family exclusion zones. In the Mid-Atlantic, Virginia passed legislation in 2023 allowing by-right duplexes statewide, and Delaware followed in 2024 with modest lot-size reforms. Maryland is the next state in the sequence.

Within Maryland, the peer-city comparison is instructive. Montgomery County has had MPDU-supported multifamily permitting as a baseline since 1974, but its suburban nodes remain under-zoned for missing middle typologies. Rockville and Gaithersburg have both pursued transit-adjacent upzones in recent years, largely through small-area plans rather than citywide zoning revisions. College Park has moved aggressively on ADU and small-lot infill around its university core. Hagerstown's context is closer to Frederick's — a mid-size city outside the main DC corridor — but Hagerstown has not pursued significant zoning modernization and offers limited comparative value for Frederick's legislative sequence.

The most directly relevant national case study is not a large coastal city. A January 2026 analysis identifies Dallas as having revised its building code to apply a streamlined residential code standard to buildings up to eight units and three stories, eliminating sprinkler requirements and reducing per-unit construction costs for small multifamily development. The same analysis notes that smaller cities are now facing the same workforce housing crisis previously associated with high-cost metros, with limited staff capacity as the primary obstacle for jurisdictions like Frederick.[^2] The framing is instructive: the constraint on missing middle production is not only zoning but also the building code, which classifies small multifamily structures under commercial rather than residential standards and imposes cost premiums that make three- and four-unit buildings economically marginal. Frederick should confirm whether Maryland's state building code creates the same dynamic and whether LMC reform alone is sufficient, or whether a building code review is also warranted.

HB 239 and SB 36 were introduced January 14, 2026, heard in their respective committees in February, and await further action as of this writing.[^3] The bills would prohibit municipalities from applying specific zoning standards — setbacks, lot coverage, design requirements — to qualifying "starter" and "silver" homes, and would enable property owners on improved lots to subdivide into smaller lots under certain conditions. The Maryland Municipal League testified acknowledging the housing supply goals but raising concerns about local APFO and infrastructure capacity guardrails.[^4]

For Frederick specifically, the baseline note establishes what is at stake. R4 requires an 8,000 sq ft minimum lot; R6 requires 7,000 sq ft. Both would be preempted if the bill passes in its current form. R4 and R6 currently permit only single-family detached dwellings — no duplexes, no townhouses, no triplexes. A proactive LMC amendment in these zones, ahead of state action, would allow Frederick to manage the transition on its own terms and frame it as council-initiated policy rather than state-compelled compliance.

## II. Anti-Displacement Strategies

The most significant development in tenant protection at the state level in Maryland occurred in 2024, when the General Assembly passed the Renters Rights and Stabilization Act (HB 693). That legislation established a statewide Office of Tenant and Landlord Affairs within DHCD, created a mandatory right of first refusal for tenants in one-to-three-unit properties — requiring landlords to notify qualifying tenants before selling to a third party and allowing tenants 30 days to make a purchase offer — and mandated the creation of the Maryland Tenants' Bill of Rights.[^5] The Bill of Rights took effect July 1, 2025 and must be attached to all new residential leases. Maryland is the first state in the nation to establish a statewide Tenants' Bill of Rights by mandate, and it is updated annually by DHCD by September 1 each year.[^6]

What state law does not provide is just cause eviction protection, local rental registration authority above what municipalities may separately enact, relocation assistance requirements, or rent stabilization. Some county and city governments have established rental unit licensing requirements or additional renter protections at the local level; DHCD has indicated it will provide a list of local offices of tenant rights.[^7] No Maryland city of Frederick's size has enacted a just cause eviction ordinance. Among Maryland peer cities, a rental registry remains the lowest-barrier local tool available and faces no state preemption obstacle.

Nationally, right of first refusal ordinances have expanded as anti-displacement mechanisms for naturally occurring affordable housing and small multifamily properties. Washington, D.C.'s Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Act remains the strongest regional model, applying to all rental properties regardless of unit count. The state right of first refusal for 1-3 unit properties took effect October 2024; properties above that threshold remain uncovered by state law, and no Maryland municipality has enacted coverage for larger buildings.[^8]

## III. Community Land Trusts and Public Land Models

Community land trust activity in Maryland has grown, though it remains concentrated in Baltimore City and the D.C. suburban counties. The South Baltimore Community Land Trust, supported through DHCD's Reinvest Baltimore initiative, is the most active Baltimore-area CLT and provides the strongest in-state city-CLT partnership model.[^9]

The most instructive Maryland peer example for Frederick is the College Park Community Preservation Trust. Launched in 2023, it was capitalized with $14.9 million in combined funding from the City of College Park, the State of Maryland through DHCD, federal Community Project Funding, and the Metropolitan Washington Council of Governments. As of 2024, the Trust had acquired nine single-family homes, completed renovation, and sold all nine to income-qualified buyers under permanent affordability covenants. The Trust is one of approximately fifteen affordable housing land trusts in Maryland.[^10] The funding structure — combining city seed funding, DHCD grants, and federal dollars — is replicable without Frederick operating a CLT internally.

The critical design distinction between a CLT and the MPDU model is permanence. The CLT holds land in perpetuity and resells only the structure under a ground lease that permanently caps resale prices, preventing subsidy recycling back to the market.[^11] Frederick's current MPDU control period is 40 years, after which units return to market pricing. For Frederick, a CLT partnership would be most appropriately paired with a public land disposition policy directing surplus city-owned parcels to CLT use rather than open-market sale. No such disposition policy exists in Frederick's code as of the March 2025 LMC supplement.

## IV. Local Housing Revenue Tools

The most consequential Maryland development in this domain over the past three years is Baltimore City's funded inclusionary zoning program, which became operational on July 21, 2024, replacing a 2007 program that produced only 34 units in seventeen years due to widespread developer waivers.[^12] Baltimore's current program (Ordinance 24-308) requires multifamily market-rate projects with 20 or more units that receive city financial assistance or a significant land use authorization to set aside 10% of units as affordable for a 30-year compliance period — five percent at or below 50% AMI and five percent at or below 60% AMI.[^13]

The structural innovation is the funding mechanism. Rather than requiring developers to absorb below-market rent as an unfunded cost — which the prior program's waiver culture demonstrated was not sustainable — Baltimore provides a property tax credit equal to the actual forgone rent revenue for each inclusionary unit each year. The credit is calculated annually based on the difference between market rent and the rent charged on each affordable unit, so it automatically adjusts to local market conditions and changes in rent over the 30-year period.[^14]

Analysis of this model by the Sightline Institute identifies Baltimore as one of only three jurisdictions in the United States with a genuinely funded inclusionary zoning program that comes close to eliminating the financial feasibility barrier for developers, alongside Portland and Shoreline, Washington. The core insight is that an IZ mandate without funding is a development cost that produces waiver pressure and unit scarcity — as Frederick's own $2.00 per square foot payment-in-lieu rate demonstrates.[^15] The current rate generates nominal revenue, is not calibrated to actual affordability gaps, and is not scheduled for recalculation for another year or more under the three-year review trigger in §19-6.1. The Baltimore tax credit model is the direct peer comparison for what a substantive reform of Frederick's funded IZ framework would look like.

The BAMBY Act includes a transfer tax provision that would authorize Maryland counties to impose an additional transfer tax of up to 5% on property transfers where an owner-occupied property will no longer be owner-occupied after sale — a housing revenue tool targeted at investor acquisition of owner-occupied stock. The BAMBY Act also includes a statewide responsible owner registry requiring residential property owners to file an annual certificate with DHCD identifying a sworn public contact, which DHCD must share with local governments upon request.[^16] For Frederick, the registry provision is directly relevant to the local rental registry gap and should be monitored before initiating a local ordinance to avoid duplication or conflict with state infrastructure.

Both the Transit and Housing Opportunity Act and the Housing Certainty Act contemplate delaying the collection of development impact fees and excise taxes until after construction is complete for qualifying residential projects. MML has sought amendments to protect municipal budget predictability, flagging concerns about cash flow impacts from delayed fee collection.[^17]

## V. Maryland General Assembly Watch

**Housing Certainty Act — HB 548 / SB 325**

HB 548 passed the House of Delegates on third reading, 123-8, on March 20, 2026 and has been referred to the Senate Economic Matters Committee. SB 325 was engrossed on March 19 and also referred to Economic Matters.[^1] With less than two weeks remaining in the 90-day session (adjournment April 7), this bill is in the final-stage posture most likely to reach the Governor's desk. If enacted, it takes effect October 1, 2026.

The operative provision for Frederick: applications for residential development projects must be governed only by the laws and regulations in effect at the time of submission of a complete application. Vested rights extend for a minimum of five years after project approval. This eliminates the City's current practice of renegotiating development conditions during the approval period and requires Frederick to confirm how its development agreement structure and conditions-of-approval process interact with the new vesting framework. The bill also streamlines impact fee collection. **Action window:** Council should direct Legal and Public Works to assess current development agreement language before October 1, 2026.

**Starter and Silver Homes Act — HB 239 / SB 36**

Both bills were heard in committee in February 2026. As of this writing, neither has had a committee vote or floor action.[^3] Significant amendments are reportedly under negotiation, and the trajectory is uncertain. If enacted in current form, the bill would preempt Frederick's R4 (8,000 sq ft) and R6 (7,000 sq ft) lot size minimums, and R8's single-family minimum (6,000 sq ft); Frederick's R12-R20 minimums (5,000 sq ft) would already comply with the statewide floor. A proactive LMC amendment in R4 and R6 permitting townhouses by right, ahead of state preemption, would allow Frederick to manage the transition on its own terms rather than the state's.

**Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act — HB 894 / SB 389**

HB 894 was placed on special order on March 19, 2026. SB 389 was referred to the Finance and Education, Energy and the Environment Committees; committee hearings occurred in February but no further action is on record. MML's formal position is Favorable with Amendments, specifically seeking modifications to the automatic Enterprise Zone designation for TOD areas and to the impact fee/excise tax payment delay provision.[^17] If enacted, the bill restricts local land use authority within a defined radius of qualifying rail stations receiving at least hourly service Monday through Friday, 8 a.m. to 6 p.m., eliminates local parking minimums for qualifying TOD projects, and mandates mixed-use development near those stations. **Key threshold question for Frederick:** whether MARC Brunswick Line service to Frederick meets the bill's qualifying frequency definition. MARC service to Frederick does not operate at true hourly frequency during the qualifying window; Legal should confirm the coverage analysis before assuming Frederick is or is not subject to the bill's restrictions.

**BAMBY Act (Building Affordably in My Back Yard Act)**

Introduced by Senator Benjamin Brooks and Delegate Nick Allen in January 2026, organized around four pillars: regulatory and construction certainty, development-friendly payment schedules, market tools including the conditional transfer tax authority, and a statewide responsible owner registry. MACo describes a compromise between the BAMBY Act's provisions and Governor Moore's package as potentially close at hand.[^16] The registry provision is a watch item for Frederick's rental registry gap.

**Maryland Housing Production Targets — DHCD December 2025**

DHCD published per-jurisdiction housing production targets on December 31, 2025, tied to a statewide need of 184,784 additional units by 2030.[^18] Frederick City's specific published target has not been independently confirmed in this research. Jurisdictions meeting their targets are eligible for the Maryland Housing Leadership Award; those falling short face reputational and legislative scrutiny. Frederick's Housing Production Target Resolution establishes a 1,400-unit-per-year baseline that Council should calibrate against Frederick's published DHCD target as soon as that figure is confirmed.

## VI. Frederick Ledger

The following reflects the current state of Frederick's position in each policy domain covered by this digest, drawn from the Frederick Housing Policy Baseline (March 22, 2026) as the authoritative code reference.

**Missing middle zoning (R4/R6):** Gap identified, no action. R4 and R6 permit only single-family detached. No ordinance extending duplex, townhouse, or triplex permissions to these zones has been introduced. The LMC Review Commission is the anticipated vehicle, but no timeline or formal scope assignment has been established. State preemption risk by October 1, 2026.

**Parking minimums:** Gap identified, no action. Per-unit minimums remain in all residential districts outside the downtown overlay. Whether MARC stations trigger the Transit and Housing Opportunity Act's parking minimum preemption is an unanalyzed legal question.

**ADU code:** Active work in pipeline. Code was substantially revised in 2021 (Ordinance G-21-15) and permits ADUs by right in most residential zones including R4. The owner-occupancy requirement and 10% cap in planned developments are identified reform targets but no ordinance is pending.

**MPDU baseline rate and payment-in-lieu fee:** Two distinct issues. The 12.5% MPDU baseline is below the 15% target in the Housing Production Target Resolution — closing that gap requires a Chapter 19 amendment. The $2.00/sq ft payment-in-lieu rate is structurally low, not calibrated to actual affordability gaps, and eligible for recalculation in late 2026. This is the clearest near-term legislative lever in the housing ordinance sequence. Baltimore's funded IZ tax credit model is the direct comparative benchmark.

**City Housing Fund capitalization:** Gap identified — structural, not legal. The fund exists in §19-18 with broad authorized uses, but is capitalized only through payment-in-lieu fees and MPDU foreclosure proceeds. No general fund appropriation, transfer fee revenue, or bond capitalization exists. Actual current fund balance is unconfirmed.

**Tenant protections / rental registry:** Gap identified, no action. No local rental registry, just cause eviction ordinance, relocation assistance requirement, or rent stabilization exists beyond state law. The state right of first refusal (October 2024) and Tenants' Bill of Rights (July 2025) are the operative floor. The BAMBY Act's statewide responsible owner registry provision is a watch item before initiating local ordinance drafting.

**Junk fees ordinance:** Active research phase, drafting not initiated. Research complete. City attorney confirmation of preemption risk and covered-unit definition required before drafting proceeds.

**CLT or public land disposition policy:** Gap identified, no action. No CLT relationship, no city-CLT partnership, and no public land disposition policy exists. College Park's Community Preservation Trust is the closest peer model. No current pipeline.

**Housing production target resolution follow-through:** Active. The resolution has been introduced establishing a 1,400-unit annual target and 15% affordability framework with a 33% sub-target at 60% AMI. Subsequent ordinance work — Chapter 19 MPDU amendment, payment-in-lieu recalibration, and funded IZ mechanism — has not been initiated. Frederick's specific DHCD-published production target has not been confirmed against the 1,400-unit baseline.
