# Abundance Model vs Vienna Model -- Housing Politics Typology

Two frameworks dominate serious housing policy discourse. Understanding them is useful for positioning Frederick's own approach and anticipating political dynamics in state and regional conversations.

## The abundance model

The abundance model is supply-side and market-oriented. It holds that the core problem is insufficient housing production, and the core solution is removing barriers to that production: reforming zoning, reducing permitting costs, streamlining approvals, and allowing market forces to filter housing downward through price tiers over time.

It is technocratic and utilitarian -- focused on maximizing individual housing choice through efficient markets. It tends to be skeptical of regulation and agnostic about housing tenure (renting vs. owning), seeing both as legitimate outcomes of individual preference.

Its weakness, per this analysis, is that it divorces housing from questions of communal identity and meaning. It has no answer for why institutional investors owning single-family homes feels wrong to most voters -- because its framework treats all supply as equivalent regardless of who owns it.

## The Vienna model

Named for Vienna's century-old social housing tradition, this model emphasizes non-market housing as a structural requirement. It argues that housing markets are inherently prone to boom-bust cycles, rent extraction, and undersupply for lower-income households. The solution is a large public or cooperative housing sector (20%+ of housing nationally, 40%+ in major cities) that operates outside market dynamics.

It comes in two political flavors: a social-democratic urbanism flavor (density, transit, "15-minute city," tenure neutrality) and a socialist flavor (working-class focus, statism, hostility to privatization and right-to-buy schemes).

Its weakness is political: homeownership is deeply popular, and neither flavor of the Vienna model offers a compelling path to homeownership for working people. The social-democratic version can read as a professional-class lifestyle preference; the socialist version is perceived as nostalgic.

## Why both are in trouble

Both models are losing political traction with a growing cohort of voters who are: pro-homeownership, anti-institutional investor, skeptical of both market solutions and large state housing programs. This cohort transcends the traditional left-right axis. Their politics are nationalist rather than cosmopolitan on housing -- they want housing for "people like them," not abstract market efficiency or state provision.

## Why this matters for César

Frederick's housing politics sit at this intersection. The [[Maryland Housing Certainty Act]] and [[LMC reform]] work engages abundance-model tools (zoning reform, by-right permitting), while the city's equity commitments push toward Vienna-model goals (affordability, access for low-income residents). Understanding the political landscape -- and the emerging cohort that neither model speaks to -- is useful for framing Frederick's approach in ways that build durable coalition support.
