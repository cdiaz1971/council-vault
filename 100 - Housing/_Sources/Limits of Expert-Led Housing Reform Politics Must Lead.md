# Limits of Expert-Led Housing Reform Politics Must Lead

## Summary

An Irish housing policy commentator argues from personal experience that experts -- including progressive ones -- have been repeatedly wrong about housing solutions, and that framing housing crises as solvable by experts depoliticizes the problem and undermines democratic legitimacy.

## Key Points

The author, an Irish housing policy analyst, offers a candid self-critique. In the mid-2010s, he and other left-leaning commentators argued that three interventions would solve Ireland's housing crisis: 10,000 social housing units per year, rent regulation, and a cost rental sector. All three have now been implemented. The crisis is not solved.

His analysis of what went wrong: they underestimated delivery costs, overestimated the transferability of European models (especially Austrian and Danish cost rental), and didn't appreciate how central cost structures were to making the models work.

The broader argument: the discourse of "if only politicians would listen to experts" is politically harmful. It implies democratic governments cannot solve the problem, which undermines trust in democratic institutions. Experts do not resolve complex social issues -- politics does. Politics is the only mechanism for navigating competing interests, trade-offs, and value disagreements. Experts inform that process but cannot replace it.

## Relevance

A useful corrective to technocratic framing in housing debates. When Frederick engages with consultants, planners, and housing advocates pushing specific solutions, this piece is a reminder to ground those conversations in political feasibility and democratic accountability -- not just technical correctness. Also a useful intellectual reference for the [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] work: the goal is political consensus, not just the right number.
