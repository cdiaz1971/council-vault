# Neighborhood Trusts as a Model for Community-Controlled Commercial Real Estate

## Summary

The Kensington Corridor Trust in Philadelphia acquired and stewarded a commercial building at affordable rents, enabling a local food entrepreneur to open the neighborhood's first grocery store in years -- demonstrating how community-controlled real estate entities can bring essential services back to disinvested corridors.

## Key Points

The Kensington Corridor Trust (KCT) is a community-controlled real estate entity that acquires and stewards commercial properties on behalf of neighborhood residents, keeping rents affordable while allowing the community to shape what gets built and who gets to operate there. It is part of a growing category of community-controlled real estate models gaining momentum nationally.

The model in practice: KCT acquired a building at 3462 Kensington Avenue in April 2023 for $225,000. When Thomas Sheridan, a Kensington native who runs a gourmet food import and wholesale business, walked in looking for a way to give back, KCT brought him into a project shaped by years of resident input that consistently identified a grocery store as the neighborhood's top need.

About $800,000 was invested in the building and renovation, combining Sheridan's own $250,000 equipment investment with Department of Commerce grant funding. The renovation prioritized hiring subcontractors from within the 19134 ZIP code, especially people of color. The store includes a shared commercial kitchen available to local food entrepreneurs.

The building also has solar panels, a green roof, and energy resilience improvements planned, funded by the Green Family Foundation -- designed to reduce operating costs for the grocery store and kitchen tenants over time.

The store accepts EBT and prices goods at roughly 30% less than Sheridan's Reading Terminal Market location.

## Relevance

The CLT model the vault has been developing in the context of residential housing has a commercial real estate analogue that KCT demonstrates at scale. For Frederick's affordability agenda, the gap between having a community land trust framework for housing and having one that can hold commercial storefronts and attract mission-aligned tenants is significant. This case study shows what the commercial application looks like operationally.

It also reinforces [[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]] -- the structural mechanism that gets fresh food into a food desert is not charity, it is land control. KCT held the building; that's what made the grocery store possible.
