# Zoning Math as Gentrification Intervention -- Denver's Unlocking Housing Choices

## Summary

Denver's "Unlocking Housing Choices" plan uses zoning reform to change the financial calculus for developers: limit maximum building size, but offer additional square footage in exchange for building more, smaller units. The goal is to make it profitable to build missing middle housing rather than teardown mansions on infill lots.

## Key Points

The problem Denver is trying to solve is specific and structural. Since 2019, and accelerating after 2022, infill redevelopment in Denver has overwhelmingly produced large single-family homes priced at $3-4 million, replacing older homes that cost $300-700k. The financial logic is simple -- developers maximize revenue by maximizing square footage at the highest price per square foot. Zoning rules that allow large homes on single lots make teardown-and-rebuild-luxury the path of least resistance.

The Unlocking Housing Choices approach tries to break that logic. The core mechanism is a size-for-units trade: cap what a single unit can be, but unlock more total square footage if the developer builds multiple smaller units. This makes the per-unit math work for missing middle housing -- duplexes, triplexes, townhomes -- that would otherwise lose to the mansion alternative.

This is a supply-side intervention, but one designed with an anti-displacement frame built in. The city is explicitly trying to shape what kind of housing gets built, not just how much. Whether the financial model actually pencils out at scale -- given construction costs, land costs, and permitting timelines -- is the open question. Denver's permitting process for larger projects averages 264 days, which adds carrying costs that can eat the density bonus.

## Relevance

Frederick's LMC modernization and Housing Production Target Resolution both implicitly assume that upzoning will produce more affordable housing. Denver's experience complicates that assumption: upzoning without design constraints can accelerate teardown and replacement with luxury product. The Unlocking Housing Choices mechanism -- size limits paired with density bonuses -- is exactly the kind of tool Frederick could consider layering into LMC reform to shape production outcomes, not just permit more units. This also connects to the [[MPDU Reform -- Chapter 19 Amendment]] gap note: payment-in-lieu fees and affordability requirements need to be calibrated against actual developer math, not aspirational targets.
