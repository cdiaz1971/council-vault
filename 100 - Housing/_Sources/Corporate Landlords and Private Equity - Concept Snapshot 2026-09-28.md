# Corporate Landlords and Private Equity

## Frederick context

- **Related local work.**
  - The Rockville Chapter 18 model, including its ban on algorithmic rent pricing, and Frederick's planning-stage tenant package are on [[Tenant Protections]].
  - Transfers of neglected buildings to nonprofits or tenants, and community land trusts, are on [[Community Land Trusts and Social Ownership]].
  - The ROAD Act's dropped rule forcing investors to sell build-to-rent homes is on [[Federal Housing Policy]].
  - The voter cohort hostile to institutional investors is on [[Housing Politics and Advocacy]].

## How the private equity model extracts value

- **The mechanics.** These create structural incentives for rent hikes, evictions, and deferred maintenance, whatever an individual firm intends:
  - **Little of their own money:** firms invest as little as 1–2% of their own capital, with the rest from institutional investors (including pension funds) and debt.
  - **"2 and 20":** a 2% annual management fee plus 20% of profits above a 6–8% threshold.
  - **Short holds:** typically 3–7 years.
  - **Opacity:** firms are largely exempt from SEC disclosure.
  - **Dividend recapitalization:** properties take on new debt to pay investors, raising bankruptcy and displacement risk unrelated to tenants.

  ([[Private Equity Housing Model How Extraction Works]])
- **Southern Towers.** CIM Group bought the 2,346-unit Alexandria, Virginia complex in 2020 for $506 million, targeting "outsized rent growth." It filed 250 evictions within six months, during a moratorium, and tenants reported rent hikes and deferred maintenance ([[Private Equity Housing Model How Extraction Works]]).
- **Scale.** Private funds grew from $1 trillion in 2004 to more than $16 trillion in 2025. Private equity owns about 13% of US apartment units. Many workers harmed by these landlords have pensions invested in the same firms ([[Private Equity Housing Model How Extraction Works]]).

## Policy levers

- **Rent stabilization** caps the projected rent growth private equity underwrites. Blackstone left Sweden after it adopted good cause eviction. New York's 2019 Housing Stability and Tenant Protection Act closed regulation loopholes ([[Policy Levers to Counter Private Equity in Housing]]).
- **LLC transparency** lets tenants find their real owner. New York's law covers only foreign LLCs, and Gov. Hochul vetoed an expansion in 2025 ([[Policy Levers to Counter Private Equity in Housing]]).
- **Carried-interest reform** would end taxing managers' profit share as capital gains. It's a federal subsidy for high-risk housing plays ([[Policy Levers to Counter Private Equity in Housing]]).
- **Social housing and patient capital** take units out of the speculative market permanently: community land trusts, tenant and community opportunity-to-purchase acts (TOPA/COPA), funded public housing, and capital that doesn't require private equity returns ([[Policy Levers to Counter Private Equity in Housing]]).

## Tenant organizing

- **Find the real owner.** Trace layered LLCs and management companies back to the firm. Research its portfolio, its track record, its executives, and which pension funds hold stakes ([[Tenant Organizing Against Private Equity Landlords]]).
- **Escalate to the money.**
  - **Code enforcement:** it has more force if liability reaches the firm itself.
  - **Pension investors:** Southern Towers tenants broke CIM's refusal to negotiate by contacting its investors, San Francisco's employee retirement system and Pennsylvania's school employees' fund.
  - **Federal lenders:** a complaint to Freddie Mac, which guaranteed $346.7 million of CIM's loan, led to the first FHFA tenant-protection requirements for multifamily finance. The source says they were gutted in April 2025.
  - **Local law:** Providence tenants won a ban on algorithmic rent pricing.
  - **State attorneys general:** organizing in Minnesota prompted AG Keith Ellison's suit against Pretium/HavenBrook, which transferred 345 homes to nonprofits.

  ([[Tenant Organizing Against Private Equity Landlords]])

## REITs and consolidation

- **The "apex predator."** UCLA researcher Alexander Ferrer calls real estate investment trusts (REITs) the corporate-landlord form that extracts the most rent and accumulates the most units with the least regulatory friction ([[REITs as the Apex Predator of the Rental Housing Market]]).
- **The merger-review gap.** REIT consolidations don't face the FTC pre-merger antitrust review other large mergers do. Debt Collective is campaigning for the FTC to close the gap. A city can add supply and still watch it absorbed into REIT portfolios, which is why protection and community ownership have to accompany production ([[FTC REIT Merger Loophole -- The Accountability Gap in Corporate Landlord Policy]]; [[REIT Merger Exemption from FTC Pre-Merger Review -- Corporate Landlord Consolidation Risk]]).
- **Vivmark.** Equity Residential and AvalonBay, flagged in May 2026 as a merger that would create the country's largest corporate landlord, combined into a $69 billion REIT called Vivmark. Debt Collective treats it as a change in organizing terrain and is holding townhalls for tenants in the merged company's buildings ([[REIT Merger Exemption from FTC Pre-Merger Review -- Corporate Landlord Consolidation Risk]]; [[Vivmark Merger of EQR and AvalonBay]]).
