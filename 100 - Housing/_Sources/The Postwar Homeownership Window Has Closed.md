# The Postwar Homeownership Window Has Closed -- Structural Shift Not a Market Cycle

## Summary
The Harvard JCHS 2026 report, analyzed in depth by Fortune, documents that the broad middle-class access to homeownership that characterized the postwar decades (roughly 1945-1973) was a product of specific and unrepeatable structural conditions, not a natural feature of American life. Those conditions have dissolved. The current moment is not a temporary affordability crunch that supply growth or interest rate changes will resolve; it is a durable structural shift in how housing distributes wealth.

## Key Points
The postwar homeownership surge -- a 20-percentage-point increase in a single generation -- depended on specific conditions: strong union density that compressed wages upward, real wage growth that outpaced home price appreciation, federal mortgage programs (FHA, VA) that subsidized entry for a broad (though racially exclusionary) middle class, and home prices that traded at roughly three times median household income.

Each of those conditions has reversed or dissolved. Union density has declined sharply. Real wage growth for non-college workers was largely stagnant for decades. Home prices now trade at nearly five times median household income, compared to three times in the 1990s. The pandemic-era price surge of 54% between 2020 and 2022 compressed the remaining entry space dramatically.

Fortune's synthesis of the Harvard data frames the core shift: housing has moved from an asset that rewards labor income to an asset that rewards prior asset ownership. The gap between homeowners and renters now compounds generationally rather than being bridgeable within a single lifetime of work. As one economist summarized it: "Today, the aspiration is still there, but it is a lot more difficult. You have to be wealthy or lucky."

The Harvard report also identifies a secondary demand shift: immigration reduction and weaker labor markets are suppressing household formation, which is softening some demand metrics -- but this is cold comfort, as it signals economic deterioration, not market normalization.

## Relevance
This note is the analytical frame for the homeownership cluster in the housing thread. Supply production arguments -- which César is pursuing through the Housing Production Target Resolution -- address the volume and cost of units, but not the wealth transmission structure that gates entry. A city can build 1,400 units per year and still see a majority of new homeowners be the children of prior homeowners if financing structure and price levels remain unchanged. The CLT model is the tool that addresses this directly. This structural argument also strengthens the case for inclusionary zoning reform and the MPDU recalibration thread -- if ownership is increasingly inheritance-gated, the 12.5% baseline and $2.00/sq ft payment-in-lieu are not adequate to the scale of the problem. For Frederick specifically, where 72% of children in rental units are ALICE, this is not an abstract policy argument but a documented feature of the city's demographic and economic landscape.
