# Housing Production -- Loft3 Middle Housing Case Study Raleigh

## Summary

Loft3 is a boutique condo development near downtown Raleigh built by developer Steven Wilk that demonstrates missing middle housing can pencil financially without subsidy. The project draws on European and Asian single-stair building models and yields five practical lessons about making small-scale multifamily viable in American cities.

## Key Points

**Lesson 1 -- Floor plan versatility is the foundation.** Smaller units must serve multiple lifestyle configurations. Loft3's layouts were designed to accommodate young professionals, roommates, students, and small families -- a second bedroom could be an office, nursery, or rentable room. Restraint in design (minimal wasted space, consolidated mechanical systems, tight building footprint) is what makes this work.

**Lesson 2 -- Elevators and parking are the biggest cost threats.** At Loft3's scale, an elevator adds roughly $20,000 per unit plus ongoing maintenance; parking can exceed $30,000 per space. The project used a single shared elevator and a podium parking structure on one side, but every parking space still represented a significant tradeoff. A required second stair for fire egress added another $10,000-$15,000 per unit. The core insight: affordability comes from eliminating unnecessary building components, not just from reducing material costs.

**Lesson 3 -- Material choices drive safety more than redundant systems.** Loft3 used cold-formed steel rather than wood framing, dramatically reducing structural fire risk. The project team argues that modern fire-rated assemblies, sprinklers, and compartmentalization are already effective -- and that requirements like additional stairs and oversized fire apparatus are often driven by outdated perception rather than actual risk data.

**Lesson 4 -- Affordability requirements are navigable.** Raleigh's affordable housing density bonus requires a percentage of below-market units. Loft3 worked through this via legal agreements tied to AMI thresholds. City staff were collaborative, financing mechanisms were available, and the bank did not treat affordability as a risk factor. The key is designing the deal backwards from the target price point.

**Lesson 5 -- This model requires a lifestyle shift from residents.** Loft3 works for people who have already rejected sprawl: smaller units, minimal private amenities, walkable location as the core value proposition. The developer's framing: location, walkability, and community are the product -- not square footage. Scaling this model requires finding (or creating) buyers and renters who accept that tradeoff.

## Relevance

Strong direct relevance to Frederick's [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] and the ongoing debate about what types of housing can realistically be produced. The Loft3 case study addresses the "it won't pencil" argument head-on with a real project. The lessons on cost drivers (elevators, parking, egress stairs) are particularly useful when evaluating Frederick's zoning code requirements -- each of those items appears in local building regulations and could be examined in the LMC review context. The lifestyle-shift argument also connects to the walkability and transit conversations in the [[Golden Mile Alliance]] corridor work.
