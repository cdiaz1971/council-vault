# Housing is a Human Right -- Ten Reasons for Realistic Hope Part Two

## Summary
Fran Quigley's continuing series at Housing is a Human Right (Substack) makes the case that structural change is possible by highlighting concrete wins. Part Two covers two reasons: growing union membership and tenant-side tech tools. Both connect to organizing capacity and power-building, not just policy.

## Key Arguments

**Reason 4 -- Unions Grew Larger and Stronger**
In 2025, union membership reached 16.5 million U.S. workers, the highest since 2009. The gains are meaningful not just for unionized workers but for surrounding local economies -- higher union density correlates with higher wages and benefits across the board. Unions are also active on housing rights, healthcare, and immigrant justice. The piece acknowledges the current federal government is hostile to labor, and notes that less than half of workers who want a union have one. The argument is that passage of the PRO Act (Protecting the Right to Organize) would accelerate these gains significantly.

**Reason 5 -- Tech Can Help Tenants Too**
Landlords have used software to coordinate rent prices and screen tenants with inaccurate data. But the same tech ecosystem includes tools that help tenants push back: Landlord Mapper and Who Owns What help identify landlord identities behind complex business structures. Legal self-help tools like Tenant Power Toolkit and MADE (from Greater Boston Legal Services) walk tenants through eviction responses without an attorney. The piece is realistic: tech tools alone don't fix structural power imbalances, and real legal protections plus a right to counsel are still necessary. But these tools matter in the meantime.

## Relevance
Both frames connect directly to the housing work. The union-housing link is underused in the local policy conversation -- labor organizing capacity is part of the political infrastructure needed to pass housing reforms. The tenant tech angle is directly applicable to the question of renter protections in Frederick: what tools exist for tenants right now, before legislation catches up?
