# HUD Work Requirements and Time Limits Would Strip Rental Assistance From 37 Million People

## Summary

The Trump HUD proposed a rule allowing housing agencies and subsidized housing owners to impose work requirements of up to 40 hours per week and time limits as short as two years on rental assistance recipients. Up to 3.7 million people, including 1.9 million children, are at risk of losing assistance under the most restrictive version of the rule. The proposal drew nearly 2,000 public comments, most opposing it.

## Key Points

The rule covers Housing Choice Vouchers (Section 8), public housing, Project-Based Vouchers, and Project-Based Rental Assistance. "Work-eligible" adults are defined as ages 18-61 who are not disabled, pregnant, or in higher education, and who are not primary caregivers for children under six or disabled household members. About half of all assisted households have at least one "work-eligible" adult.

The rule is framed as optional for housing agencies and private owners, but HUD could pressure adoption through funding mechanisms, states with conservative governments could mandate it, and trigger laws already exist in Arkansas and Wisconsin. The framing as "voluntary" does not mean it will remain voluntary.

CBPP estimated that a 40-hour-per-week work requirement would put 3.7 million people at risk. A two-year time limit would put more than 3 million at risk, including more than half of them children. There is not a single community in the US where a minimum wage worker at 40 hours per week can afford a two-bedroom market rate apartment.

Rigorous research on prior work requirement experiments shows they do not increase employment or income. They do increase paperwork burdens, cause housing agencies to lose staff capacity to actual housing work, and result in more families losing assistance without achieving self-sufficiency.

The proposal bypasses Congress, which previously rejected a 40% cut to rental aid and the block grant consolidation of five HUD programs.

## Relevance

Frederick's ALICE rate is 45%. Among District 2 residents, the poverty rate is 1.5x the county rate. Work requirements and time limits would fall heaviest on the population César's district serves. The people most likely to lose Section 8 vouchers under this rule are the same people who make up the 72% of children in rental housing who are ALICE or in poverty.

This is part of the federal disinvestment pattern alongside SNAP cuts and Medicaid threats: the federal government withdrawing from the safety net programs that keep ALICE households in housing, and leaving cities and counties to absorb the consequences through homelessness, school instability, and emergency services. Frederick has no direct lever on HUD rulemaking, but the council can document the local impact, use it to build legislative findings for the Real Rent Ordinance and housing production targets, and support state advocacy.
