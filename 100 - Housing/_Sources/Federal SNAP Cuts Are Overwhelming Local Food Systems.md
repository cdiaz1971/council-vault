# Federal SNAP Cuts Are Overwhelming Local Food Systems

## Summary

More than 4 million Americans have lost SNAP benefits since February 2025 under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act's expanded work requirements. Food pantries are rationing protein, charitable systems are at capacity, and Federal Reserve data shows food insecurity now exceeds pandemic-peak levels -- a direct pressure point for Frederick's Affordability Beyond Housing agenda.

## Key Points

**The scale of the cuts:** The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed by President Trump, ended SNAP work requirement exemptions for older adults, homeless people, veterans, and some rural residents. More than 4 million Americans lost SNAP benefits between February 2025 and February 2026, with further losses expected as states implement new eligibility rules. The average benefit is approximately $188 per month, or $6.17 per day, for recipients typically earning under $1,100/month.

**What pantries are experiencing:** The Ritenour Co-Care Food Pantry outside St. Louis spent $120,000 on food last year; they budgeted $180,000 this year and expect that may be insufficient. They have replaced ground beef and chicken legs with cheaper ground chicken and hot dogs. Seven new families enrolled in a single morning; 15 were expected by end of day. People are traveling to multiple pantries monthly to stock their shelves.

**The data:** The Federal Reserve Bank of New York (May 2026) found food insecurity at levels exceeding the pandemic peak. The Trump administration terminated the annual Household Food Security report last year, making national tracking harder. What data remains consistently points upward.

**State responses:** California is adding $100 million to a program that doubles SNAP purchasing power for fresh produce. Twenty-three state attorneys general wrote to Senate leaders urging SNAP restoration. Nebraska, West Virginia, and others are navigating with patchwork local responses. None of these are adequate substitutes for federal funding.

**The structural point:** Food banks were explicitly designed as emergency supplements to SNAP, not replacements. "I just don't think we can replace the government," the Ritenour director said. The federal withdrawal is creating a structural gap that charitable systems cannot fill, and every decision not to restore cuts is a policy choice to accept rising childhood hunger and its long-term consequences for development and mental health.

## Relevance

The [[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]] note documented the landscape as of June 2026. This piece adds the urgency layer: the federal food safety net is contracting in real time, meaning local food access infrastructure is under increasing pressure not just structurally but immediately. Frederick's community food partners are operating in this same environment.

This connects directly to the Affordability Beyond Housing thread. The food access policy gap was identified as open with no action taken yet. That gap is now actively widening due to federal policy. The [[Affordability Agenda -- Council Authority Tiering by Domain]] note is relevant for what Frederick can actually do at the local level versus what requires state or federal action.

One concrete Frederick angle: Centro Hispano and other community organizations serving immigrant populations are likely seeing direct impact, since refugees and some immigrant households who lost SNAP eligibility are among the most affected groups. The Frederick Rapid Response Network and partner organizations are a live lens on this.
