# National Tenants Bill of Rights (2024)

## Summary
The National Housing Law Project, the National Low Income Housing Coalition, and the Tenant Union Federation wrote this agenda with tenants, organizers, legal experts, and advocates. It is organized around a tenant's experience: applying, signing a lease, and living in the home. It is aimed at federal action and positioned as complementing the Biden administration's Blueprint for a Renters Bill of Rights, but says its principles apply at the state and local levels. It is an advocacy agenda, not law, and its factual claims are the authors'. Clara Jackson flagged it for its rights-based framing. A copy is in Google Drive under City council / 02 - Legislation / Tenant Protection.

**Framing claims in the introduction**
- About 114 million people rent in the U.S. The typical tenant is rent burdened, and rents rose nearly 30% above pre-pandemic levels.
- In most places a landlord can evict for no reason on a few days' notice, and eviction courts give "only the thinnest veil of due process."
- Institutional investors and corporate landlords have exploited weak protections, which also puts small landlords at a disadvantage.
- State and local wins, more than 300 renter protections since 2020, leave a patchwork, so a national floor is needed.
- **Policies are only as good as their enforcement.** Every section pairs rights with enforcement and funding.

## Key Points

**Section 1. The right to a fair application**
- Screening on credit, eviction records, and criminal history is "prognostic," predicts housing outcomes poorly, and carries forward racial and disability bias. The combined effect traps Black and disabled renters in low-opportunity areas.
- **Landlord obligations:** publish screening criteria detailed enough for applicants to estimate their chances; give applicants a written copy of the criteria, notice of tenant rights, and a copy of the lease on request before applying; tell applicants about portable screening reports and accept them with no screening fee; on denial, give specific written reasons and supporting information, a chance to correct or explain, and an appeal.
- **Fair screening:** affirmative marketing to low-income and marginalized renters including disabled renters; a narrow link between each criterion and lease performance; no adverse action on outdated, disputed, incomplete, or irrelevant information; no blanket bans on criminal history, eviction records, or credit score, with individualized assessment instead; enumerated mitigating factors; no automated or algorithmic screening without human review.
- **Eliminate application fees**, because screening benefits only the landlord (Vermont, 9 V.S.A. § 4456a, is the cited example).
- **Screening companies (Fair Credit Reporting Act amendments):** bar reporting of arrests without conviction, older convictions, juvenile records, unrelated acts, and eviction filings without judgment, cases the tenant won, reinstated tenancies, moratorium or emergency-period cases, sealed cases, and default judgments; require validated, tested models; disclose scores, data, and sources; publish inaccuracy rates; written notice within three days of adverse action.
- Federal programs: one streamlined application for all federally assisted housing with publicly posted, agency-approved criteria.

**Section 2. The right to a fair lease**
- Landlords give a written lease, a plain-language rights explainer (including reasonable accommodation), and owner and agent contacts, with notice of ownership changes.
- Leases in plain language, multiple languages, and accessible formats.
- **Mandatory lease disclosures:** rent includes all services and maintenance and defines included utilities; an itemized list of every non-rent fee with its purpose; a clear line between rent (which can support a nonpayment eviction) and non-rent charges (which cannot); mandatory fees limited to necessary services and separate from opt-out fees; a no-retaliation statement; whether the property has a federally backed mortgage; where and how to report habitability problems.
- 60 days' written notice of any change in terms at renewal.
- **Banned terms:** waivers of tenant protections; making fees collectible as "additional rent" or applying payments to fees before rent; confession of judgment; indemnification of the landlord; penalty clauses; class action waivers; mandatory arbitration; one-way terms that bind tenants for months but let landlords terminate on 30 days; one-sided attorney fee clauses.
- Right to split a lease without rescreening in some cases, such as survivors and roommates.

**Section 3. The right to freedom from discrimination and harassment**
- Add to the Fair Housing Act: source of income (vouchers, emergency rental assistance, guaranteed income, cash assistance), eviction history, criminal history, credit history, housing status, rental debt, **immigration status**, veteran status, sexual orientation and gender identity, pregnancy, and survivor status. About 57% of voucher families are already covered by some state or local source-of-income law.
- Discrimination as an affirmative defense in eviction cases.
- Funding for fair housing testing, enforcement, Title VI and Section 109, limited English proficiency protections, outreach, and legal aid training.
- Disability: codify the interactive accommodation process, written accommodation policies attached to leases, cover LIHTC under Section 504, document accessible housing supply, no statute of limitations for design and construction violations, digital accessibility of tenant portals, full accessibility in federally assisted and federally financed homes.
- Public reporting by state and local enforcement agencies on complaints, actions, and resolution time. Right to counsel on all housing matters, not only eviction.
- Federally subsidized providers should not follow crime-free or nuisance ordinances that conflict with federal obligations.
- **Harassment:** private right of action, eviction defense, and attorney fees. Harassment examples include threats, misstating tenant rights, refusing rent, cutting services, failing to repair, interfering with organizing, abusing entry rights, interfering with use of the unit, and **asking about or disclosing immigration or citizenship status**.
- **Entry:** 48 hours' written notice stating a specific reason (agreed repairs, inspection, showing), and written explanation within 48 hours after any emergency entry.
- End unannounced compliance checks and police raids on voucher holders, and bar housing authorities from sharing voucher holders' information with third parties including law enforcement.

**Section 4. The right to a habitable home**
- Warranty of habitability cannot be waived and is not lost because rent is late. Legal counsel to enforce it.
- **Tenant information rights:** the unit's maintenance and repair request history, rights and resource information, 24-hour emergency contacts, and code enforcement contacts.
- Repairs within set time frames by severity, regular status updates, after-hours emergency response, told the expected time frame up front, and **free temporary relocation during habitability repairs** at the tenant's request.
- **Repair and deduct** through a user-friendly process, with court rent escrow as an option.
- Tenant unions may file consolidated building complaints.
- Remedies should avoid harming tenants and can create pipelines to social housing.
- Notice of neighborhood-level environmental risks, with a right to stay during remediation or move with relocation support.
- Habitability as a claim or defense in nonpayment cases, and a **rebuttable presumption of retaliation for evictions within six months of a repair request**.
- **Rental registries** with owner identity including beneficial ownership, LLC formation documents and officers, property manager license, complaints, code history, eviction history and rates, affordability covenants and expiration dates, federal financing, and whether rental assistance is accepted. Cited working examples: Minneapolis, Concord (CA), Portland, Syracuse, Buffalo.
- Federal housing: tenant participation in inspections and remediation, temporary relocation nearby, right to return without rescreening, no net loss of affordable units, and full Uniform Relocation Act benefits.

**Section 5. The right to reasonable rent and costs**
- Context: 21.6 million cost-burdened renter households in 2021, 11.6 million severely; 57% of Black and 53% of Hispanic renters cost burdened against 45% of White renters; median rent up nearly 18% from 2001 to 2021 against 3% income growth; a full-time minimum wage worker cannot afford a two-bedroom at fair market rent anywhere; 59% of extremely low-income LIHTC tenants without rental assistance are severely burdened.
- Uncapped increases and surprise fees let landlords evict for nonpayment and get around other protections, especially where tenants must be current on rent to use their rights.
- **Rent regulation** to ban unreasonable increases; no rent increases while a property fails habitability standards; **prohibit state preemption of local rent stabilization**.
- **Fees:** at least one free payment method; receipts; payments applied to rent first; access to the rent ledger; rent covers all services, maintenance, and utilities that cannot be individually metered, with no mandatory fees for basics like trash; additional fees only for optional, refusable services actually provided and at cost.
- Security deposits capped at one month, with notice of where the deposit is held and interest, a move-out walkthrough with a written checklist, no deductions for normal wear, turnkey items, or permitted early terminations, and refundable deposit-insurance fees.
- Late fees reasonable and proportionate to rent.
- Submetered utilities billed with meter readings, rates, and an appeal process, with no common-area charges. Uninterrupted utilities if the landlord fails to pay the utility.
- Renter's insurance not required, and the tenant chooses the provider if purchased.
- Federal programs: expand savings programs, discount LIHTC rents and require owners to accept available subsidy, no eviction of over-income tenants who cannot move, realistic utility allowances, and bar owners with high eviction rates or unreasonable increases from federal programs.

**Section 6. The right to organize**
- Most states do not protect tenants from retaliation for organizing others, and LIHTC does not protect organizing.
- **Rights:** form tenant organizations, convene, distribute literature, post information, and give outside organizers building access, including common areas and tenants' own units.
- **Rebuttable presumption** that adverse action against a tenant organization member is retaliation. Fourteen listed forms of retaliation, including eviction over minor violations, rent increases, withheld repairs, privacy violations, harassment, destroying organizing materials, false statements to police or mental health institutions, and **inappropriate police calls**.
- Remedies: civil penalties, injunctions, damages to tenants and the organization, suspension or revocation of the owner's business license or registration, attorney fees, and landlord liability for agents including security guards.
- Mandatory periodic bargaining between landlords and tenant organizations, with rent reduction as a penalty for noncompliance (San Francisco's Union-at-Home ordinance requires landlords to attend a tenant association meeting each quarter).
- **Tenant Opportunity to Purchase** for multifamily buildings, with an entity that funds and supports tenant acquisitions. D.C.'s TOPA produced most of its 4,400 limited-equity co-op homes in 99 buildings.
- An Office of the Tenant Advocate at HUD.

**Section 7. The right to safeguards against eviction**
- 30% of tenants move at the first sign of eviction to avoid a record.
- **Just cause** for evictions, non-renewals, and no-fault terminations. At-fault causes: nonpayment of at least one month's fair market rent (after facilitating emergency rental assistance and diversion), uncured substantial lease violation, or nuisance. No-fault causes: owner or buyer move-in, renovation the tenant cannot stay through, demolition or substantial rehab, leaving the rental market, or approved condo or co-op conversion.
- No-fault terminations need 120 days' notice and relocation assistance of two months' fair market rent or two months' rent waived. Presumption of bad faith if the stated reason is not carried out within 90 days.
- Notice and right to cure: at least 30 days' notice of a lease violation, in plain and accessible language, describing the violation, the cure, and tenant rights, with confirmed delivery. In nonpayment cases, paying back rent preserves the tenancy.
- **Early termination grounds:** becoming eligible for subsidized housing, survivors of gender-based violence and other crime victims, job loss, new employment requiring a move, poor or unsafe conditions including water quality, disability accommodation including treatment or assisted living, divorce or separation, and school closure for students.
- LIHTC: Treasury guidance defining good cause, one year's notice before affordability restrictions expire, and closing the qualified contract loophole.
- **Eviction court:** good-faith pre-filing mediation or diversion with the case paused; filings under seal; landlord filing fees high enough to deter serial filing and not passed to tenants; a hearing without preconditions like paying rent into court; full no-cost counsel with continuance if counsel is unavailable; damages limited to actual losses; applying for emergency rental assistance before filing; time for discovery; legally trained judges; jury trial; appeal without a prohibitive bond; reinstatement by paying the judgment; emergency stays; **automatic sealing and expungement after three years**; standards to prevent violence during physical evictions.
- **Self-help evictions:** banned in all circumstances, with a pro se friendly court process to regain entry, a published police policy on illegal lockouts with data collection, and outreach to police, landlords, and tenants. Philadelphia's police directive is the cited model.

## Relevance
- **Framing.** The seven-rights structure, which follows a tenant from application to eviction, is a ready outline for Frederick's renter landing page, handbook, and the findings section of a second tenant ordinance.
- **Checklist against Frederick's work.** Items Frederick is already pursuing or that Maryland already provides: all-in fee disclosure and opt-in fees ([[Real Rent Ordinance]]), one-month deposit cap and interest, criminal screening conditional offer, self-help eviction ban, right to counsel in evictions, anti-retaliation for organizing. Items not yet in Frederick's plans: published screening criteria and written denial reasons with appeal, a ban on applying payments to fees before rent, maintenance history disclosure, a rental registry with beneficial ownership, tenant organizing access rights for outside organizers, a presumption of retaliation for organizers, and a notice fee ban.
- **Immigrant renters.** The agenda treats asking about or disclosing immigration status as harassment and proposes immigration status as a protected class. Maryland already criminalizes using immigration threats to extract money or labor. A Frederick ordinance could name immigration-status inquiries as harassment, consistent with the [[City Values Act]].
- **Rental registry overlap.** Its registry contents line up with Rockville's Article V reporting and with Frederick's rental licensing data. Beneficial ownership disclosure for LLC owners connects to [[Corporate Landlords and Private Equity]].
- **Rent "versus" fees in eviction.** Its insistence that non-rent charges can never drive a nonpayment eviction matches Maryland's base-rent-only rule for failure-to-pay-rent cases.
- **Limits.** Many recommendations are federal (FCRA, Fair Housing Act, HUD, LIHTC) or court procedure (sealing, jury trials, filing fees) that Frederick cannot set. Its statistics are the authors' and should be checked against primary sources before reuse.
