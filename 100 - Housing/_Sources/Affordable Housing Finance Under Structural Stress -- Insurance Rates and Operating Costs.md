# Affordable Housing Finance Under Structural Stress -- Insurance Rates and Operating Costs

## Summary

Affordable housing developers and operators across the US are experiencing a structural squeeze: the costs of running affordable housing -- insurance, debt service, utilities, maintenance -- are rising faster than rents (which are capped by affordability covenants) and faster than public subsidy increases. The result is a growing gap between what it costs to operate a building and what revenue it can generate within affordability constraints.

## Key Points

The stress is multi-fronted. Insurance premiums for affordable multifamily properties have increased sharply, driven by climate-related claims, reinsurance market tightening, and insurer exits from certain markets. Interest rate increases since 2022 have raised refinancing and development costs. Operating expenses including labor, utilities, and maintenance have followed broader inflation trends. None of these inputs are capped -- but the income side is.

Affordable housing developers interviewed for the Shelterforce analysis describe margin compression that forces difficult tradeoffs: deferred maintenance, reduced services, difficulty refinancing, and in some cases decisions about whether to allow affordability covenants to expire rather than absorb the cost of renewal.

The system is described as "resilient overall" but under growing strain, with the mismatch between costs and revenue forcing hard choices about which properties can be preserved at affordability over the long term.

## Relevance

This is the macroeconomic context for Frederick's MPDU reform and housing production work. The argument for a higher payment-in-lieu rate is partly that the affordable housing finance stack needs more subsidy at every layer -- the City Housing Fund is one source, and it is currently undercapitalized at the $2/sqft rate. If operating costs are rising faster than rents even for subsidized properties, the gap between what the market produces and what low-income households need is widening, not narrowing. This dynamic also argues for preservation as a policy priority alongside production: losing existing affordable units to cost-driven covenant expiration sets back production targets even when new units are being built.
