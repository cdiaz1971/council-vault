# Spain Eviction Protests and the Failed Housing Bill

## Summary
The eviction of an 87-year-old Madrid tenant after her institutional landlord raised rent from 500 to 2,650 euros triggered national protests and Spain's most ambitious housing bill, which then failed in Parliament. Michael Byrne reads it as more evidence that the private rental sector is now a central political fault line.

## Key Points
Maricarmen had lived in the same Madrid apartment for more than 70 years. When her institutional landlord raised the rent more than fivefold, the Madrid Tenants Union organized around her case. Police carried her out on a stretcher after the union failed to block the eviction, and the image set off demonstrations in Madrid and other Spanish cities, including a standing protest camp in Puerta del Sol. The union is demanding a 50 percent rent reduction and expropriation of units held by institutional landlords.

The national government responded within days with a housing bill that would have banned speculative investors from buying housing, barred evictions of vulnerable people without a housing alternative, made rental contracts indefinite, frozen rents through 2028, created tax deductions for social housing, and set up a housing investment fund. It failed on Friday because the government could not secure the centre-right Catalan party Junts. There is now talk of an early election framed as a referendum on housing. The landlord has since agreed to take Maricarmen back at an affordable rent.

Byrne ties this to Berlin, where Die Linke came out as the big winner of last week's elections largely on its pledge to implement the 2021 referendum to expropriate institutional landlords. His broader argument is that housing policy has entered a "post-neoliberal" phase. Governments are returning to direct social housing construction (Germany and Spain are both setting up national public construction agencies) and are expanding rent regulation and security of tenure across Ireland, Scotland, the Netherlands, France, Spain, and several US states. That all sits inside a financial architecture that still depends on institutional investment to fund construction and on local governments hollowed out by austerity. His read is that the public no longer finds market-only supply arguments credible after three decades of them failing to deliver affordability.

## Relevance
This is the clearest current example of a single displacement case by an institutional landlord turning into a national political crisis. It strengthens the political case behind [[Corporate Landlords and Private Equity]] and gives a live international reference point for the tenant stability arguments in [[Tenant Protections]]. Byrne's post-neoliberal framing lines up with the typology in [[Abundance Model vs Vienna Model -- Housing Politics Typology]], since it describes governments pursuing public construction and rent regulation at the same time rather than choosing between supply and protection. The failure of the bill on coalition math is also a reminder for [[Housing Politics and Advocacy]] that public outrage moves agendas quickly but does not by itself produce votes.
