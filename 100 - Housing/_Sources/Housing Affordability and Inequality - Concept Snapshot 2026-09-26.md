# Housing Affordability and Inequality

## Frederick

- **ALICE (2024 data).** United For ALICE's 2026 Maryland data (2024 figures) finds **42.7% of City of Frederick households** (14,343 of 33,584) below the ALICE threshold, the income needed to cover housing, child care, food, transportation, health care, and technology. That's 9.5% in poverty and 33.2% ALICE.
  - **County:** 31.8% of Frederick County households (34,465 of 108,330) are below the threshold. The County median household income is $122,059.
  - **Thresholds:** the County's 2024 ALICE threshold is $88,330 for households headed by someone under 65 and $73,428 for those 65 and older.
  - **By ZIP:** 21702 (west Frederick and the Golden Mile) and 21703 (southwest) are at 40%, 21701 at 37%, and 21704 at 20%.
  - **Why the City is higher:** larger shares of renters, immigrants, single-parent households, and lower-wage workers.

  ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]])
- **From the prior report (2023 data).** The 2025 report put the City at 45%, the County at 33%, and Maryland at 39%.
  - County thresholds were about $49,380 for a single adult and $111,588 for a family of four, as reported by WYPR.
  - ALICE households in the County rose by 2,017 from 2022 to 2023.
  - In the County, 76% of single mothers and 85% of adults under 25 were below the threshold, and Black veterans were at twice the rate of white veterans (35% vs. 17%).

  ([[ALICE Report -- Frederick County 2025 (2023 Data) -- Reference Note]])
- **The ALICE gap.** At Maryland averages, the 2024 survival budget for a single adult is $50,868 a year ($25.43 an hour) ([[Food Access Landscape -- Frederick and Peer City Models]]). The 2023 figures were $39,552 for a single adult and $98,136 for a family of four, against federal poverty levels of $14,580 and $30,000. Most program eligibility is keyed to the federal poverty level, so households in between are ineligible for aid and still can't meet basic costs.
  - In 2023, 73% of Maryland renter households below the threshold were rent-burdened, up from 68% in 2021.
  - A retail worker at the 2023 median wage ($16.06 an hour) was $21,730 short of a one-adult, one-child budget. That's slightly further behind than in 2010, despite a 62% nominal raise.
  - Only 32% of households below the threshold in the South Atlantic region had three months of savings, which is why one car repair or medical bill can cascade into housing instability.

  ([[ALICE Report -- Frederick County 2025 (2023 Data) -- Reference Note]])
- **What the City can do, by cost domain.** César's authority map sorts the ALICE domains into three tiers: direct City action, City as partner or facilitator, and advocacy or mitigation only ([[Affordability Agenda -- Council Authority Tiering by Domain]]).
  - **Housing (direct).**
    - Direct tools: rental fee rules (the Real Rent Ordinance), the MPDU rate and payment-in-lieu rate, zoning for missing middle in R4/R6, and land donation to affordable housing (the Lucas Village precedent).
    - Partner tools: HACF on LIHTC redevelopment, a community land trust, and a City land inventory.
    - Advocacy only: voucher funding.
  - **Child care (partner, with some direct tools).**
    - Direct tools: zoning for provider siting and home-based providers, and permit-fee waivers for new facilities.
    - Partner or advocacy: the County administers the state subsidy, and the State sets subsidy rates.
  - **Food (partner).**
    - Direct tools: commercial zoning for grocery stores, markets, and co-ops; urban agriculture rules; and City land for gardens and pantries.
    - Partner tools: nonprofit funding.
    - Advocacy only: SNAP and WIC, which are federal.
  - **Transportation (partner, with direct land-use tools).**
    - Direct tools: parking policy, density near transit, and street design.
    - Partner tools: TransIT is a County program, and the City's role is advocacy on routes and frequency.
  - **Health care (advocacy).**
    - The cost drivers are state and federal.
    - Local tools address access. 47% of United Way Ride United trips since July 2024 were for health appointments, so transportation to care is a local lever, along with language access and community health workers.
  - **Technology (direct and partner).** Cable and internet franchise agreements, right-of-way policy for fiber, public Wi-Fi, and digital-equity partnerships.
  - **Financial stability (cross-cutting).** The Municipal ID (for access to banking), income-sensitive City fees, living wage in City contracts, VITA tax preparation, and benefits navigation.
- **Related Frederick work:** supply-side tools are on [[Zoning Reform]] and [[Parking Reform]], renter protections on [[Tenant Protections]], and planning-stage items, including a proposed allocation principle, in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].

## Measuring affordability

- **The 30% rule.** The benchmark that housing should cost no more than 30% of income is calculated on gross, not take-home, pay. It also ignores debt, child care, and health costs that come out of a paycheck before rent. Realtor.com reports that 58% of young adults who moved out of their family home have moved back ([[The 30 Percent Housing Cost Rule Is Broken]]).
- **Survival budgets instead.** ALICE builds a local Household Survival Budget from:
  - 110% of HUD Fair Market Rent
  - state child-care rates
  - the USDA Thrifty Food Plan
  - AAA vehicle costs
  - broadband plus a phone plan

  It's a cost-of-living measure rather than a share-of-income rule ([[ALICE Report -- Frederick County 2025 (2023 Data) -- Reference Note]]).

## Explanations beyond supply

The supply explanation, that restrictive zoning drives prices, is covered on [[Zoning Reform]]. These sources argue it's incomplete.

- **Wage inequality and the knowledge economy.** Michael Byrne's four-part series in *The Week in Housing*:
  - **Part I.** Knowledge industries cluster in dense cores, polarizing wages. Non-college workers may now face an urban wage *penalty* once housing costs are counted ([[Knowledge Economy Double Divide -- Housing Unaffordability and the Urban Wage Penalty]]).
  - **Part II.** The same shift widens the gap between thriving hubs and "left behind" places, and voting patterns increasingly follow that divide ([[The Great Divergence and Left Behind Places -- Knowledge Economy and Housing]]).
  - **Part III** draws on Buchholz et al. (2026).
    - Housing prices track a city's *average* income, which masks unequal income growth.
    - High earners bid up well-located housing and reshape local amenities to their tastes, raising costs for everyone else.
    - Since 1950 the most expensive cities went from 2× to 4× the national average home price.
    - Six of seven "superstar" cities have negative internal migration alongside positive international immigration.

    ([[Wages vs Housing Policy -- Knowledge Economy and Superstar City Inequality (Byrne Part III)]])
  - **Part IV, Dublin.**
    - More than 75% of Irish ICT jobs created from 2003 to 2018 were in Dublin.
    - Domestic-sector wages fell in most years from 2008 to 2017.
    - Prices in the city's most expensive area are nearly seven times those in the cheapest.

    ([[Divided Dublin -- Knowledge Economy Concentration and Housing Inequality]])
- **Frederick's position in that pattern.** César's syntheses argue:
  - Frederick sits at the edge of the DC–Baltimore knowledge-economy corridor and acts as its affordability relief valve. Lower-income workers and longtime residents are priced out as higher-earning commuters and remote workers arrive ([[Inequality Is the Housing Crisis -- Synthesis]]).
  - Because the driver is structural, the responses are deed-restricted and MPDU production, employer-assisted housing, and cross-agency coordination.
  - The model for coordination is North Carolina's Executive Order 36, which created a housing coordinator linking transportation, environmental review, permitting, and workforce decisions to housing ([[NC Governor Executive Order 36 -- Cross-Agency Housing Strategy]]). A Maryland equivalent would link MDOT investments, MDE review, and Commerce Opportunity Zone nominations to housing goals ([[Knowledge Economy and Housing -- Synthesis]]).
- **Mortgage credit.** Kevin Erdmann attributes rising rents in both expensive and cheap cities to post-2008 credit tightening. From 2008 to 2019, lending to borrowers with scores above 760 nearly doubled while lending at 720–760 fell 58%. That pushed working households into renting. Michael Lewyn responds that Canada and Australia had no comparable credit freeze but saw similar rent increases ([[Erdmann's Contagion Cities and the 2008 Credit Crackdown]]).
- **Inherited housing wealth.** Harvard's 2026 State of the Nation's Housing report:
  - The median existing single-family home now sells for nearly five times median household income, against about three times in the 1990s.
  - Rental units under $1,000 a month fell by 7 million over a decade.
  - Listings affordable at $75,000 or less fell from 49% of inventory (March 2019) to 23% (March 2026).
  - Family wealth transfers increasingly decide who becomes an owner. Children of equity-extracting homeowners hold about a third more housing wealth by 30 than children of renters.

  ([[Homeownership as Inherited Advantage Harvard JCHS 2026]]) Fortune's reading is that the postwar homeownership boom depended on conditions that have since dissolved:
  - union density
  - wages outpacing prices
  - broad (and racially exclusionary) FHA/VA subsidy
  - prices near three times income

  ([[The Postwar Homeownership Window Has Closed]])
- **Power, concentration, and debt.** Demos president Taifa Smith Butler frames affordability as a question of who holds economic and political power, citing corporate concentration in housing, child care, utilities, and groceries ([[Affordability as Power -- Demos on Structural Drivers of Economic Insecurity]]). The June 2026 Demos/People's Action report argues the crisis is the product of policy choices and proposes five pillars:
  - public investment
  - wages and worker power
  - corporate accountability
  - racially just taxation
  - democracy as economic policy

  ([[Solving the Affordability Crisis -- Demos and Peoples Action 2026 Report]]) Hannah Appel of the Debt Collective treats debt (mortgage, student, medical, utility) as a mechanism of extraction and distinguishes "free" from "freeing" ([[How Debtors Build Power -- Hannah Appel on Racial Capitalism and Housing Justice]]).
- **From the power framing to a policy stack.** César's synthesis reads these sources as describing three registers of one problem:
  - **market power:** new supply is absorbed into the same concentrated ownership
  - **debt as extraction**
  - **who writes the rules:** inclusionary requirements get weakened and rent rules preempted

  It derives a four-part local stack:
  1. **production targets**, necessary but not sufficient
  2. **ownership structures** that take a share of housing out of the speculative market (community land trusts, deed restrictions, community benefit agreements)
  3. **anti-extraction provisions** in City contracts and development deals
  4. **an organizing frame** that treats ALICE households as a constituency with shared interests rather than as individual consumers

  ([[Affordability Is a Power Problem Not a Price Problem -- Synthesis]])

## Racial disparities

- **Housing.** Demos and People's Action report that half of all renters, and 56% of Black renters, are priced out of the market ([[Solving the Affordability Crisis -- Demos and Peoples Action 2026 Report]]).
- **ALICE by race.** In Maryland, 49% of Black and 47% of Hispanic households are below the ALICE threshold, against 33% of white non-Hispanic households ([[ALICE Report -- Frederick County 2025 (2023 Data) -- Reference Note]]).
- **Labor market.** Black teens looking for work face 23.9% unemployment, nearly twice the rate for white teens and a level white teens reach only in recessions ([[Black Youth Unemployment as Structural Inequality -- Demos on the 23.9% Rate and Persistent Racial Disparity]]).
- **Commutes.** In superstar cities, Black workers face lower earnings and longer commutes together ([[Wages vs Housing Policy -- Knowledge Economy and Superstar City Inequality (Byrne Part III)]]).

## Framing and allocation

- **"Affordability" as a frame.** A *Working Today for Tomorrow* essay argues that "affordability" describes an inconvenience when low-wage households are experiencing a catastrophe. It calls the cause coordinated upward redistribution ([[Class War Framing -- Why Affordability Talk Undersells the Structural Crisis]]).
- **Beyond abundance and Vienna.** César's synthesis argues that both dominant models treat housing mainly as a question of unit counts:
  - The supply-side "abundance" model treats all supply as equivalent regardless of who owns it.
  - The social-housing "Vienna" model routes the answer through the state.

  A growing cohort of voters is pro-homeownership, against institutional investors, and skeptical of both models. The synthesis names what the two frames leave out in Frederick: LIHTC affordability that expires, institutional purchases of single-family homes, and no community land trust to hold affordability permanently. It proposes leading with who owns housing and captures its appreciation, with community land trusts as the instrument that answers the cohort's question ([[What We Mean When We Say Affordable -- Class War Framing and the Housing Model Choice]]).
- **Collective-benefit framing.** Joseph Rowntree Foundation research identifies "scarcity" and "fatalism" as the public mindsets that make housing and immigration debates toxic. It recommends:
  - leading with collective benefit
  - tying housing to shared infrastructure
  - showing that policy works
  - focusing on system design rather than blame

  It also concedes that framing can't substitute for a defensible account of who gets housed ([[Housing and Immigration Framing]]).
- **Allocation.** César's September 2026 synthesis argues that messaging can build support for producing housing but not for distributing it, because every subsidized unit requires an allocation choice. In several European countries, the far right is turning allocation into a question of national belonging. His response:
  - Adopt a stated need-based principle that holds up under a consistency test, applying equally to longtime residents, newcomers, and immigrant families.
  - Reconcile it with collective-benefit framing: building more benefits everyone, and distributing scarce units by need is fair because anyone can fall into need.
  - Candidate criteria: need and cost burden, displacement risk, local employment or ties, and household composition.
  - Length-of-residency preferences in particular would fall hardest on immigrant households.

  ([[The Allocation Question Cannot Be Messaged Away]]; drawing also on [[Populist Planning and Cultural Housing Aspirations]] and [[Social Housing Allocation as a Nativist Flashpoint]])
