# Single Stair Reform Policy Brief

## Summary

Single stair reform allows midrise apartment buildings to be built with one interior stairway instead of two, reducing construction costs 6-13%, unlocking small-lot infill, and enabling family-sized units. Maryland is in a study phase following HB 489/SB 436 (2025); Frederick has no local provision and will need to act when state enabling legislation passes.

## What It Is

Single stair reform refers to changes in building code that would allow midrise apartment buildings to be constructed with one interior stairway rather than the two currently required by the International Building Code (IBC) for buildings above three stories. The double-egress rule has been standard in most of the United States since the early 20th century. The double-loaded corridor layout it produces constrains apartments to windows on only one side, making natural ventilation impossible and producing increasingly thin, dark units suited primarily to studios and one-bedrooms rather than families.

## The Housing Case

Single-stair designs can lower apartment costs by 6 to 13 percent compared with standard construction, and eliminating the second stairway frees space that can cost up to $300,000 on a small building. That savings can be redirected to an elevator for ADA access or absorbed as reduced per-unit cost. More consequentially, the building type opens narrow infill lots that are currently undevelopable under the double-stair requirement. Single-stair buildings dedicate up to 95 percent of floor space to housing, compared to 70 to 87 percent in double-loaded corridor buildings, and can be built on smaller lots while producing larger, more flexible floor plans better suited to families and seniors.

## Fire Safety

The principal objection is fire safety, and it is addressable. A February 2025 study by the Center for Building in North America and the Pew Charitable Trusts found that single-staircase buildings equipped with sprinklers do not present higher fire safety risks than two-staircase construction. Between 2012 and 2024, the rate of fire deaths in modern single-stairway apartment buildings with four to six stories in New York City was similar to other residential buildings. Seattle has permitted single-stair buildings up to six stories since 1977 without stair-related fatalities. Standard safeguards in adopted ordinances include full sprinklers, pressurized stairwells, fire-rated construction, maximum of four units per floor, and a 20-foot maximum corridor travel distance to the stair.

## National and State Momentum

Since 2022, 19 states and Washington, D.C. have introduced bills to study or allow single-stairway buildings. In 2025 alone, seven states passed and signed bipartisan legislation: Colorado, Montana, New Hampshire, and Texas now allow them, with Montana's law the broadest, permitting buildings up to six stories with the fewest additional restrictions. At the federal level, the Housing for the 21st Century Act, which passed the House 390 to 9 in February 2026, directs HUD to establish guidelines for single-stair buildings up to five stories and authorizes competitive grants for pilot projects.

## Maryland Status

Maryland is in the study phase. HB 489/SB 436 (2025) directed the Maryland Department of Labor to perform a study and make recommendations for legislation authorizing single-stair buildings up to six stories. The study is underway. Baltimore City Council Bill 25-0062, introduced in May 2025 by Councilman Ryan Dorsey, would amend the Baltimore Building Code directly to allow single-exit residential buildings under controlled conditions. Frederick has no parallel provision in the LMC and operates under state building code minimums.

## Relevance for Frederick

Frederick cannot unilaterally amend the state building code. The actionable path depends on the Maryland Department of Labor study outcome and any 2026 legislative session follow-up. A resolution supporting the reform, timed to the Maryland study release, would create public record and put Frederick in the peer company of Baltimore and other jurisdictions already on record. When and if Maryland enacts enabling legislation, local adoption will require Council action.

The housing supply argument connects directly to Frederick's documented 8,133-unit shortage and to the 15 percent affordability target in the housing production resolution under development. Single stair reform is a supply-side tool that addresses construction cost and small-lot feasibility simultaneously -- both constraints relevant to District 2's infill corridors.

## Watch Items

- Maryland Department of Labor study release is the critical near-term signal. If it recommends enabling legislation and the General Assembly acts in the 2026 session, Frederick will need to be ready to adopt.
- Fire department opposition is the predictable local friction point and should be anticipated in any public engagement.
- No preemption issue arises from a resolution. A future ordinance implementing a state-authorized code change would require legal review of the adoption mechanism under state building code law.
