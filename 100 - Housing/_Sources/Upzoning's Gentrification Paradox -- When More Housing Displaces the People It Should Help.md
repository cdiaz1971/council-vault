# Upzoning's Gentrification Paradox -- When More Housing Displaces the People It Should Help

## Summary

The core tension in supply-side housing reform is that the same zoning changes intended to increase affordability can accelerate gentrification when market conditions favor luxury development. Without mechanisms to shape what gets built, upzoning may produce more units for wealthier residents while displacing the lower-income households it was meant to help.

## Key Points

Denver's experience illustrates the paradox directly. In historically lower-income, majority-Latino neighborhoods on the city's west side, upzoning and density liberalization produced a wave of teardown-and-replace development -- but what replaced the older stock was not affordable housing. It was large, expensive homes targeting buyers with significantly higher incomes than existing residents. The neighborhood added units and population, but the population changed.

Urban Displacement Project data on Denver shows more than 50,000 low-income households living in areas at risk of gentrification, concentrated in the southwest and central city -- exactly where upzoning pressure is highest. The city's response -- pausing rezoning in affected neighborhoods until affordability tools can be layered in -- reflects a recognition that supply alone is not sufficient.

The political dynamic compounds the policy challenge. YIMBY coalitions and displacement-focused tenant advocates often disagree sharply on whether upzoning helps or hurts low-income communities. This disagreement is not purely ideological: both sides point to empirical evidence, because the outcomes are genuinely context-dependent. Upzoning in a hot market with low affordability requirements produces different outcomes than upzoning with strong inclusionary requirements and anti-displacement tools.

## Relevance

Frederick's District 2 -- majority-minority, with a poverty rate 1.5x the county rate and a foreign-born population 1.4x the county rate -- is precisely the kind of neighborhood where undifferentiated upzoning carries displacement risk. The [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] and LMC reform should both be paired with explicit anti-displacement analysis: who currently lives in the areas being upzoned, what is the likely development pressure, and what affordability mechanisms are in place? The [[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]] and MPDU reform thread are the right tools to layer alongside any zoning liberalization.
