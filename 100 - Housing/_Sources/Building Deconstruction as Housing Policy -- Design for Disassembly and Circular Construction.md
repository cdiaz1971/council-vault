## Summary

The US generates over 600 million tons of construction and demolition waste annually, far exceeding municipal solid waste. Conventional demolition prioritizes speed and cheap disposal, which mixes and contaminates materials that could otherwise be reused, sending valuable lumber, brick, windows, and structural components to landfills.

Deconstruction -- "construction in reverse" -- carefully dismantles buildings piece by piece to maximize material recovery. Each salvaged component represents avoided extraction, manufacturing, and transport of replacement materials, preserving embodied carbon already invested in production.

## Key Points

**Why conventional demolition wastes so much**: Heavy machinery crushes materials together, making separation for recycling or reuse expensive and economically unattractive. Once mixed, concrete, wood, and metals lose most of their reuse value.

**Benefits of deconstruction**: Environmental (landfill diversion, embodied carbon preservation, reduced demand for virgin materials), economic (more local skilled labor than heavy equipment demolition, material resale markets, circular economy growth), and community (reduced dust, noise, truck traffic from demolition).

**Design for Deconstruction (DfD)**: Emerging design philosophy that treats buildings as temporary assemblies of valuable materials rather than permanent disposable structures. Prioritizes standardized components, mechanical fasteners, accessible connections, and modular systems to enable future disassembly.

**What makes buildings hard to deconstruct**: Cast-in-place reinforced concrete (concrete and rebar permanently bonded), nail-heavy wood framing with adhesives and composites, engineered wood products with layers that cannot be separated. Precast concrete, bolted steel, and modular systems are far more recoverable.

**Existing policy models**:
- Boulder, CO (2020): Deconstruction ordinance requiring 75% diversion by weight, documentation requirements. Now a national model.
- Portland, Palo Alto, Pittsburgh, San Antonio: Similar requirements.
- Chapel Hill / Orange County, NC: Regulated Recyclable Material Ordinance -- waste management plan required for every demolition, recyclability assessment required for structures over 500 sq ft, increased landfill tipping fees for mixed debris.

**Policy levers available to municipalities**: Demolition permit requirements (waste management plan, recyclability assessment), minimum diversion targets by weight, increased tipping fees for mixed debris, design standards for new construction.

## Relevance to Frederick

As the LMC Reform Commission moves forward, there is an opening to incorporate deconstruction requirements or waste diversion standards into demolition permit processes. Frederick does not currently have material recovery requirements for demolition. A modest ordinance modeled on Chapel Hill's approach -- waste management plan required, assessment for larger structures, tipping fee incentives -- would not require a full deconstruction mandate to start shifting practice.

The Design for Deconstruction framing also connects to future development review: new construction permitted under a reformed LMC could include standards that favor reversible assemblies over permanently bonded systems, particularly for commercial development.

Libby Taylor's proposed building moratorium, if enacted, would pause new construction but not address the waste and embodied carbon implications of demolition activity on existing structures -- a gap this policy approach could fill.
