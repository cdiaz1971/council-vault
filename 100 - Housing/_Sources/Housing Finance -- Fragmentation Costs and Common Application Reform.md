# Housing Finance Fragmentation -- Cost of Coordination and Reform Strategies

## Summary

The Terner Center quantifies the cost of fragmented affordable housing funding: each additional funding source adds an average of 4 months to construction timelines and $20,460 per unit in development costs -- a finding driving interest in common applications and consolidated intake systems at the state and local level.

## Key Points

Affordable housing development almost always draws from multiple funding sources simultaneously -- federal, state, local, bank, foundation, and nonprofit -- each with different timelines, requirements, and formats. This fragmentation creates compounding inefficiencies. The UC Berkeley Terner Center's February 2026 "Cost of Fragmentation" report puts a number on it: on average, each additional funding source adds four months to the construction start timeline and $20,460 per unit in total development costs.

Colorado's experience tracks closely. Between 2021 and 2025, average per-unit development costs rose from roughly $325,000 to just under $500,000, while the number of completed developments fell. Legal fees alone associated with project closings have reportedly tripled over the past five to ten years, attributed largely to coordination complexity. Colorado is now piloting a response: Housing Hub Colorado, a one-stop information site launched in November 2025, with a statewide common application for developers in development -- expected by end of 2025 -- that would allow a single data entry to reach multiple funding agencies simultaneously.

The demand side has parallel inefficiencies. Many municipalities lack a unified listing or application system for renters seeking subsidized housing, forcing residents to navigate multiple siloed providers. New York City didn't have a coherent database of available affordable units until high school students built one in 2024. Jackson Hole, Wyoming is working with the Urban Institute to design a universal intake system, though the form it will take -- common application, universal listing, or something else -- is still being determined.

The throughline: administrative consolidation doesn't require new money, but it can unlock meaningful capacity within existing budgets.

## Relevance

Frederick's Housing Production Target Resolution and any future housing trust fund work will be shaped by the same fragmentation dynamics described here. If Frederick ever layers local funds on top of state or federal sources -- CDBG, LIHTC, HOME, state bonds -- the coordination costs will be real. The Terner Center's $20K-per-unit figure is a useful reference for making the case that streamlining is worth the political investment. The Colorado common application model is also worth watching as a possible template. See [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] and [[Maryland Housing Certainty Act]].
