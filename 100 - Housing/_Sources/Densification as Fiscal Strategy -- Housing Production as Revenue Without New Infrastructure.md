# Densification as Fiscal Strategy -- Housing Production as Revenue Without New Infrastructure

## Summary

Adding housing units to existing neighborhoods generates tax revenue without adding infrastructure the city must maintain -- reframing housing production as a fiscal resilience move rather than an equity or affordability move, and potentially reaching skeptics who don't respond to the affordability frame.

## The Idea

The standard case for housing production targets leads with affordability and equity: more units means downward pressure on rents, more options for low- and moderate-income residents, reduced displacement pressure. That case is correct, but it has a known ceiling. Skeptics hear it as a redistribution argument and tune out.

The Strong Towns framing, surfaced in [[Make Caring Cool Again -- The Strong Towns Case for Maintenance Over Novelty]], offers a different entry point. When you add units to an existing street -- whether through ADUs, missing middle infill, or upzoning -- you add to the tax rolls without adding to the infrastructure the city is on the hook to maintain. The street is already there. The water and sewer lines are already there. The sidewalk, the streetlights, the fire coverage area -- already paid for. The new units ride on existing capacity and generate new revenue against a fixed maintenance obligation.

This is the opposite of greenfield suburban expansion, where every new development adds lane-miles, utility extensions, and stormwater infrastructure that the city must maintain in perpetuity -- usually at a cost that exceeds what the development generates in taxes over its lifecycle.

Incremental densification is therefore a fiscal efficiency argument, not just a housing argument. More units per acre means more revenue per lane-mile. It's the same logic that makes a mixed-use main street financially stronger than a strip mall: the productive surface area is higher relative to the infrastructure serving it.

## Why the Framing Matters for Frederick

The Housing Production Target Resolution is currently encountering skepticism from colleagues who are either unconvinced by the affordability frame (Atkins) or prefer process language over specific targets (Brehm). Neither objection is primarily about fiscal health -- but fiscal health is a frame both might respond to differently.

The argument would run something like: Frederick's long-term financial resilience depends on growing its tax base faster than its infrastructure obligations grow. Greenfield expansion does the opposite. Incremental densification in existing neighborhoods -- the kind the resolution supports -- does the former. Setting production targets is therefore not just a housing policy choice; it's a balance sheet choice.

This framing also works in budget season conversations, where capital projects compete with operating costs and housing production often loses to more visible infrastructure. Framing density as the thing that makes all the other infrastructure more financially sustainable reorders the priorities.

## Open Questions

- Has the city ever modeled revenue per acre or revenue per lane-mile for different development types in Frederick? That data would make this argument concrete rather than theoretical.
- Is there a Strong Towns or Urban3 analysis of Frederick or comparable Maryland cities that could be cited directly?
- How does this interact with the MPDU affordability targets -- does the fiscal argument hold when a percentage of units are price-restricted?
