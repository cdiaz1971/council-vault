# Massachusetts Rent Control Ballot Measure -- 5% Cap With New Construction Exemption

## Summary

Massachusetts housing advocates gathered more than 124,000 signatures to likely secure a November 2026 ballot spot for a rent control measure -- the first since the state legislature repealed local rent control authority in 1994 by a 51-49 margin. The proposed measure caps annual rent increases at 5% or the rate of inflation (whichever is lower), with exemptions for small owner-occupied buildings and buildings under 10 years old.

## Key Points

The 1994 repeal's aftermath was documented: by 1998, average rents on previously rent-controlled units in Massachusetts had risen 54%. This provides the clearest available empirical window into what decontrol produces in the short term.

The structure of the proposed measure is worth studying as a design model:
- Cap: 5% or CPI, whichever is lower
- Exemption: small owner-occupied buildings (protects small landlords and owner-occupants)
- Exemption: buildings under 10 years old (new construction exclusion addresses the supply argument -- it removes the disincentive to build)

The new construction exemption is the design choice most relevant to Frederick's Real Rent Ordinance. It attempts to reconcile rent stabilization with housing production by ensuring that developers of new units face market rents for a period before caps apply. The 10-year window is a common benchmark in current rent stabilization design.

The proposal comes amid rapidly rising rents, growing tenant organizing, and a broader national debate about whether rent stabilization can complement rather than substitute for housing production strategies.

## Relevance

The Massachusetts measure is a useful peer case for three reasons:

First, the 10-year new construction exemption is a design element worth considering for the Real Rent Ordinance. Frederick's housing production target (1,400 units/year through 2035) depends on not discouraging new supply. An exemption window for new construction is one way to address that concern without abandoning rent stabilization.

Second, the 5% or CPI cap structure is among the most common in current rent stabilization policy. The California Costa-Hawkins framework, Portland's ordinance, and now Massachusetts are all converging on similar parameters. Having a peer ballot measure to cite in council deliberations is useful.

Third, the 54% rent increase data from the 1994 decontrol is the strongest available empirical argument for why rent stabilization that covers existing tenants matters and why repeal or grandfathering produces predictable harm.
