# Camden County Shared Housing -- Tackling Homelessness Without New Funding

## Summary
Camden County, NJ used a yearlong Shared Housing Institute training program -- paid for by the county -- to build interagency capacity for shared housing pilots funded entirely from existing resources: the county's Homeless Trust Fund and foundation partners. With federal supportive housing funding shrinking and county homelessness up 21% year-over-year, the county framed scarcity as a design constraint rather than a dealbreaker. The result is an emerging community-led, government-catalyzed system that doesn't wait for new dollars.

## Key Points

**What shared housing is:**
Two or more unrelated adults share a home or apartment, splitting rent and responsibilities. Unlike permanent supportive housing, it uses existing market-rate units rather than requiring new construction or dedicated subsidized inventory -- making it faster and cheaper to scale. Wraparound supports (case management, mental health, life skills) can be layered in to address the root causes of eviction: isolation, untreated mental health, housemate conflict, income loss.

**Camden's approach:**
- County paid for ~80 local service providers to complete a Shared Housing Institute training program in early 2025
- Providers were tasked with designing pilots financeable from existing resources
- County framed the challenge: how could a small landlord incentive pool ($10,000--$50,000) support a real program?
- County's Homeless Trust Fund already funds shared housing for youth and women re-entering after incarceration through a master lease model

**Master leasing as a cornerstone:**
Under master leasing, a nonprofit -- not an individual tenant -- is the contractual partner with the landlord. The organization pays rent and manages tenant matching and problem resolution. This shifts risk from the individual (who may have a criminal record or unstable housing history) to the organization. County made master leasing a funding condition for highest-acuity populations.

**Landlord engagement as the center of gravity:**
Providers identified landlord buy-in as the make-or-break factor. What landlords actually care about: guaranteed rent, reduced vacancies, lower turnover and repair costs, fewer eviction filings. Incentives don't have to be cash -- help with unit turnover (painting, flooring, minor repairs) can matter as much as a check. One provider started proactively building a shared landlord contact list with small property management companies and real estate agents.

**Role division:**
Government as catalyst (funding training, seeding incentive pools, convening providers, legitimizing the strategy) and nonprofits as implementers (designing pilots, building landlord relationships, writing concept papers, testing programs). County director Robert Jakubowski: if a provider can "write exactly what that looks like and how you would use the funds," the county could likely find money for it.

**Early results (under one year):**
- Shared housing pilots for women re-entering after incarceration (Kommunity Kares) and youth 16--24 (Transformative Justice Initiative, Making It Work) funded through the Homeless Trust Fund
- Master leasing required for all highest-acuity programs
- Landlord engagement event held April 1, 2025
- Provider network committed to documentation and shared learning

**The key insight:** Camden shifted the conversation from "we can't afford this" to "what's the simplest version we can test?"

## Relevance
This is directly applicable to Frederick. The city faces the same pressures -- rising homelessness, federal funding uncertainty, a tight rental market, and limited flexible capital. The Camden model suggests Frederick could launch a shared housing initiative using the city's existing housing trust fund and nonprofit partners without waiting for new federal or state dollars. Key questions for local adaptation: Does Frederick have a housing trust fund with flexible enough terms to seed a landlord incentive pool? Which local nonprofits (FCAA, organizations serving re-entry populations) have capacity to serve as master lessees? Is there appetite at DHCD or through a housing workgroup to convene a similar training cohort? The 21% homelessness increase and the framing of "government as catalyst, community as implementer" map well onto how Frederick's Council could frame a new initiative without taking on operational risk.
