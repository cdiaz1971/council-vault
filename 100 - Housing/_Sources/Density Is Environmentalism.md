# Density Is Environmentalism -- The Suburban Green Space Myth

## Summary

Suburban single-family development is substantially less energy-efficient and more environmentally harmful than dense multifamily housing, dismantling the popular belief that low-density living is more "natural" or sustainable.

## Key Points

The piece systematically dismantles the idea that suburban living is environmentally virtuous. A new high-rise multifamily development is 82% more energy efficient than a comparable new single-family home. The per-square-foot efficiency advantage single-family homes appear to have disappears once you account for the fact that they average 2.5 times the size of multifamily units.

On stormwater and land use: compact multifamily developments produce less pollutant and sediment runoff than suburban homes, and urban density actually preserves rural green space by slowing the outward sprawl of development. The "green yards" of suburbia exist partly because density elsewhere contains the pressure to develop.

On transportation: traffic congestion and its emissions are largely produced by suburban commuters driving into urban cores, not by urban residents. The average American commutes 54 minutes daily by car. Urban residents commute significantly less. The critique of urban traffic as a climate problem misidentifies who is generating it.

The Sierra Club formally recognizes that density is environmentalism.

## Relevance

This is a direct counter-argument to the "character of the neighborhood" and "green space preservation" objections that surface in Frederick LMC debates and opposition to infill and multifamily development. When opponents claim that denser development harms the environment or erases green space, the evidence runs the opposite direction. The frame "density is environmentalism" is usable in council remarks, public hearings, and communications around the LMC review and the housing production target resolution.
