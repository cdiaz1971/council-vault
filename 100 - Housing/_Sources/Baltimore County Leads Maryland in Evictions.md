# Baltimore County Leads Maryland in Evictions

## Summary
Baltimore County recorded more evictions than any Maryland jurisdiction in FY2025 even as statewide evictions fell, and its fixes (longer notice, repeal of the abandonment ordinance, a storage rule still being designed) map directly onto Frederick's Renter Protection Package.

## Key Points
Baltimore County recorded more than 4,300 evictions in fiscal year 2025, the most of any Maryland jurisdiction, according to DHCD and District Court data. DHCD Assistant Secretary of Homeless Solutions Danielle Meister called the county a significant outlier in the wrong direction. The Maryland Multi-Housing Association noted the county's numbers rose while statewide evictions declined.

The drivers the reporting identifies are layered. Pandemic-era federal emergency rental assistance is gone. The county spent all of its $115 million in ERA to prevent an estimated 8,500 evictions, and housing director Terry Hickey said that effort deferred rather than solved the underlying problems. Access to legal representation and government aid is thinner than elsewhere in the state. Median rent is about $1,700 and has risen nearly 8 percent over a decade, while median household income fell almost 8 percent from 2018 to 2024 to just under $88,000. A December 2025 county-commissioned assessment found a shortfall of at least 19,000 homes, and the county has struggled to add 1,000 affordable units in ten years despite a federal agreement to do so. County Council resistance to new housing, including APFO-style school capacity limits and the stalled Lutherville Station redevelopment, compounds the shortage. Homelessness in the county rose 28 percent over two years, faster than the rest of Central Maryland.

Most evictions are for nonpayment, with only a small share for lease violations. About a third of county households rent.

Rent court offers little protection in practice. A single 9 a.m. docket in Towson carried 735 eviction cases. Landlord agents work the room before the judge arrives, tenants who miss check-in lose their chance to be heard, and Maryland Legal Aid has observed tenants defaulting because they could not hear their names in an overcrowded courtroom. That morning most tenants did not appear and the judge ruled for landlords by default.

The county's abandonment ordinance treated anything left behind after an eviction as abandoned. One tenant, Sherry Gudger, alleges in a Maryland Legal Aid lawsuit that she was told she had 24 hours to retrieve belongings, which were instead thrown away, including identification documents. Her landlord was a nonprofit affordable housing provider.

In response, the County Council passed a bill last month extending pre-eviction notice from 6 days to 14 and repealing the abandonment ordinance. Stakeholders have until December 31 to recommend who stores tenants' belongings after an eviction, who pays, and for how long. Baltimore City is considering a similar storage proposal, and Anne Arundel recently gave tenants an extra day to collect possessions.

Zafar Shah of Maryland Legal Aid points to Montgomery County as the comparison. By population and rent levels it should have the highest eviction rate in the state, and it has the lowest, which he attributes to local government resources and nonprofit aid.

## Relevance
This is a near-neighbor case study for the Rights That Hold Up component of the [[Renter Protection Package - Legislative Case]]. Three provisions are worth checking against Frederick's draft and current code. The first is the length of pre-eviction notice. The second is whether Frederick or Frederick County has anything resembling an abandonment rule, and who handles tenant belongings after a writ is executed. The third is the storage design question Baltimore County left open until December 31, which Frederick could watch and borrow from rather than solve alone.

The Montgomery versus Baltimore County contrast is the strongest argument in the piece for local investment. It suggests eviction rates track local legal aid and assistance capacity more than rent levels, which supports pairing tenant protections with the eviction prevention approaches in [[Eviction Prevention as Local Housing Stability Policy]] and the installment model in [[Alexandria Eviction Diversion Program Lets Tenants Pay Back Rent in Installments]].

The defaults in Towson rent court also bear on a right-to-counsel or courtroom navigation conversation, and the Gudger case is a reminder that nonprofit affordable housing operators evict too, which matters for any argument that mission-driven ownership alone is sufficient protection.
