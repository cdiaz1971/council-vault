# AI-Assisted SNAP Case Triage -- Nevada SAS Platform Reduces Error Rates

## Summary
Nevada's Division of Social Services deployed a SAS Institute AI platform to triage SNAP case risk, saving roughly $330,000 across 3,000 case reviews in its first months and identifying 45 fraudulent cases in month one. The platform illustrates how AI can function as a workflow tool -- directing human attention to high-risk cases -- rather than as a decision-maker, in a domain where the stakes for individual families are high.

## Key Points
Nevada's SNAP payment error rate sat at 6.22% in FY2025, just above the new 6% federal threshold established by the Big Beautiful Bill. States above that threshold face paying a larger share of SNAP costs -- Nevada's potential additional liability is nearly $50 million starting in 2028.

The SAS platform, which went live in April 2026, pulls beneficiary data alongside external sources including income databases to generate a monthly risk-ranked list of cases most likely to contain errors. Caseworkers then triage from that list rather than reviewing the full 200,000+ case universe randomly or on complaint.

The platform catches common error types -- staff mistyping rent figures into case management systems, for example -- by cross-referencing the entered amount against ZIP code average rents. It also flags fraud indicators like multiple addresses or phone numbers appearing across SNAP cases. The key framing from the Nevada official: catching errors before payment leaves the agency is "much easier" than pursuing overpayment recovery from clients who received incorrect payments in good faith.

The Nevada official was candid about uncertainty: "It's way too soon to say we're going to make the federal deadline, but I am cautiously optimistic."

## Relevance
The Maryland SNAP Penalty Trap note documents a specific risk that this Nevada model speaks to directly: Maryland faces the same compliance cliff, and Frederick's ALICE households are the ones most exposed when states lose federal cost-sharing. This is the affirmative case for AI in benefit administration -- narrow, auditable, workflow-augmenting rather than decision-replacing -- and it maps onto the distinction César is developing between AI as a productivity tool and AI as a rights-affecting actor. See also the synthesis note [[When AI Governs vs When AI Assists -- Procurement Distinctions for Local Government]].

The tool did not replace caseworkers. It changed what caseworkers looked at first. That's the model worth citing when the govtech and surveillance policy threads intersect.
