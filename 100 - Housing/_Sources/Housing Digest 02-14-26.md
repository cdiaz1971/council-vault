## 1. Unlock Suburban Housing Potential and Put Empty Offices to Work

**Source:** American Planning Association (APA) - Planning Magazine

**Summary:**  
This article examines office-to-multifamily conversions as a dual-purpose solution to revitalizing commercial corridors while addressing housing supply shortages. With nearly 20% of office buildings sitting empty nationwide and a housing deficit of 4.5-7 million units, suburban areas are identified as particularly fertile ground for these projects. The piece highlights successful examples like the Amber Union project in Falcon Heights, Minnesota (125 attainable housing units) and Row on Merchant in suburban Cincinnati. Key findings include that tax credits, subsidies, and preservation incentives can offset high renovation costs, and that only 25% of office buildings are suitable candidates for conversion based on factors like location, floor plate size, and walkability.

**Relevance to Frederick:**  
Frederick's downtown and suburban office corridors may contain underutilized commercial buildings suitable for conversion to housing. As a mid-sized city with employment centers and transit connections, Frederick could benefit from adaptive reuse strategies to address housing needs without extensive new construction. The article emphasizes the importance of zoning code adaptations, reduced parking requirements, and expedited permitting—all tools Frederick could deploy. The case studies of similar-sized communities suggest that combining attainable housing goals with adaptive reuse could help Frederick address both housing affordability and commercial vacancy challenges.

**Key Issue Topics:**
- Office-to-residential conversions
- Adaptive reuse strategies
- Zoning reform for conversions
- Parking requirement reductions
- Tax credits and preservation incentives
- Attainable housing development
- Feasibility assessments for conversions

**URL:** https://www.planning.org/planning/2025/apr/unlock-suburban-housing-potential-and-put-empty-office-to-work/

## 2. Missing Middle Housing: Modeling Success Across an Unaffordable Region

**Source:** Congress for the New Urbanism (CNU)

**Summary:**  
This CNU Charter Award-winning project documents how Cape Cod addressed its housing crisis through Missing Middle housing strategies that respect regional character. Union Studio Architecture's Cape Cod Resiliency project demonstrates that context-appropriate Missing Middle housing (duplexes, townhouses, small apartment buildings) can achieve substantial density—up to 20 units per acre—while maintaining 1.5-2.5 story heights preferred by the community. The $450,000 state-funded project included extensive community engagement, documentation of historical development patterns, and creation of demonstration projects. The work shows how traditional building types can provide needed housing while reinforcing neighborhood character, particularly relevant where year-round workers (median income $67,000) are priced out by second-home buyers.

**Relevance to Frederick:**  
Like Cape Cod, Frederick has distinctive historical character and faces pressure from higher-income buyers from nearby metropolitan areas (Washington, D.C. and Baltimore). The Cape Cod model demonstrates how Frederick could increase housing density through context-sensitive Missing Middle housing that complements existing neighborhoods rather than disrupting them. The community engagement approach—including surveys on height preferences and use of local architectural precedents—provides a roadmap for Frederick to achieve housing goals while maintaining community buy-in. The project's success in achieving meaningful density (20 units/acre) with lower building heights could be particularly applicable to Frederick's historic districts and established neighborhoods.

**Key Issue Topics:**
- Missing Middle housing typologies
- Context-sensitive design
- Community engagement strategies
- Historical development pattern analysis
- Density without height increases
- Affordable housing for working families
- Traditional neighborhood patterns
- State housing grants

**URL:** https://www.cnu.org/publicsquare/2025/05/28/modeling-missing-middle-across-unaffordable-region

## 3. America's Housing Supply Crisis: Is the Suburban Frontier Closing?

**Source:** National Association of Home Builders (NAHB) - Eye on Housing

**Summary:**  
This analysis of research by economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko reveals a fundamental shift in housing markets: the traditional relationship between increasing home prices and new construction has weakened or reversed in many metro areas. Using Census tract data from the 1970s to 2010s, researchers found that housing supply growth has slowed significantly in low-density suburban areas, particularly in higher-priced markets where expansion traditionally occurred. The research focuses on Sunbelt regions (Dallas, Atlanta, Phoenix) that once led in new home construction but now face sharp slowdowns. The findings attribute this to growing regulatory barriers—stricter zoning, longer permitting processes—making suburban development less responsive to market demands. Even the historically pro-growth South is seeing increased regulatory barriers as incomes rise.

**Relevance to Frederick:**  
As a Maryland city within the Washington-Baltimore corridor, Frederick faces dynamics similar to those described in this research. While not a Sunbelt city, Frederick experiences pressure from higher-income metropolitan areas and must navigate Maryland's regulatory environment. The research suggests that Frederick should examine whether its own zoning and permitting processes are creating barriers to housing supply that prevent the market from responding to demand. The finding that regulatory barriers increase as incomes rise is particularly relevant, as Frederick's proximity to high-income job centers may trigger similar dynamics. Understanding this broader national trend can help Frederick policymakers recognize that housing supply challenges require proactive regulatory reform, not just reliance on market forces.

**Key Issue Topics:**
- Housing supply constraints
- Regulatory barriers to development
- Zoning restrictions
- Permitting process delays
- Price-supply relationship breakdown
- Low-density development challenges
- Metropolitan housing dynamics
- Market responsiveness

**URL:** https://eyeonhousing.org/2025/08/americas-housing-supply-crisis-is-the-suburban-frontier-closing/

## 4. Texas Suburbs Resist New State Law Allowing More Apartments

**Source:** The Texas Tribune

**Summary:**  
This investigative report examines how suburban officials in the Dallas-Fort Worth region are implementing local rules to curtail a sweeping Texas state law designed to allow more apartment construction. Despite state legislation aimed at reducing housing costs by enabling more multifamily development, suburban cities with deep devotion to single-family neighborhoods are creating design and size requirements to limit the law's impact. The article notes the irony that Texas suburbs have evolved from "bedroom communities with cheap, plentiful housing" into major corporate centers with some of the state's highest housing costs. Research cited in the piece shows that denser developments cost less to serve with infrastructure than sprawling single-family homes and generate more property tax revenue per acre—important context as Texas cities face financial strain from stagnating property values.

**Relevance to Frederick:**  
Frederick shares characteristics with the Texas suburbs described: it has evolved from a bedroom community to an economic center with growing employment. The Texas experience provides a cautionary tale about implementation gaps between state housing policy goals and local resistance. Frederick policymakers should note that the research cited demonstrates denser development actually reduces infrastructure costs and increases tax revenue—countering common local objections. The article highlights that financial arguments against multifamily housing (infrastructure strain, service costs) are contradicted by research, which could inform Frederick's own discussions. Additionally, the piece underscores how local design requirements can effectively nullify state or regional housing goals, suggesting Frederick should ensure its own regulations genuinely support rather than undermine affordability objectives.

**Key Issue Topics:**
- State vs. local housing policy
- Multifamily development resistance
- Single-family zoning protections
- Infrastructure cost analysis
- Property tax revenue per acre
- Design standards and regulations
- Housing affordability legislation
- Implementation challenges

**URL:** https://www.texastribune.org/2025/09/03/texas-legislature-apartments-housing-suburbs/

## 5. NeighborWorks America Survey: Broad Support for Missing Middle Housing

**Source:** NeighborWorks America

**Summary:**  
This October 2025 national survey of 4,201 adults reveals strong public support for diverse housing types and affordability measures across all community types. Key findings include: 95% of Americans (96% in suburbs) say affordability is important; 92% prioritize proximity to essential services; nearly two-thirds of Americans (67% in suburbs) would support zoning changes for affordability; 64% would consider neighborhoods with mixed housing types; and half are open to multi-generational housing solutions. The survey demonstrates that Americans are ready for Missing Middle housing solutions (duplexes, townhomes, ADUs, tiny homes) that fill gaps between single-family homes and large apartment buildings. The research supports findings that residential mobility enables affordable housing strategies through nonprofit ADU programs and other innovative models already being implemented by NeighborWorks network organizations.

**Relevance to Frederick:**  
This survey data provides Frederick with powerful evidence that suburban residents—the majority of Frederick's population—support housing diversity and zoning reform for affordability. With 67% of suburban residents supporting zoning changes to improve affordability and 64% open to mixed-housing-type neighborhoods, Frederick has a potential mandate for progressive housing policies. The findings challenge assumptions that suburban communities will uniformly oppose density or housing variety. Frederick could use this data to build public support for Missing Middle housing, ADU programs, and zoning reforms. The survey's emphasis on proximity to essential services (92%) aligns well with Frederick's walkable downtown and could guide infill development strategies. The broad support across income levels (65% under $50K, 62% at $50-100K, 65% over $100K) suggests housing diversity policies could build coalition support across Frederick's economic spectrum.

**Key Issue Topics:**
- Public opinion on housing diversity
- Missing Middle housing acceptance
- Zoning reform support
- Accessory Dwelling Units (ADUs)
- Multi-generational housing
- Proximity to services
- Mixed-income community support
- Suburban housing preferences
- Affordability as priority

## Summary of Key Themes

Across these five sources, several consistent themes emerge relevant to Frederick's housing policy:

1. **Regulatory Reform Imperative:** Multiple sources highlight that zoning restrictions, parking requirements, and permitting delays are major barriers to housing supply
2. **Missing Middle Solutions:** There is growing momentum and public support for housing types between single-family homes and large apartment buildings
3. **Context-Sensitive Approaches:** Successful housing initiatives maintain community character while increasing density through thoughtful design
4. **Economic Benefits of Density:** Research demonstrates that denser development reduces infrastructure costs and increases tax revenue
5. **Adaptive Reuse Opportunities:** Converting underutilized office and commercial properties offers paths to housing without greenfield development
6. **Public Support Exists:** Survey data shows suburban residents support housing diversity and zoning reform for affordability

These insights suggest Frederick has multiple policy tools and community support to address housing needs through incremental, context-appropriate strategies.
