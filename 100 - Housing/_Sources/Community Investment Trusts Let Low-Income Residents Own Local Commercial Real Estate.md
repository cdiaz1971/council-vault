# Community Investment Trusts Let Low-Income Residents Own Local Commercial Real Estate

## Summary
New community ownership vehicles, including Portland's Community Investment Trust, diversified community investment funds, and TREND's crowdfunded lane, let residents who are not accredited investors own neighborhood commercial property and have a say in local development.

## Key Points
Federal securities law mostly limits direct local investment to accredited investors, meaning people with $1 million in net worth outside their home or $200,000 in income. These models work inside the exceptions.

Portland's Community Investment Trust bought Plaza 122, a 29,000-square-foot strip mall with about 30 tenants, for $1.2 million in 2014, renovated and leased it up, and only then opened it to residents in 2017. Residents invest $10 to $100 a month, capped at $5,000, after a six-hour peer-taught course. A bank's direct pay letter of credit guarantees they can cash out, which is what makes it legal for low-income investors. By 2024, 345 households had invested $767,000, earned 7 percent annual dividends, and seen share value double; 51 percent were renters and 64 percent low-income. Replications are underway in Minneapolis, South Dallas (a "necklace" of small buildings), and Albany.

Diversified community investment funds pool resident money across local businesses, with at least 60 percent in real estate to satisfy securities rules. Rhode Island's Local Return is structured as a co-op with one vote per investor and higher dividends for small investors. The Lansing Growth Fund, anchored by a $9 million city commitment to a 28-unit brownfield project, gives a supermajority of small investors a veto over property sales.

TREND uses a foundation-backed fund to buy majority stakes in shopping centers, including one in Baltimore, and sells 5 to 49 percent to community members through regulation crowdfunding, with a $1,000 minimum and about 5 percent average dividends.

## Relevance
The Golden Mile has over 30 underutilized commercial parcels and the highest ALICE concentration in the city, which is the exact profile these models target: commercial displacement risk plus households with no path to asset ownership. A CIT or similar vehicle could pair with the CLT concept, with the CLT holding land for housing and a CIT holding neighborhood retail. The city's role in Lansing, a capital commitment to the first building, is the realistic ask of local government. The Maryland TREND property makes a regional site visit possible.
