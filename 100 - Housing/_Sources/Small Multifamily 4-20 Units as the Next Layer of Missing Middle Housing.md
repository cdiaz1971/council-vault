# Small Multifamily 4-20 Units as the Next Layer of Missing Middle Housing

## Summary

Desmond Dunn (CityBuilder NC) argues that Raleigh's missing middle conversation has stalled at duplexes, ADUs, and townhomes when the more consequential opportunity is in the 4-20 unit range: small apartment buildings that already exist quietly in older neighborhoods, that are neighborhood-scale when designed with intention, and that open the door to smaller and emerging developers who cannot compete on large projects.

## Key Points

The core provocation is that a six-unit building on a quarter acre, built in 1984, is already a normal part of Raleigh's older neighborhoods -- people just don't notice it. The argument is that "missing middle" as a reform agenda has undersold itself by stopping at one or two units. Fourplexes, 6-10 unit buildings, and 10-20 unit neighborhood-scale developments are the next layer, and they require the same logic: they are not foreign insertions, they are things that used to be legal and simply aren't anymore.

Dunn is explicit that the 4-20 unit range creates space for smaller, local developers -- a point directly relevant to the politics of housing reform, since it decouples production from the large regional developers who tend to generate NIMBY opposition. Pasadena's courtyard apartment buildings are cited as the livability proof case.

He identifies the systems that need to evolve alongside zoning: entitlement pathways, financing tools for smaller projects, appraisal and valuation approaches, and predevelopment support for emerging developers. These are not just technical problems; they are political ones.

## Relevance

This is exactly the argument Frederick needs for the next phase of LMC reform. The conversation has been anchored on ADUs, duplexes, and density bonuses -- the CityBuilder framing gives language for why 4-20 unit buildings are neighborhood-compatible and already present in Frederick's older stock. The small-developer angle is also useful for coalition purposes: it reframes density reform as a small business opportunity, not just a housing volume argument.

Worth pairing with the Dallas building code note and the Loft3 Raleigh case study when making the case for missing middle expansion in Frederick.
