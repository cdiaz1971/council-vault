# Social Housing Requires Zoning Reform to Scale

## Summary

The debate over social housing often skips a foundational precondition: you cannot build publicly financed housing at scale in cities where restrictive zoning blocks most new construction. A Jacobin piece (republished by Shelterforce in March 2026) uses New York City's ballot measures and Zohran Mamdani's proposed 200,000-unit social housing plan to argue that progressive housing advocates must also be pro-supply if social housing is to succeed.

## Key Points

New York City's land use regime requires case-by-case approval for most new construction, giving local elected officials enormous veto power over individual projects. The Creedmoor development illustrates the problem: a state-financed effort to build thousands of units of senior housing on an abandoned psychiatric facility site was delayed for years and ultimately shrunk by over 800 units to satisfy local officials wielding land use vetoes. Public financing alone could not overcome the regulatory obstacle.

The article identifies an unusual coalition opposing zoning reform: conservative outer-borough homeowners who oppose all construction, joined by tenant advocacy groups who use land use vetoes as leverage to extract affordability commitments from developers. The counterintuitive argument is that these vetoes, while sometimes winning individual concessions, make large-scale social housing politically and logistically impossible.

The left case for supporting ballot measures that streamline land use: if government wants to finance and build housing at scale, it needs a regulatory environment that doesn't block government projects the same way it blocks private ones. Supply-side reform is a precondition for social housing ambitions, not a concession to market-rate developers.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick's LMC review is the local analogue to New York's zoning debates. The question of whether Frederick's land management code enables or obstructs housing production -- including affordable and workforce housing -- connects directly to this argument. If the city commits to housing production targets, the code has to make that production possible. The framing here -- that pro-supply and pro-social-housing positions are mutually reinforcing -- is useful for the LMC conversation and for engaging advocates who treat these as competing positions.
