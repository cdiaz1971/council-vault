# San Francisco Upzoning Produces First Approved Tower

## Summary

San Francisco's Planning Commission approved an eight-story mixed-income condo tower at 230 Anza Street -- the first project permitted under a new upzoning law designed to increase the city's housing supply. It is a concrete peer city data point that upzoning generates real projects, even in low-rise neighborhoods with community resistance.

## Key Points

The project replaces a two-story building with an eight-story condo tower containing a mix of affordable and market-rate units, including family-sized apartments. The city's Planning Department framed the units as "missing middle" housing critical to keeping families in the city. The Planning Commission acknowledged the tower looks out of step with the surrounding low-rise neighborhood -- but attributed that visual awkwardness directly to the prior prohibition on density, not to the upzoning itself. The project cleared the approval despite neighborhood opposition.

## Relevance

This is the kind of live implementation evidence that strengthens Frederick's case for zoning reform. Opponents of LMC modernization and MPDU threshold changes often argue that upzoning produces theoretical benefits but not actual projects. San Francisco's approval is a rebuttal: a specific address, a specific project type, and a specific approval under a named law. The "missing middle" framing and the family housing emphasis both map onto Frederick's housing production arguments. The fact that the visual awkwardness is a product of past restriction -- not the new zoning -- is a useful rhetorical point when arguing for height and density reform in Frederick neighborhoods that currently underbuild.
