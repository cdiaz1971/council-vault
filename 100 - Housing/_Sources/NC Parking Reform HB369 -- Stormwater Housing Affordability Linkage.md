## Core Argument

Local parking mandates in North Carolina force small developers, churches, and property owners into impossible site design tradeoffs: build the required parking spaces, but then get penalized by stormwater and impervious surface regulations for the pavement you just had to pour. HB369 proposes to resolve this by reducing parking mandates while encouraging better stormwater management -- making it simultaneously a housing production bill and a flood resilience bill.

This is the kind of regulatory linkage that tends to get missed when parking and stormwater are handled by different departments with different mandates. The insight is that excessive parking requirements don't just increase housing costs -- they increase flood risk. Removing them does both jobs at once.

## NC Legislative Context (2026 Short Session)

CITYBUILDER is tracking several state-level housing reform priorities in the 2026 NC short session:

**HB369 (Parking Reform):** Reduces parking mandates; encourages stormwater performance. Currently in Senate State and Local Government Committee. Strong advocacy ask to contact committee members.

**Single-Stair Reform (SB492 predecessor):** Allowing taller single-stair apartment buildings with updated life safety standards -- more flexible layouts, family-sized units, smaller-footprint infill. Did not cross over in the long session; advocates pushing for incorporation into broader housing/code negotiations. The federal Road to Housing Act also touches this.

**Housing by Right in Commercial Zones (SB499 predecessor):** Allow residential development by right in commercial-zoned areas where roads and utilities already exist. Reduces approval uncertainty, supports corridor revitalization and incremental development.

**Small Lot Freedom:** Remove minimum lot size requirements for ADUs, remove minimum primary home size requirements, reduce barriers to cottage courts, allow private water/sewer connections across property lines via easements.

**Expand Administrative Approvals:** Create clearer review timelines, reduce duplicative processes, expand exempt plat and minor subdivision allowances. CITYBUILDER's point: when local approval systems become too complicated, pressure for broader state-level preemption grows.

**Affordable Housing Funding:** Several bills moving this session to expand affordable housing construction financing, including workforce housing and CLT support. NC Housing Coalition leading.

## Dillon's Rule Context

North Carolina is a Dillon's Rule state -- local governments can only use tools the General Assembly explicitly authorizes. This makes state-level reform essential: even municipalities that want to allow more housing can't, unless the legislature clears the path. Maryland operates similarly. Frederick's ability to implement LMC reforms is bounded by what state law permits, which is why Maryland's legislative session outcomes matter directly for local policy.

## Why This Matters for Cesar

HB369's framing -- parking reform as both affordability and flood resilience -- is directly transferable to Frederick's LMC conversation. The city already has stormwater requirements that interact with site design; the argument that parking minimums create impervious surface problems could be an additional vector for making the case to reduce or eliminate them in walkable zones.

The broader NC legislative agenda mirrors Frederick's LMC reform priorities almost exactly: single-stair buildings, by-right approvals, ADU reform, administrative streamlining. Useful to cite as a peer state leading on these issues.
