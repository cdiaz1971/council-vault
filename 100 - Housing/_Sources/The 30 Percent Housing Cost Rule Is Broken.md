# The 30 Percent Housing Cost Rule Is Broken -- Wages Cannot Support It

## Summary

The long-standing benchmark that housing should cost no more than 30% of income is functionally obsolete. It was designed around gross income, not take-home pay, and was calibrated to a wage and housing cost environment that no longer exists. Most renters in the United States cannot meet it regardless of their choices.

## Key Points

The 30% rule originated in federal housing affordability guidelines as a way to determine whether housing costs were reasonable relative to income. The core flaw: it calculates against gross income, not the money that actually lands in a paycheck after taxes, insurance, and other deductions. A household earning $60,000/year gross keeps far less than $5,000/month available for all expenses.

Beyond the gross/net problem, the rule treats all households as having equivalent financial obligations. It ignores debt loads, childcare costs, healthcare costs, and other fixed expenses that consume take-home pay before rent is considered. A renter carrying student loans and childcare has a very different effective housing budget than the rule implies.

The behavioral consequences are already visible: 58% of young adults who moved out of their family home have since moved back. Adults across age groups are turning to roommate arrangements to lower individual housing costs. People are relocating farther from employment centers, choosing smaller units, and making quality-of-life trade-offs that represent real costs the rule never captures.

The Northeast in particular has not benefited from the supply-side pressure relief that Sun Belt markets have experienced. Restrictive zoning is the primary variable -- markets that responded with new supply have seen price growth moderate.

## Relevance

This is foundational framing for the Housing Production Target Resolution. The 30% rule has historically been the benchmark that defines "affordability" -- and its collapse makes clear that affordability cannot be achieved simply by holding rents at 30% of some abstract income figure. If wages are structurally insufficient and take-home pay is the actual constraint, then production volume, income subsidy, and structural wage interventions are all necessary levers simultaneously.

This connects directly to [[Inequality Is the Housing Crisis -- Synthesis]] (income concentration as the underlying driver) and [[Affordability Is a Power Problem Not a Price Problem -- Synthesis]] (affordability as a structural power problem, not a price optimization problem). The Frederick ALICE data -- 45% of city residents at or below ALICE thresholds -- is the local expression of the same dynamic this article describes nationally.

For the Real Rent Ordinance, this framing also matters: rent stabilization is more defensible when the baseline standard for "affordable" has collapsed for the majority of renters.
