# Maryland Housing Law and Policy

## Frederick

- **State production targets.** DHCD's first targets (January 1, 2026) give the City of Frederick:
  - 824 units in 2026, rising to 931, 1,039, 1,146, and 1,254 in 2030: 5,194 over five years.
  - The City's share is proportional to its 32% of the County's housing stock (34,181 units).

  Frederick County's target rises from 2,566 to 3,905 a year (16,177 cumulative), against average recent production of 2,231 a year. Progress will be measured annually through Census building-permit data, and targets are recalculated in 2030 ([DHCD, 2030 Housing Production Targets](https://dhcd.maryland.gov/HousingStartsHere/Documents/housing-starts-here-executive-order-summary.pdf)). César's Resolution on Affordable Housing Action, tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]], currently sets a 4,000-unit floor over ten years.
- **Vesting implemented.** Frederick implemented the Housing Certainty Act through G-26-11, passed 5-0 on September 17, 2026 ([[Zoning Reform]]).
- **What the 2026 session didn't change.** With Starter and Silver Homes dead, R4 and R6 stay single-family-only unless the City acts ([[Zoning Reform]]). HB 894's parking limits don't reach Frederick's Brunswick Line MARC stations ([[Parking Reform]]).
- **City and County may split.** Maryland's association of counties (MACo) flagged counties as central to any state preemption push. Frederick City's interests in density, infrastructure, and school capacity may differ from Frederick County's, so the City shouldn't assume the County shares its position on state housing bills ([[Frederick City vs Frederick County on State Housing Preemption]]).

## The Housing Starts Here framework

- **Executive order.** Gov. Wes Moore's September 3, 2025 order (EO 01.01.2025.19):
  - directs DHCD, MDOT, and General Services to identify state land for transit-oriented development
  - cuts state permitting timelines and improves transparency on local production
  - creates a state housing ombudsman to help projects through permitting
  - directs DHCD to publish production targets for the state, each county, and each municipality with land-use authority, updated every five years
  - creates Maryland Housing Leadership Awards that give bonus points in DHCD funding competitions

  ([[Housing Executive Order Press Release]])
- **The targets.** DHCD estimates Maryland needs 184,784 more units by 2030. At the current pace of about 18,900 permits a year, the state would produce about half that. Statewide targets ramp from 22,947 units in 2026 to 39,041 in 2030, roughly doubling permitting. The method assumes a 4% vacancy rate and adds in the unhoused population. DHCD cautions that the targets don't fully capture low-income need and that supply must be paired with subsidies and renter protections ([DHCD, 2030 Housing Production Targets](https://dhcd.maryland.gov/HousingStartsHere/Documents/housing-starts-here-executive-order-summary.pdf); [[Maryland Housing Production Targets 2030]]).

## The 2026 session

- **Housing Certainty Act (HB 548 / SB 325).**
  - **Vesting:** a developer's rights vest for at least five years from submission of a complete application, locking in the rules in effect then. It also defers certain fee payments.
  - **Votes:** it passed the House 124-7 and the Senate 34-12, after a 2025 version died.
  - **County opposition resolved:** MACo, the chief 2025 opponent, supported the 2026 bill after coordination language was added.
  - **Sponsors:** Sen. Malcolm Augustine and Del. Dylan Behler.
  - **Timing:** signed May 26, 2026, effective October 1, 2026.

  ([[Maryland Housing Certainty Act -- Developer Vesting Rights 2026]]; [[Maryland Housing Certainty Act and Transit-Oriented Development Law Signed]]; [[Maryland Housing Certainty Act (2026)]])
- **Transit and Housing Opportunity Act (HB 894).** It restricts parking minimums and certain local decisions near qualifying transit and defers certain county taxes until near completion. The goal is to unlock 300+ acres and at least 7,000 homes ([[Maryland Housing Certainty Act and Transit-Oriented Development Law Signed]]).
- **What died.**
  - The Starter and Silver Homes Act (HB 239 / SB 36) would have limited local minimum lot sizes, down to 5,000 sq ft, for smaller starter and downsizing homes. It drew fierce county and local opposition and never got a floor vote ([[Maryland Housing Supply Crisis -- Inventory Down 16.4 Percent, Prices Up 4.7 Percent in 2026]]; [[Maryland Starter and Silver Homes Act (2026)]]).
  - Middle Housing Options (HB 778), the Generational Housing Act ADU mandate (HB 1538), and Good Cause Eviction (HB 774 / SB 462) also died.
  - HB 85 / SB 511, which created a process for cooperative limited-equity housing corporations, passed.

  ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]])
- **The counties' vehicle: the BAMBY Act.** MACo backed the Building Affordably in My Back Yard Act, introduced in January 2026 by Sen. Benjamin Brooks and Del. Nick Allen, as its counterpart to the governor's package. It has four pillars:
  - regulatory and construction certainty
  - development-friendly payment schedules
  - market tools, including authority for counties to add a transfer tax of up to 5% when an owner-occupied home is sold and won't remain owner-occupied
  - a statewide responsible-owner registry requiring residential owners to file an annual certificate with DHCD naming a sworn public contact, shareable with local governments

  MACo described a compromise with the governor's package as possibly close ([[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]]).
- **Municipal concerns.** The Maryland Municipal League took these positions:
  - **HB 894:** favorable with amendments, seeking changes to the automatic Enterprise Zone designation for transit areas and to the deferral of impact fees and excise taxes.
  - **Fee deferral generally:** it flagged cash-flow risks for municipal budgets.
  - **Starter and Silver Homes:** it raised concerns about local adequate-public-facilities (APFO) and infrastructure guardrails.

  ([[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]])

## Vesting and Frederick's LMC review

- **César's synthesis:**
  - **The timing incentive.** Vesting starts at complete application, so any LMC changes won't reach projects already on file. Developers aware of the coming review have reason to apply early.
  - **The moratorium.** A development moratorium during the review would need to address new applications, not just approvals, or projects would keep vesting under the old code.
  - **Speed matters.** Given that, the better path is to complete the review quickly and adopt new standards so vesting works with the new code. A long moratorium that stalls production while the review drags on is the worst outcome on both counts.
  - **Political tension.** A production resolution and a building moratorium send conflicting signals that need to be resolved on the record.

  ([[Maryland Housing Certainty Act ↔ Frederick LMC Reform Synthesis]]) Frederick's implementing ordinance and the LMC Review Commission are covered on [[Zoning Reform]].

## State land and transit

- **Baltimore regional TOD strategy.** Gov. Moore and MDOT plan to build about 5,000 homes on 134 acres of state-owned land near Baltimore transit stations, mostly existing parking lots, generating an estimated $1.4 billion in tax revenue. The first site is about 9 acres at Rogers Avenue Metro, with about 400 homes. Frederick's MARC service isn't part of it, but the approach of an agency developing its own surplus land near transit is transferable ([[Maryland Transit Housing Plan -- 5,000 Homes Near Transit]]).

## Market and shortage estimates

- **The market in 2026.** In May 2026, compared with a year earlier, statewide active inventory fell 16.4% to 15,395, new listings fell 22.1%, and the average sale price rose 4.7% to $547,405. Realtors blame a lack of building. The Montgomery County Renters Alliance cautions that "shortage" in the real-estate sense means market demand, not people without homes, and that deregulation shouldn't weaken renter protections ([[Maryland Housing Supply Crisis -- Inventory Down 16.4 Percent, Prices Up 4.7 Percent in 2026]]).
- **Why estimates differ.** Figures depend on method:
  - about 96,000 current shortage (Up for Growth, cited in Moore's order)
  - about 94,000 (Moore, 2026)
  - about 100,000 (Comptroller, October 2025)
  - 184,784 needed by 2030 (DHCD)
  - about 590,000 by 2045 (National Center for Smart Growth)

  DHCD's report notes that all methods point to sustained underproduction ([[Housing Executive Order Press Release]]; [[Maryland Housing Certainty Act and Transit-Oriented Development Law Signed]]; [[Maryland Housing Certainty Act -- Developer Vesting Rights 2026]]; [DHCD, 2030 Housing Production Targets](https://dhcd.maryland.gov/HousingStartsHere/Documents/housing-starts-here-executive-order-summary.pdf)).
- **The counties' framing.** MACo's 2026 preview:
  - Average Maryland home prices rose 39% from 2019 to 2025, from $320,600 to $446,400.
  - Fewer than half of Marylanders can afford the average-priced home.
  - Single-family construction costs rose about 80% from 2017 to 2024.
  - It named both national construction costs and Maryland land-use rules as causes, and flagged preemption as a likely legislative vehicle.

  ([[Housing Digest 02-24-26]])

## State housing politics

- **Candidates split on solutions.** Most of 141 General Assembly candidates surveyed agreed housing costs are too high but divided into three camps:
  - **Supply-side** (bipartisan, skewing Republican): Del. Chris Tomlinson (R-Frederick and Carroll) named permitting simplification and regulatory reduction.
  - **Tenant protections** (Democratic): rent stabilization, stronger protections, and a ban on private equity owning single-family homes.
  - **Root causes:** some Democrats pointed to wages and economic insecurity.

  ([[Maryland Candidates Divided on Housing Solutions -- Supply vs Tenant Protections]])
