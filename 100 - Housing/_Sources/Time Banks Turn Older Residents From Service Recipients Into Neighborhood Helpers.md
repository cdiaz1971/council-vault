# Time Banks Turn Older Residents From Service Recipients Into Neighborhood Helpers

## Summary
Time banks let neighbors trade help hour for hour, and in Detroit and Sebastopol older adults became the most active contributors. They fill everyday gaps city programs cannot, but only survive when someone funds the coordination work.

## Key Points
A time bank is an exchange where members earn a credit for each hour they help someone and spend credits when they need help, with every hour valued the same regardless of task. The model traces to civil rights attorney Edgar Cahn's "Time Dollars," developed in the early 1980s after federal social spending cuts, as a way to value caregiving and community work the market ignores. Because credits only buy help from other members, the value stays local.

The gap time banks fill is the ordinary stuff no government program is built for: a ride to a doctor, a loose porch step, an errand, a check-in. Paying for each of those is out of reach for many older adults, and asking a neighbor feels like an imposition. Detroit's Unity in Our Community TimeBank (842 members, about 1,000 exchanges last year) was partly founded inside an elder-serving organization to meet seniors' needs, and older members quickly became its most active contributors, doing repairs, making calls, mentoring, and offering companionship. Sebastopol, California saw the same thing. That reframes aging residents as a source of capacity, with real benefits for purpose and isolation in a population where about one in three report loneliness.

The limits are real. Time banks do not cover medical or personal care, demand for home repair can outrun supply, and matching is slower than hiring someone. The deciding factor is coordination: someone has to recruit, match requests to offers, onboard new members, and build trust. Volunteer coordinators burn out, and the exchange itself does not fit standard grant categories, so organizers end up funding it through side activities like a yoga or cooking class. The failure mode runs the other way too. A Pontiac time bank with institutional money but thin member participation collapsed when the grant ended.

The Sebastopol model shows what a city can do. The city provided seed funding in 2015, the time bank is now fiscally sponsored by a community cultural center, and it partners with the senior center so volunteers there earn credits usable across the network.

## Relevance
This belongs with the care economy and Affordability Beyond Housing work. Frederick's ALICE data shows 44 percent of senior households below the threshold and seniors as the largest age group below it, which is exactly the population for whom paid help is unaffordable and everyday needs go unmet. A time bank is a low-cost municipal lever that does not require building a new service: seed funding for a paid coordinator, space, and a partnership with an existing senior center or a congregation like UUCF. The Pontiac lesson matters for design. City money should back an organization with existing member relationships, not create one from scratch.
