# Affordability Is a Power Problem Not a Price Problem -- Synthesis

## Summary

Synthesis across Demos, Debt Collective, and the ALICE/CLT research threads showing that the affordability crisis is not a price problem to be solved by supply-and-demand interventions alone -- it is a power problem, requiring structural changes to who controls land, capital, and the rules of the economy.

## The Pattern

Two sources from this week's inbox converge on an argument that runs through several existing vault threads but hasn't been named directly as a synthesis:

- **Taifa Smith Butler (Demos)** argues that the affordability crisis is a story about power -- who concentrates it, who lacks it, how that imbalance writes the rules -- not simply a story about prices or supply
- **Hannah Appel (Debt Collective)** argues that the goal isn't "free" housing or education but "freeing" -- the distinction between transactional relief and structural transformation. Debt is not a personal failure; it is a mechanism of extraction built into systems designed to harvest rather than support

Both are making the same underlying claim: the conventional affordability frame (lower costs, increase supply, reduce inflation) treats symptoms. The actual disease is that a small number of actors hold enough power to shape both markets and the policies that govern them, and the households bearing the burden have no equivalent leverage.

The ALICE data for Frederick confirms this locally. Forty-five percent of City of Frederick households cannot meet basic needs -- housing, childcare, transportation, food, healthcare, technology. These are working households. The ALICE framing makes clear that they are not failing to work; the rules of the economy are written so that working does not clear the bar.

## Three Registers of the Same Problem

The power-framing shows up at three scales, each with different policy implications:

**1. Market power (corporate concentration)**
Smith Butler's frame: corporate landlords, private equity in housing, consolidated grocery and childcare markets. These actors can set prices above what households can sustain because they face insufficient competition and because they have the lobbying power to prevent regulation. Supply-only interventions don't touch this because new supply gets absorbed into the same concentrated ownership structures.

**2. Debt as extraction (racial capitalism)**
Appel's frame: debt is not incidental to the housing crisis -- it is one of its primary mechanisms. The mortgage system, student loans, medical debt, and utility arrears all function to transfer wealth upward from households with no buffer to actors with capital. Racial capitalism names why Black and brown households are disproportionately in this position: the same rules that exclude them from wealth-building expose them first to extraction. "Freeing" rather than just "free" means changing the extraction mechanism, not just the price.

**3. Rules and governance (who writes the policy)**
Both Demos and the CLT research make this point: affordability interventions that don't address who makes the rules will be reversed, undermined, or captured. Inclusionary zoning gets watered down by developer lobbying. Rent stabilization gets preempted at the state level. The CLT model is the clearest local counter-mechanism: permanently removing land from the speculative market and placing it under community governance removes it from the extractive loop entirely -- it can't be bought out because it's not for sale.

## What This Means for Frederick

The conventional Frederick housing debate often defaults to supply-and-demand: we need more units, zoning is too restrictive, permitting is too slow. That's not wrong -- production constraints are real and the LMC reform work is essential. But it is incomplete.

A supply argument without a power argument produces housing that gets built and then quickly priced out of reach of the households it was meant to serve -- unless the ownership and governance structures are intentionally designed to resist that outcome.

The policy stack that follows from the power framing:

1. **Production targets** (Housing Production Target Resolution) -- necessary but not sufficient alone. Sets the floor for how many units the city commits to enabling.

2. **Ownership structures** (CLT, deed restrictions, community benefit agreements) -- permanently decouples a portion of housing stock from market appreciation. The Frederick CLT work is the clearest available tool here.

3. **Anti-extraction provisions** (contract language, procurement standards, data center community benefit agreements) -- the same logic applies to municipal contracts: who captures value from public infrastructure? The City Values Act and the Municipal Tech Consent Architecture thread both operate in this register.

4. **Organizing frame** (debtor solidarity, tenant organizing, ALICE constituency) -- households are not isolated consumers making individual market decisions; they are a constituency with shared structural interests. The ALICE data provides the empirical foundation for that coalition argument at the Frederick level.

## The Rhetorical Implication

The power framing is also strategically useful for public communication. "We need to lower housing costs" invites a supply/demand debate that developers dominate. "45% of Frederick households are working full-time and still can't meet basic needs because the rules are written against them" is a different kind of argument -- one that names accountability rather than inviting a technical dispute.

Smith Butler's formulation is worth borrowing directly: this is not a story about rising costs. It is a story about who hoards power and who lacks it. That framing puts landlords, developers, and corporate actors in the position of defending their structural advantage rather than presenting supply restriction as the sole villain.
