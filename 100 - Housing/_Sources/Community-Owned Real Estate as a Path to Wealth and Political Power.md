# Community-Owned Real Estate as a Path to Wealth and Political Power

## Summary

The Kensington Corridor Trust explicitly frames community ownership of commercial real estate not as service delivery but as wealth accumulation and political power-building for communities that have been historically excluded from both.

## Key Points

KCT executive director Adriana Abizadeh-Barbour articulates a theory of change that is explicitly about power, not just amenities: "Historically, those who have amassed land, amass wealth. We want to build that same paradigm, right? Let's amass some land to amass some wealth to amass some power, so we can change policy and change systems as a community -- together, collectively."

This is a systems-change framing, not a service-delivery one. The grocery store is not the end goal. The end goal is community control over the economic conditions of the neighborhood -- and the land ownership is the mechanism.

The Kensington context matters: a predominantly Black and Brown low-income neighborhood that experienced decades of post-industrial disinvestment. Abizadeh-Barbour names the structural causes directly -- disinvestment, policy failures, and deliberate neglect -- rather than treating food access as an incidental gap to fill.

KCT's model involves holding properties long enough to attract mission-aligned operators, keeping rents below market so that operators can price products affordably, and building shared infrastructure (commercial kitchens, loading docks) that lets small local businesses scale.

The grocery store also connects to KCT's broader work of turning vacant commercial storefronts into community-owned, occupied, affordable spaces -- reducing vacancy not through speculative redevelopment but through community-controlled stewardship.

## Relevance

This is the political theory that underlies the CLT work already in the vault. This note extends it explicitly to commercial real estate and names the wealth-and-power logic directly -- which is useful when César needs to articulate why a CLT approach is worth the complexity compared to simpler subsidy or zoning mechanisms.

District 2's demographics -- foreign-born population 1.4x county rate, poverty rate 1.5x county rate -- make this framework directly applicable. Communities that have been systematically excluded from property ownership don't need more services delivered at them; they need the ownership structures that generate durable economic and political influence.
