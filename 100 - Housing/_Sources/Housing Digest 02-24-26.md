## 1. Governor Moore Announces Housing Growth and Affordability Agenda for 2026 Legislative Session

**Source:** Office of Governor Wes Moore / Maryland DHCD  
**URL:** https://governor.maryland.gov/news/press/pages/Governor-Moore-Announces-Housing-Growth-and-Affordability-Agenda-for-2026-Legislative-Session.aspx

**Summary:** Governor Moore unveiled three housing bills heading into Maryland's 2026 legislative session. The Maryland Transit & Housing Opportunity Act would eliminate minimum parking requirements near transit stations, promote mixed-use development, and give the state authority over land adjacent to transit hubs — unlocking over 300 acres of state-owned land for an estimated 7,000+ units. The Starter and Silver Homes Act would allow smaller single-family homes on lots as small as 5,000 square feet statewide, projecting homes up to 30% cheaper than current new construction. The Housing Certainty Act targets regulatory delays by locking in rules at time of project approval and streamlining impact fee collection.

**Relevance to Frederick:** All three bills directly affect Frederick. The Starter and Silver Homes Act's 5,000 sq ft minimum lot provision would override stricter local standards — Council needs to understand the preemption implications. The Housing Certainty Act removes the city's ability to modify conditions after approval, which affects how development agreements are structured. Frederick has MARC connections, so the transit-oriented provisions are also worth watching even if less immediately applicable than for PG County.

**Issue Topics:** Zoning reform, housing affordability, transit-oriented development, regulatory certainty, starter homes, state preemption of local land use

## 2. State of Maryland Publishes Housing Production Targets for Jurisdictions

**Source:** Maryland Department of Housing and Community Development  
**URL:** https://news.maryland.gov/dhcd/2026/01/09/state-of-maryland-publishes-housing-production-targets-for-jurisdictions/

**Summary:** Released December 31, 2025, DHCD's 2030 Housing Production Targets report estimates Maryland needs 184,784 additional housing units by 2030 to house projected households — nearly double what current permitting rates would produce. The state is on track to deliver only about 94,620 units at current pace. DHCD published per-jurisdiction targets for every county and municipality with land use authority. The report establishes annual benchmarks and positions housing production as a shared accountability framework between state and local governments.

**Relevance to Frederick:** Frederick City has land use authority and will have a specific published target. The report creates political and reputational stakes: jurisdictions that meet or exceed targets are eligible for a new Maryland Housing Leadership Award, while those that fall short will face scrutiny. Council should confirm what Frederick's specific unit target is and assess whether current pipeline projects close the gap. This also sets the baseline that state legislators will use when evaluating local compliance with proposed preemption bills.

**Issue Topics:** Housing production targets, state-local accountability, affordability benchmarks, workforce housing, permitting rates

## 3. Cities Rewrite Zoning Rules to Boost Housing Supply in 2026

**Source:** Smart Cities Dive  
**URL:** https://www.smartcitiesdive.com/news/zoning-housing-regulation-dallas-trends-2026-outlook/809833/

**Summary:** A January 2026 analysis from Smart Cities Dive examines how cities nationwide are pursuing zoning and building code reforms to unlock housing supply. Dallas is the featured case study — it revised its building code to apply a lighter version of the International Residential Code to buildings up to 8 units and 3 stories, eliminating sprinkler requirements and reducing costs for small multifamily development. Cities are also cutting parking minimums, creating by-right approvals, and streamlining permitting through pre-approved plan sets. The National League of Cities notes that smaller cities are now facing the same workforce housing crisis that was previously seen as a high-cost-metro problem. Limited staff capacity is flagged as the primary obstacle for smaller jurisdictions.

**Relevance to Frederick:** Frederick fits the "smaller jurisdiction with limited capacity" profile explicitly called out. The building code reforms Dallas made — specifically applying residential code standards to small multifamily buildings rather than commercial standards — are directly applicable. Frederick's missing middle production could be constrained by the same code mismatch. The piece also reinforces that parking reform is one of the highest-ROI regulatory changes available, which aligns with what the state is pushing via the Transit & Housing Opportunity Act.

**Issue Topics:** Zoning reform, building codes, missing middle housing, parking minimums, by-right approvals, permitting streamlining, workforce housing, small city capacity

## 4. Housing Reform in the States: A Menu of Options for 2026

**Source:** Mercatus Center  
**URL:** https://www.mercatus.org/research/policy-briefs/housing-reform-states-menu-options-2026

**Summary:** This Mercatus Center policy brief catalogs proven housing reforms for state legislatures to consider in 2026 sessions. Key recommendations include eliminating or capping parking minimums, applying residential building codes to small multifamily construction, simplifying HOA statutes to enable small-scale ownership structures (citing Nashville's success unlocking duplex zones), and enacting zoning certainty laws that vest entitlements at time of application. The brief frames restrictive zoning as the fundamental cause of the national housing shortage and argues that supply-side regulatory reform is the most durable solution.

**Relevance to Frederick:** The Nashville HOA statute example is actionable intelligence — Frederick could explore whether Maryland's HOA law similarly constrains small-scale development, and whether a state-level fix or local workaround is feasible. The vesting/certainty argument reinforces the Housing Certainty Act's purpose and gives Council a framework for evaluating the bill beyond just its immediate development pipeline effects. The menu format makes this useful as a reference document when evaluating specific proposals brought to Council.

**Issue Topics:** Parking minimums, zoning certainty, HOA reform, duplex/triplex legalization, missing middle, building codes, regulatory barriers

## 5. 2026 Issue Preview: Housing Availability and Affordability

**Source:** Conduit Street (Maryland Association of Counties)  
**URL:** https://conduitstreet.mdcounties.org/2025/12/30/2026-issue-preview-housing-availability-and-affordability/

**Summary:** MACo's year-end 2025 preview of the housing debate heading into Maryland's 2026 session. Key data points: average Maryland home prices rose 39% from 2019 to 2025 ($320,600 to $446,400), and fewer than half of Marylanders can afford the average-priced home. The state's Housing Needs Assessment projects a need for 590,000 new units by 2045. The brief identifies two structural causes: national construction cost increases (80% rise in single-family build cost from 2017–2024) and Maryland-specific land use regulations that constrain density and add process costs. Counties are flagged as central to the outcome of any legislative housing push, and preemptive legislation is explicitly described as a potential legislative vehicle.

**Relevance to Frederick:** This is the county-government lens on the same fight Governor Moore is pushing from the state level. Frederick City should be aware that MACo and Frederick County may have different — and potentially opposing — interests from the city on preemption bills. Frederick City and Frederick County have historically had jurisdictional friction on development boundaries. If the state overrides local zoning statewide, Frederick City's ability to manage density, infrastructure capacity, and school impact could be constrained in ways that differ from the county's concerns. This brief is useful for anticipating where County government may push back on state legislation.

**Issue Topics:** Housing affordability, construction costs, state-local preemption, land use regulation, housing needs assessment, county-state relations, long-term housing demand
