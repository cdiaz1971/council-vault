# Maryland Statewide Housing Revolving Fund Proposal

## Summary

Marks and Coale propose a $250M Maryland statewide housing revolving fund administered by DHCD, structured as subordinate bond-backed financing that recycles as projects repay, designed to fill the capital stack gap that stops approved projects from breaking ground.

## Key Points

Maryland faces a housing deficit of roughly 96,000 units concentrated in high-cost, high-demand jurisdictions. Land use reform alone -- even with the momentum from Governor Moore and the General Assembly -- cannot close that gap without a durable financing tool independent of annual budget cycles.

The proposed mechanism: Maryland issues bonds to seed a $250M fund administered by DHCD. The fund provides subordinate financing at below-market rates, covering 15-30% of a project's capital stack above what conventional lenders will reach. Developers repay with interest, servicing the bonds, while principal recycles into the next project. The result is a one-time public commitment that becomes a self-sustaining engine.

The authors argue explicitly against designing the fund around deep affordability only. Montgomery County's experience shows that funds built exclusively for low-income units serve the fewest projects and can't grow supply at scale. A mixed-income structure -- workforce and market-rate alongside deeply affordable, with a meaningful affordability set-aside required to participate -- deploys capital faster, generates returns that sustain the fund, and reaches more of the pipeline.

New York's $100M fund attracted $115M in additional private capital. Maryland could expect similar leverage through DHCD's existing LIHTC administration infrastructure, multifamily bond authority, and developer relationships -- advantages Nashville, Austin, and Columbus built their programs without.

## Relevance

The Housing Production Target Resolution (active, in-progress) documents a need for 13,774 units by 2035 and names financing infrastructure as a priority recommendation. A Maryland statewide revolving fund directly addresses the financing gap that makes affordable housing production structurally impossible at current subsidy levels -- the same gap documented in [[Affordable Housing Cannot Pay for Itself The Math Behind the Gap]].

If the fund advances in Annapolis, Frederick could be an early user through DHCD relationships and the city's existing pipeline of stalled or underfinanced housing projects. Worth tracking SB/HB introduction in the 2027 session. The mixed-income flexibility argument is also directly applicable to the ongoing MPDU reform debate in Public Works Committee -- it is the same structural logic.
