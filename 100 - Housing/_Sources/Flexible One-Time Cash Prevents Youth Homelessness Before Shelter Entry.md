# Flexible One-Time Cash Prevents Youth Homelessness Before Shelter Entry

## Summary
Point Source Youth's Targeted Housing Assistance Program gives young people a one-time flexible payment tied to an individualized housing plan, and in a seven-state Johns Hopkins evaluation 92.8 percent had not entered the homelessness system six months later. Baltimore is now testing whether the result holds locally.

## Key Points
Most youth homelessness starts with a specific, fixable financial shock: a family conflict, an unexpected bill that eats the rent, a security deposit a young parent cannot cover, lost campus housing, or no way to get to work. The amount needed to stop the slide is usually small next to the cost of what follows. The problem with existing assistance is fit. It is often too small, arrives too late, or can only be spent on a category that does not match the actual barrier.

The Targeted Housing Assistance Program is built around that mismatch. A young person facing an imminent crisis works with a trained community provider to write a housing action plan, and the program then pays whatever that plan needs in a single flexible payment, whether rent, utilities, transportation, moving costs, or something else.

The evidence so far comes from a seven-state pilot evaluated by the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health. 345 people aged 16 to 27 received an average of roughly $3,700, generally within 48 hours, reaching 623 people once dependents and household members are counted. Administrative records showed 92.8 percent had not used homelessness services six months later.

Baltimore is the replication test, run through a partnership with Thread, a local organization that can reach young people before they show up in the formal homelessness system. Private money has funded it so far. The authors argue the City and State should fund future cohorts and the delivery capacity around them (outreach, staffing, planning, follow-up, disbursement procedures, data sharing, and independent evaluation), scaled by what the local evidence shows. The piece is advocacy commentary, and the six-month window is short, so the durability of the effect is still an open question.

## Relevance
This is the upstream end of the Homelessness topic: prevention aimed before shelter entry rather than rehousing after. It also fits the logic of the WAVE Fines Act and the broader utility-assistance work, where a small, well-timed intervention keeps a household stable. Frederick does not run a youth-specific prevention fund. If a City Housing Fund or HHS flexible-assistance line is ever on the table, the design lessons here are concrete: individualized plans, unrestricted use, delivery in days, and a trusted community partner rather than an agency intake process. The Baltimore results will be the Maryland-specific evidence to watch.
