# Montgomery County Housing Production Fund as State Model

## Summary

Montgomery County's Housing Opportunities Commission built a $100M Housing Production Fund that replaces private equity in market-rate deals, cutting equity costs from 20% to 5%, projected to produce 6,000 units over 20 years with 1,800 permanently affordable -- and has been copied from Atlanta to Chicago to state-level programs in Michigan, Massachusetts, and New York.

## Key Points

HOC's fund was capitalized through an HOC bond and a matching county appropriation. The mechanism is equity replacement: HOC takes an ownership position in market-rate developments, replacing private equity investors. Private equity demands 15-20% returns; HOC charges 5%. That cost reduction is enough to make stalled, entitled projects financially viable without requiring deep public subsidy.

Projected output: 6,000 residential units over the fund's 20-year bond life, 1,800 permanently affordable. Interest on the fund's loans flows back to the county, offsetting the annual appropriation -- a structure that costs taxpayers almost nothing at steady state while producing housing at scale. Over time HOC accumulates cash flow and asset appreciation to reinvest.

The complementary tool: a $14M Affordable Housing Opportunity Fund, a rapid-acquisition vehicle matched 3-to-1 by private lending, used to preserve at-risk affordable properties faster than federal programs allow.

Developer uptake has been strong because HOC brings certainty of execution. Developers who have spent millions designing and entitling a project want a counterparty who can actually close -- not just a subsidy application. That execution certainty is what makes the model scale when the fund expands.

The model has been cited as the inspiration for housing production funds in Atlanta, Chicago, Michigan, Massachusetts, and New York. It is inside Maryland's borders, administered by a county agency, and scalable through DHCD's existing infrastructure.

## Relevance

This is the direct peer model for the Maryland statewide proposal in [[Maryland Statewide Housing Revolving Fund Proposal]]. For Frederick specifically, the equity-replacement mechanism is worth understanding as the city pursues its housing production targets -- it reframes the question from "how do we subsidize affordable units" to "how do we cut the cost of the capital that stops market-rate projects from penciling." Both mechanisms serve the 4,000-unit production floor in the Housing Production Target Resolution. The Montgomery County case is also useful for council education: a county-level success story inside Maryland is a more persuasive peer citation than Austin or Columbus.
