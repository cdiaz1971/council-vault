# Maryland Transit Housing Plan -- 5,000 Homes Near Transit

## Summary
Governor Wes Moore and MDOT unveiled the Baltimore Regional Transit-Oriented Development Strategy, a plan to develop 134 acres of state-owned land near transit stations into nearly 5,000 homes while generating an estimated $1.4 billion in state and local tax revenue. The strategy focuses on infill development using existing parking lots rather than clearing new land, and is directly tied to Moore's broader framing of housing as an economic mobility issue.

## Key Points

**The core numbers:**
- 134 acres of state-owned land available across the Baltimore region
- ~5,000 new homes projected from full buildout
- ~$1.4 billion in projected state and local tax revenue

**First target -- Rogers Avenue Metro Station (Baltimore):**
- ~9 acres of state land
- ~400 new homes
- ~$27 million in projected state and city tax revenue

**Transit context:**
- 78 new rail cars arriving for metro and light rail by 2027
- Current Baltimore Metro: 14 stations, 15.5 miles (Owings Mills to Johns Hopkins Hospital)
- 12,500 average weekday riders; 5.5 million annual riders in 2024

**Strategic framing:**
- Moore: "connected to transit, connected to community and connected to opportunity"
- Baltimore Mayor Brandon Scott: development reduces the need to drive to reach transit
- Explicitly uses existing parking lots -- no new land clearing required

## Relevance
This is a Baltimore-region initiative using Baltimore Metro subway land, so Frederick is not directly in scope -- Frederick's rail access is MARC commuter rail, not the Baltimore Metro. However, the model matters: a state agency (MDOT) proactively developing its own surplus land for housing near transit is a transferable concept. If MDOT or MTA holds land near the Frederick MARC station or other state-owned parcels in the city, there may be an opportunity to raise this frame with state partners. Also worth watching as a precedent for how Maryland is positioning transit-oriented housing as an economic argument, not just a planning one -- useful framing for local conversations about density near the downtown Frederick transit hub.
