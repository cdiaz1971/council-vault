# Housing Advocacy -- Ten Reasons for Realistic Hope Part Three

## Summary

Part three of a running series from Housing is a Human Right offering evidence-based grounds for optimism on housing and economic justice. This installment covers reasons six and seven: growing momentum to tax the wealthy at higher rates, and state-level progress raising minimum wages toward a living wage standard.

## Key Points

**Reason 6 -- Progress on taxing the wealthy.** The author argues that the funding gap for universal housing and healthcare is a political choice, not an economic constraint -- the U.S. maintained top marginal tax rates of 91% in the 1950s. California has a Billionaire Tax on the November ballot, targeting roughly 200 in-state billionaires to fund healthcare, education, and food assistance. National polling shows broad majority support for higher taxes on wealthy individuals and corporations. The Roosevelt Institute has a new federal-level proposal. Wealth inequality is at historic highs; the essay frames rising taxes on the ultra-wealthy as the structural response.

**Reason 7 -- Minimum wage momentum.** The federal minimum wage remains $7.25/hour, well below what a living wage requires in most states. The essay cites research showing minimum wage increases are politically durable (passing in red, blue, and purple states), economically net-positive (lower turnover, higher productivity, small price adjustments), and broadly popular. 34 states and the District have already raised wages above the federal floor. Virginia committed to $15/hour by 2028. Federal legislation has been filed. The author frames the cost-of-living crisis as fundamentally a pay crisis, not just a housing cost crisis.

Earlier installments in the series covered: military spending scale relative to social needs, disability income support, Minnesota grassroots resistance to federal overreach, union growth, and tenant-side technology tools.

## Relevance

Background context for Frederick's housing and human services budget conversations. The framing that housing instability is downstream of wages is directly relevant to the water shutoff prevention and eviction diversion work in the FY2027 budget cycle. The minimum wage argument is also relevant when evaluating workforce housing needs and MPDU thresholds -- what counts as "affordable" changes depending on where wages actually land. Less directly actionable than the other housing notes this cycle, but useful as a framing reference for the [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] and any public testimony or council communications on housing affordability.
