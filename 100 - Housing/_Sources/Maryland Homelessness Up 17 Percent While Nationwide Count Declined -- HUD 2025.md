# Maryland Homelessness Up 17% While Nationwide Count Declined -- HUD 2025

## Summary

HUD January 2025 point-in-time count shows national homelessness down 3% to 745K, but Maryland up 17%, third-worst nationally; Trump administration used the data to attack Housing First policies while simultaneously shifting HUD toward transitional housing with work requirements.

## Key Points

**The national numbers:** HUD counted 745,652 homeless people in January 2025, down 3% from 2024. Decline driven largely by a 4% drop in emergency shelter population; unsheltered count fell 3%. The count was conducted before the Trump administration's shift away from long-term housing (Housing First) toward transitional housing with work and addiction treatment requirements.

**Maryland's outlier position:** Maryland homelessness up 17% year-over-year -- third-worst nationally after North Carolina (+33%, largely Hurricane Helene-driven) and Oregon (+19%). Hawaii (-41%) and Illinois (-44%) saw the largest decreases.

**National context:** Homelessness is up 27% since 2013 overall. The Trump administration is using this to attack Housing First policies pursued by cities and states over that period, framing the long-term trend as proof of policy failure.

**HUD's policy shift:** Before the count was conducted, the administration announced a move away from permanent supportive housing toward transitional housing with work and addiction treatment requirements -- a significant ideological reversal that will affect how federal homelessness dollars flow.

**One positive trend:** Unhoused families with children declined 11% nationally from 2024 to 2025.

**Benchmark:** About 22 of every 10,000 Americans are homeless on a given night nationally. New York leads at 73 per 10,000.

## Relevance

Maryland's 17% increase in homelessness, in a year when the national number declined, is a significant data point for the Affordability Beyond Housing agenda. It suggests that Maryland's mix of housing costs, income stagnation, and service capacity is producing worse outcomes than most states even during a period of national improvement.

For Frederick, this matters in two ways: (1) it grounds the housing stability argument in state-level data rather than just national trends, and (2) it creates political context -- the Trump administration is actively using homelessness data to undermine Housing First and redirect federal resources away from permanent housing. Frederick's HHS strategy and any reliance on federal homelessness funding should account for that policy shift.
