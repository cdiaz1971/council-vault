# Incremental Developer Builds a 35-Unit Affordable For-Rent Pocket Neighborhood in Memphis

## Summary
Memphis builder Andre Jones completed Malone Park Commons, a 35-unit for-rent pocket neighborhood of cottages and fourplexes, over five years by revising the pro forma more than 30 times; his work also pushed Memphis to reduce minimum lot sizes.

## Key Points
Aaron Lubeck, a faculty member of the Incremental Development Alliance, profiles Malone Park Commons in Memphis, a 35-home rental pocket neighborhood built by Andre Jones, a builder and son of a brick mason who attended the Alliance's inaugural 2015 boot camp. Jones built Memphis's first new ADU after the city relegalized them and its first new fourplex in generations before taking on this project.

The development keeps classic New Urbanist hallmarks (front porches, homes clustered around a shared green, walkable small-scale retail including a matcha and smoothie shop) but is built and leased as rental housing rather than for-sale product, which changes the design math: porches sit roughly 22 feet apart rather than the 30-40 feet Lubeck would recommend for a for-sale cottage court, because renters invest less in personalizing semi-private space than owners do.

Jones revised the project's financial pro forma more than 30 times to make it pencil and built the 35 units incrementally over five years, leasing and stabilizing each phase before starting the next. Lubeck frames this as the central lesson: absent deep subsidy, affordable new-construction rental housing requires more homes on less land, smaller homes, cheap land and debt, and a developer willing to fight the regulatory obstacle course, not a single trick.

That obstacle course is itemized: minimum lot sizes, fee-simple subdivision limits, utility easement standards sized for large subdivisions, large rear setbacks, metering requirements built around either conventional subdivisions or conventional apartment buildings, parking requirements governing both quantity and placement, open-space mandates that work against genuine shared commons, and special-permission review processes that add months, legal costs, and uncertainty. Each requirement sounds reasonable alone; together they block the kind of small-scale development most cities say they want.

Jones's work is credited with persuading Memphis to reduce minimum lot sizes and with influencing state and local building codes elsewhere in Tennessee. Lubeck contrasts this "build it, then use it to argue for the rule change" model with the more common assumption that policy change has to precede practice. He also cites Oregon's statewide middle-housing reforms, which by-right legalized cottage clusters and made them common in Portland, as the clearest demonstration of latent demand once regulatory barriers are removed.

## Relevance
Directly relevant to the ADU Ordinance (PC25-614ZTA) and the broader missing-middle production argument César is already making: minimum lot size, parking, setback, and permitting-process barriers are the same categories identified in Frederick's own ADU debate. The incremental, rental-focused framing is a useful rebuttal to the Citizens for Responsible Growth argument that relaxing owner-occupancy and size caps favors absentee investors -- this case shows a small, local, incremental builder producing affordable rental units precisely because regulatory barriers were lowered, not because of investor-scale capital. Also useful evidence for Inclusionary Zoning and Housing Production Strategies on why market-rate missing-middle rental production needs regulatory relief, not just subsidy, to pencil.
