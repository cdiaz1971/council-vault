# State-Level Land Use and Permitting Reform -- Patterns and Models

## Summary

States are increasingly preempting restrictive local land use regulations and streamlining permitting processes to accelerate housing production. Vermont's "permit czar" model -- routing applicants through a single entry point while government handles cross-agency coordination internally -- is among the more ambitious examples.

## Key Points

A NYU Furman Center / States Forum report documents a wide gap in housing permitting rates across states, attributing much of it to restrictive local zoning and land use processes. States are responding with both sticks and carrots:

- Arizona (2024): Municipalities must publish a housing needs assessment every five years and report annual progress to the state housing department.
- Montana: Municipalities over 5,000 residents must allow increased density.
- Massachusetts: Requires a percentage of each town's housing stock to be affordable to low- or moderate-income households.
- New Hampshire: Rewards towns that pass pro-housing ordinances.

Several states are also directly limiting local discretion over permitting -- preventing lengthy review processes with open-ended standards, and capping the time projects can be debated before a decision is required.

Vermont's approach is the most operationally interesting. Gov. Phil Scott's September 2025 executive order introduced a "permit czar" model: rather than requiring developers to navigate up to six separate permit applications across multiple agencies, a single state entry point accepts the application and routes it internally through all relevant agencies. The developer gets one package back, completed. This doesn't change what permits are required -- it changes who does the coordination work. Vermont has also inventoried 130 eligible parcels of state-owned land for potential housing use, with 5 to 10 selected as test cases.

The key insight across these models: the cost and delay aren't always in the regulations themselves, but in who bears the burden of navigating them -- developers (expensive) or government (more efficient at scale).

## Relevance

Maryland's housing legislation landscape is moving in a similar direction, with state preemption of local zoning becoming more active. Frederick's LMC reform process and Housing Production Target Resolution sit squarely in this context -- understanding the range of state-level approaches helps anticipate what Maryland may mandate and where Frederick can get ahead of it. Vermont's permit czar model is also worth flagging for the city's permitting and planning operations: the model doesn't require regulatory change, just coordination redesign.
