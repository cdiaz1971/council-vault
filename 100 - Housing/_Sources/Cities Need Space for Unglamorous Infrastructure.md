# Cities Need Space for Unglamorous Infrastructure

## Summary

Every vision of the good city edits out the logistics layer that makes it possible. The plaza depends on delivery trucks. The walkable neighborhood depends on warehouses and cold storage. The knowledge economy depends on substations and data centers. When cities fail to plan deliberately for these uses, they scatter into worse locations or disappear entirely, creating crises that parcel-by-parcel zoning cannot resolve.

## Key Points

Ryan Puzycki opens with the Plaza de la Constitución in San Sebastián -- a quintessential European plaza that is, at 9am, a working parking lot for delivery trucks. The trucks make the café life possible. Once they leave, the plaza becomes what people picture when they imagine the good city. His point: the glamorous and unglamorous are not in opposition; the glamorous depends on the unglamorous.

He extends this to a zoning case in Austin, where an autonomous vehicle company sought to rezone an industrial lot near Dell's headquarters for fleet maintenance. The lot was surrounded by residential development. Commissioners disagreed about allowing an auto-intensive use in an area that had been gentrifying. Puzycki frames the question not as "should we allow this" but "if not here, where?" -- because the need doesn't disappear when the rezoning is denied.

The deeper argument is about planning at the system level versus the parcel level. Early zoning was designed to separate genuinely noxious uses from residential areas, which made sense. But as zoning became more granular, it became a tool for deferring city-scale tradeoffs onto individual parcels and neighborhood hearings. The result is that planners spend their time adjudicating setbacks for triplexes rather than planning where the next generation of logistics infrastructure goes.

The "sanctuary suburb" mindset -- capturing the benefits of urban life without the costs -- applies equally to logistics. Nobody wants a warehouse or a bus depot nearby, but every city needs them. When communities successfully exclude these uses, the uses reappear somewhere less suited to absorb them, often in lower-income neighborhoods with less political power to resist.

## Relevance

This connects directly to Frederick's data center conversation. The tension Puzycki describes -- high-impact, unglamorous infrastructure that enables the rest of the economy, sited through parcel-level political fights rather than system-level planning -- maps almost exactly onto what Frederick is navigating with data center permitting and community benefit agreements. The question "where should data centers go in Frederick County?" is the same structural question as "where should the AV depot go in Austin?" It requires a city-wide or county-wide answer, not a case-by-case one.

It also connects to the LMC review. One of the tensions in that process is between housing advocates (who want to convert every available parcel to residential) and the broader urban system (which needs non-residential uses to function). Puzycki would argue that every industrial site converted to housing is one fewer place for the infrastructure that makes housing livable.
