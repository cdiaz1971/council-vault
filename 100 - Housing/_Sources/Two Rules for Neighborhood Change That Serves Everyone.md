# Two Rules for Neighborhood Change That Serves Everyone

From Strong Towns, 2020. A framing for evaluating whether neighborhood growth is producing broadly shared benefit or concentrating gain among a few while displacing others.

## The Two Rules

Strong Towns frames healthy neighborhood change around two criteria: first, that change should improve conditions for the people already there -- not just attract new, higher-income residents while pushing out current ones; second, that change should build the tax base and fiscal strength of the city in ways that are durable, not dependent on continued growth to service infrastructure debt.

The current growth model -- large projects, greenfield development, subsidized by infrastructure spending that outlasts its productive life -- produces a few winners (developers, landowners who sell) and many losers (renters displaced by rising rents, municipalities left with infrastructure they can't maintain). The alternative is incremental, fine-grained change that strengthens existing neighborhoods rather than replacing them.

## The Equity Lens

The displacement question is the one Frederick council members will face most directly as housing pressure increases. Upzoning and densification produce affordability benefits at the metro scale over time, but they can produce localized displacement in the short term if not paired with tenant protections, anti-displacement policy, and genuine affordability requirements. Knowing which outcome you're producing requires tracking who benefits from each change, not just whether the change increases unit count.

## Why This Matters for César

This framing is useful for council deliberation on LMC changes, upzoning proposals, and any developer-driven rezoning requests. The question to ask of every proposal: does this serve the people already in the neighborhood, or does it replace them?
