# Homelessness in an Age of Criminalization -- Shelterforce Shifting Ground Series

## Summary
Editor's note launching Shelterforce's Shifting Ground series on homelessness after Grants Pass. Frames criminalization as the dominant local and federal response, names housing costs as the main driver of record homelessness, and previews coverage of funding conditions, attacks on Housing First, camping-ban campaigns, and street medicine.

## Key Points
- After *City of Grants Pass v. Johnson* (2024), municipalities have stepped up enforcement of camping laws against unsheltered people even when no shelter is available. The current federal administration has made criminalization its preferred approach.
- Homelessness has hit repeated record highs, rising everywhere, highest in New York and California. Much of it is new: a HUD AHAR finding that about two-thirds of families using shelters in 2022 had not been in the system in the prior two years. NLIHC and other experts attribute the rise mainly to housing costs.
- **Barriers named:** new conditions on federal homelessness funds creating provider uncertainty; attacks on Housing First; inadequate funding; sweeps, citations, and property destruction; and a national camping-ban campaign run by a group founded by tech billionaires (unnamed in the piece).
- **People doing the work:** outreach providers such as Melissa Moore (homeless seven years, now runs an outreach nonprofit, describes sweeps as damaging people's sense of self); street medicine teams; a Phoenix team providing care to people sleeping outside in extreme heat; advocates in Pittsfield, Massachusetts, who beat back a camping ban.
- No policy recommendations in this piece; the series will cover them. Worth watching for the Pittsfield and funding-conditions installments.

## Relevance
Frederick has not adopted a camping ban, and the Pittsfield installment is likely to be the most useful counter-campaign playbook if one is proposed locally. The new-to-homelessness statistic reinforces the affordability framing in Resolution 26-15 and the ALICE work: homelessness here is mainly a housing-cost outcome, not a separate population.
