## Summary

Urban3's Joe Minicozzi walked Falls Church, VA city council member Justine Underhill through Providence, Rhode Island to illustrate a counterintuitive truth about urban infrastructure: costs go up when you use more land, not when you add more people. The video walkthrough makes the case that downtown districts, which generate the most economic activity per acre, routinely end up subsidizing financially counterproductive suburban sprawl -- a dynamic that no council ever explicitly voted for.

## Key Points

- **The moving chain problem inverted**: While the CITYBUILDER piece focuses on housing supply moving chains (luxury housing frees up cheaper units downstream), Urban3 focuses on the fiscal moving chain -- dense buildings generate tax value that flows outward to fund infrastructure for lower-yield sprawl.
- **The $180M choice that was never made**: Minicozzi's central example is a city that ended up spending $180M more on stormwater than it needed to, as a direct consequence of accepting sprawl as a fait accompli. That money was never debated -- it was structurally determined by land-use decisions made without fiscal transparency.
- **The core principle**: Most infrastructure costs (stormwater, roads, utilities) scale with land area, not population. A 10-story building housing 100 people uses roughly the same land footprint -- and therefore the same infrastructure burden -- as a single-family home. The per-unit cost of that infrastructure is orders of magnitude lower in the dense building.
- **Political framing**: The argument is not just fiscal -- it's about democratic accountability. Councils that accept sprawl patterns are making a fiscal commitment they never explicitly authorized. Minicozzi frames this as a governance failure as much as a planning failure.
- **Falls Church as a peer city**: A VA city council member learning this framework from Urban3 suggests this kind of fiscal urbanism analysis is becoming a standard tool for local elected officials.

## Relevance

This note feeds directly into Frederick's housing production argument. The case against data center sprawl and the case for Missing Middle/infill housing share the same underlying logic: land-intensive uses impose infrastructure costs that dense uses do not. Urban3's framework could be applied to Frederick's fiscal note on density vs. sprawl, and offers quotable framing for council communications on why infill housing is a fiscal responsibility, not just a housing justice issue.

The Providence walkthrough is also useful as a peer city data point showing how fiscal urbanism analysis translates into council-level conversations.
