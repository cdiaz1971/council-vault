# Federal Housing Policy -- HUD Homelessness Grant Rules Struck Down

## Summary

On March 31, 2026, U.S. District Judge Mary McElroy (a Trump appointee) ruled that HUD violated the Administrative Procedure Act by drastically changing eligibility criteria for the Continuum of Care Builds grant program without adequate procedural justification. The ruling blocked changes that would have cut permanent supportive housing funding, restricted access for sanctuary jurisdictions, and imposed ideologically driven conditions on nonprofits.

## Key Points

HUD's November 2025 Notice of Funding Opportunity for Continuum of Care Builds would have reduced the share of grants available for permanent supportive housing from roughly 90% to a maximum of 30%. It also required that applicants not use definitions of sex other than binary, prohibited "racial preferences," and restricted access for programs in sanctuary jurisdictions.

The court found HUD acted "arbitrarily and capriciously" under the APA -- imposing major policy changes at the last minute, after statutory deadlines, without evidence or reasoned justification. Judge McElroy's language was sharp: she called it a "slapdash imposition of political whims."

On April 1, a separate three-judge panel issued a related ruling in a parallel case. Together, the rulings have blocked HUD's overhaul of homelessness funding for now. The legal fight has been ongoing since November 2025 when 20+ states' attorneys general sued.

The practical stakes: approximately 200,000 people were at risk if the funding changes had taken effect. Nonprofits were already running out of grant money with no replacement in sight by March 2026.

HUD had argued the new criteria promoted self-sufficiency and public safety -- framing rejected by the court.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick's Housing and Human Services department receives federal homelessness funding through CoC programs. Any major restructuring of HUD homelessness grants would flow down to Frederick's service providers and the city's capacity to address housing instability. The sanctuary jurisdiction restriction in the original HUD NOFO is directly relevant to Frederick's City Values Act -- it was explicitly designed to penalize jurisdictions like Frederick that limit cooperation with federal immigration enforcement. This ruling protects that position for now, but the legal battle is ongoing. See also [[City Values Act]] and [[FY2027 Budget -- Housing and Human Services Department]].
