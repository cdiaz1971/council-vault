# Maryland SNAP Penalty Trap -- Improving Error Rates Triggers Federal Cost-Sharing Cliff

## Summary

Maryland improved its SNAP payment error rate from 13.64% in FY2024 to 13.08% in FY2025 -- a genuine administrative achievement after the state had the second-highest error rate in the nation as recently as 2022. But that improvement now costs the state: states with error rates above 13.33 when multiplied by 1.5 and equal to or greater than 20 qualify for a one-year delay on new federal cost-sharing requirements. Maryland's improved rate falls just outside that threshold. The state faces at least $240M in new SNAP cost-sharing liability starting October 2027.

## Key Points

H.R. 1 (the federal reconciliation bill) introduced SNAP cost-sharing requirements for states with error rates above 6.0%, starting in October 2027. The penalty tiers: states pay 5%, 10%, or 15% of SNAP benefit costs depending on their error rate. Maryland's 13.08% rate puts it in the 15% tier.

The grace period provision was negotiated into H.R. 1 to give high-error-rate states more runway. A state's error rate multiplied by 1.5 must equal or exceed 20 to qualify (i.e., an error rate above 13.33%). Maryland's FY2024 rate of 13.64 x 1.5 = 20.46 -- that would have qualified. Its FY2025 rate of 13.08 x 1.5 = 19.62 -- it does not qualify.

The perverse incentive is explicit: the state was penalized for improving. An advocate quoted in the article described it directly as "an incentive to do worse." Maryland's DHS leadership is correctly refusing to follow that logic and is continuing to drive the error rate down -- but the short-term financial consequence is real.

Payment error rates measure administrative mistakes (overpayments and underpayments), not fraud. Maryland's rate has dropped from 35.56% in 2022 to 13.08% in 2025 through staffing improvements, training, and streamlined procedures.

More than 30 states face the cost-sharing requirement. The National Governors Association is calling for Congress to delay implementation until 2030 for all states. That effort has not succeeded as of this writing.

The downstream risk: states that cannot absorb the cost-sharing requirement could risk losing access to federal SNAP matching funds entirely, which would pull federal food assistance dollars out of the state and harm grocery workers, food supply chain workers, and SNAP-enrolled families simultaneously.

## Relevance

This is a live threat to the Affordability Beyond Housing agenda. [[Federal SNAP Cuts Are Overwhelming Local Food Systems]] documented the federal abdication of food security support; this story documents the specific mechanism by which that abdication is being imposed on Maryland through a structurally perverse penalty. The $240M+ liability hits a state already managing steep budget shortfalls -- which means it will eventually cascade to county-level programs, HHS capacity, and the food access infrastructure Frederick depends on.

For the council's Affordability Beyond Housing work, this is the context in which any Frederick food access policy operates. The city cannot offset $240M in state cost-sharing losses, but it can be on record understanding the landscape and arguing for state and federal relief. This is also useful background for any future conversation with Stuart Campbell (HHS) about food security programs and their federal dependency.
