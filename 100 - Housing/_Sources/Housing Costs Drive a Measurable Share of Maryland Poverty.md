# Housing Costs Drive a Measurable Share of Maryland Poverty

## Summary

New research by Zachary Parolin, summarized by Pew, finds Maryland among ten states where 16 to 34 percent of poverty is attributable to above-average housing costs. In those states rising rents absorbed more than half of SNAP benefit increases from 1989 to 2023.

## Key Points

Parolin, an Oxford economist, used the Census Bureau's Supplemental Poverty Measure, which unlike the official poverty measure counts benefits and adjusts the poverty line for local housing costs. Where rents run above the national average, a household needs more income to clear the poverty line. By separating out that housing adjustment, he identified the states where housing costs account for the largest share of measured poverty: Hawaii, California, D.C., New Jersey, Massachusetts, Colorado, Maryland, Connecticut, New York, and New Hampshire. Across these states the housing-driven share ranges from 16 to 34 percent, with Hawaii highest.

The second finding is about the safety net. In these states, rising housing costs offset more than half of the growth in SNAP benefits between 1989 and 2023. In New Jersey, rent increases consumed 84 percent of the added SNAP money. Income support that flows to households is being captured by housing costs before it can lift them out of poverty.

## Relevance

This gives the affordability agenda a poverty frame backed by recent, peer-reviewed-style research rather than just a cost-burden frame. Maryland is named directly, so the finding applies to Frederick's residents without needing to be translated from another state.

It pairs naturally with the ALICE data. Frederick City's 43 percent ALICE rate, against 32 percent countywide, describes households squeezed between poverty and a survival budget where housing is the largest line item. Parolin's work explains part of the mechanism: when rent rises faster than income and benefits, the gains from wages and programs disappear into housing.

The SNAP offset is also a useful bridge to the Food Access and SNAP work. With H.R. 1 tightening SNAP and shifting costs to states, the argument that housing costs are already eating the benefit is a reason to treat housing supply and food security as one problem.

Caveat for use: this is a state-level analysis. Frederick-specific numbers would require local SPM-style data that does not exist at the city level, so cite it as a Maryland finding, not a Frederick figure.
