# Housing Production Strategies

## Frederick

- **Need.** The City's Housing Needs Assessment projects 13,774 additional units needed by 2035, about 1,377 a year. About 8,133 cost-burdened households earn under $75,000 ([[Housing Production Target Memo]]). Ryan Trout's memo puts Frederick County's need at 31,853 units by 2035, about 14,000 of them in the City, and says production is running at less than half the required pace ([[A.1 Frederick Housing Fund Memo]]).
- **Process.** Review timelines run 18–24 months, against the Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force target of 8–10 months ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]).
- **The production target.** César's Resolution on Affordable Housing Action (Notion: Drafting) sets production goals and recommendations, including expedited permitting and a housing dashboard. It's tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]]. Zoning changes are on [[Zoning Reform]], funding on [[Housing Finance and Preservation]], and code and pre-approved-plan fixes on [[Building Types and Codes]].

## Local production plans

- **Portland.** In August 2024 Portland unanimously adopted its first Housing Production Strategy (Resolution 37673), as Oregon law requires within a year of a Housing Needs Analysis.
  - The analysis found a need for more than 120,000 units by 2045, about 6,000 a year.
  - The strategy's 35 actions across eight categories direct coordination among the land use, transportation, infrastructure, economic development, and housing bureaus. They include zoning and permitting reform, wider inclusionary access, and new TIF districts.
  - It pairs supply with anti-displacement goals.
  - A 2025 resolution ordered a study of social, municipal, and cooperative housing, and a temporary waiver of system development charges followed.

  ([[Portland Housing Production Strategy -- 35-Action Six-Year Plan]])
- **Building a target from data.** Lancaster, Massachusetts' 2024–2029 plan derives a 295-unit target from three drivers: household growth (210), restoring a healthy vacancy rate from 2.4% (77), and replacement (8).
  - It singles out households at 30–60% AMI as the fastest-growing and least served.
  - It compares the pipeline against the target.
  - It maps development constraints, which leave 36% of the town buildable.
  - An adopted plan with measurable progress gives the town "safe harbor" under state law.

  ([[Lancaster HPP 2024-2029 -- Comparative Reference for Housing Target Resolution]])
- **Public goals, publicly tracked.** Washington, DC set a goal of 36,000 new units by 2025, published affordable targets by neighborhood on a public dashboard, and hit the goal five months early. Philadelphia cut zoning-appeal times from 78 to 12 days through performance management. Denver's cross-department permitting team cut review times 30% ([[AI for Housing Permitting Use Cases and Guardrails]]).

## State coordination and reform menus

- **North Carolina's EO 36.** Gov. Josh Stein's May 19, 2026 order:
  - created a Senior Advisor for Housing to coordinate across agencies
  - directed an integrated platform linking zoning, infrastructure, transportation, permitting, and feasibility data
  - asked NCDOT to measure transportation projects' effect on housing and use Complete Streets
  - asked DEQ to bring flood-resilience data into housing patterns
  - told Commerce to weight housing in Opportunity Zone nominations

  ([[NC Governor Executive Order 36 -- Cross-Agency Housing Strategy]]) Whether it matters depends on implementation: agency staff buy-in, dedicated oversight capacity, and sustained outside pressure from advocates and elected officials ([[NC Housing Executive Order 36 Implementation Factors]]).
- **Mercatus's state menu for 2026:**
  - **ADU laws that go beyond legalization** to remove fees, owner-occupancy, and parking barriers (California's lesson).
  - **Commercial-to-residential conversion.**
  - **Duplex legalization paired with a two-unit HOA statute,** so conventional financing flows (Nashville).
  - **Vested rights,** as Tennessee enacted in 2025.
  - **Remedies where most existing buildings don't conform** (Boston found under 1% complied).

  ([[Mercatus State-Level Housing Reform Menu 2026 -- ADUs Vested Rights Duplex Legalization]]) Maryland has since enacted five-year vesting in the Housing Certainty Act, which Frederick implemented through G-26-11 ([[Zoning Reform]]).
- **State permitting models.** Examples of state action:
  - Arizona requires five-year housing needs assessments with annual progress reports.
  - Montana requires added density in cities over 5,000.
  - New Hampshire rewards pro-housing towns.
  - Several states cap how long reviews can run.

  Vermont's "permit czar" routes up to six permits through one entry point, so government, not the developer, does the coordination. Vermont also inventoried 130 state parcels for housing ([[State Land Use Reform -- Permitting Streamlining Models]]).

## Process tools

- **Exempt plats.** Raleigh's draft TC-4-26 would legalize a streamlined minor subdivision process. Creating a small-lot subdivision takes one to two months in Durham but one to two years in Raleigh ([[Raleigh Exempt Plat Ordinance]]).
- **AI in permitting, narrowly used.**
  - San Jose uses AI to prescreen permit applications for missing documents.
  - Pittsburgh scans voucher applications for errors.
  - Both keep humans in charge, and the stated guardrails are trustworthy data, clear policies, oversight, feedback, and audits. AI amplifies whatever the underlying system is designed to do.

  ([[AI for Housing Permitting Use Cases and Guardrails]]) Urban Institute researchers say "the jury's out" on AI solving permitting backlogs. Clear, objective code language is a precondition: an AI inherits a code's conflicts rather than resolving them. HUD funded a $3 million round of automated-permitting pilots in 2026 ([[AI Permitting Tools Are Promising but Not Sufficient to Solve the Housing Supply Crisis]]).

## Plans and bonds

- **Comprehensive plans set the lens.** Raleigh's first full comprehensive plan rewrite since 2009, with 12,000+ residents engaged in Phase 1, is framed by advocates as a shift from managing growth to welcoming it. The comp plan isn't binding, but it's the lens for rezonings, infrastructure, and capital planning, while the development ordinance is the rulebook ([[Raleigh Comprehensive Plan 2026 Update CITYBUILDER Framing]]).
- **Housing and transportation together.** Raleigh's 2026 housing and transportation bond hearing treated the two as one affordability problem, since a household's real cost is housing plus transportation ([[Raleigh Housing and Transportation Bonds -- Case Study]]).
