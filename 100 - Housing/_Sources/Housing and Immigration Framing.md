# Housing and Immigration Framing: Scarcity, Fatalism, and the Collective Benefits Strategy

## Summary

Research from the Joseph Rowntree Foundation on how public perception of housing is shaped by scarcity and fatalism mindsets, and how communicators can reframe housing debates away from eligibility and blame toward collective benefit.

## Key Points

The JRF/Nationwide Foundation seminar identified two dominant public mindsets that make housing and immigration debates toxic: scarcity (resources are finite, newcomers are a threat) and fatalism (the crisis is unsolvable, so why bother). Two additional frames -- reciprocity and "citizens first" -- both center eligibility rather than supply, which is the wrong frame for making the case for affordable housing.

The proposed counter-strategy has four components:

1. Lead with collective benefit, not individual need. The framing shift the seminar demonstrated: instead of "we need to house people who are struggling," say "building more affordable homes benefits all of us -- healthier, employed, connected communities." The move is from eligibility to universality.
2. Connect housing to broader community infrastructure (healthcare, transit, education) to avoid triggering scarcity anxieties about local capacity.
3. Demonstrate system efficacy -- highlight that policy interventions work, name successes, break complex problems into concrete steps. Cynicism about whether solutions exist is itself a political obstacle.
4. Focus on systemic redesign, not individual blame. Explain how systems were designed and can be redesigned.

The author notes that framing alone is insufficient -- the substantive questions around allocation fairness (what the system is, why it's fair, why it produces better outcomes than alternatives) also need to be communicable.

## Relevance

This is directly applicable to Frederick. The Real Rent Ordinance and City Values Act both sit at the intersection of housing and immigration politics, and the scarcity frame is exactly what opposition uses. The JRF framework gives a tested vocabulary: lead with "benefits all of us," connect to community infrastructure (schools, healthcare), and demonstrate that policy works. The collective benefit reframe is also consistent with the democratic survival synthesis -- making the case for civic investment as universal rather than targeted.

The author's note on cost rental cynicism in Ireland maps onto MPDU and LIHTC skepticism locally: overcritiquing affordable housing programs creates the impression that no solutions exist, which depresses political will.
