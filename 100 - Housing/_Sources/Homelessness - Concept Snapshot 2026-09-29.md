# Homelessness

## Frederick

- **Penalty review.** A state law change on October 1, 2025 raised the maximum misdemeanor and municipal-infraction fine from $1,000 to $5,000. At the June 25, 2026 work session, City Attorney Rachel Gray asked Council whether to raise City penalties. Her summary acknowledges that higher fines fall hardest on lower-income residents and suggests monitoring citation data. The misdemeanor list includes §6-21 (camping on public property) and §15-27.2 (panhandling), offenses that directly affect unhoused residents. The session took no vote ([[Workshop Brief 2026-06-25]]). As of late September, the City Attorney's office is still preparing the final fine amounts (per César, 2026-09-25).
- **Federal funding (unconfirmed).** Frederick's Housing and Human Services department receives federal Continuum of Care homelessness funding, according to the note on the HUD ruling. The vault doesn't otherwise confirm this ([[Federal Housing Policy -- HUD Homelessness Grant Rules Struck Down]]).
- **Regional support.** The Metropolitan Washington Council of Governments offers technical assistance on housing affordability and homelessness. Frederick's HHS Director sits on its Human Services Policy Committee and Housing Directors Advisory Committee ([[Workshop Brief 2026-07-23]]).
- **Related work.** Eviction diversion is on [[Tenant Protections]], and the SRO ordinance draft on [[Building Types and Codes]]. A shared housing pilot with master leasing, which the Sept 8 brief calls the cheapest homelessness intervention per household ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]), is among the planning-stage items in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].

## Scale

- **National.** HUD counted 771,480 people experiencing homelessness in January 2024, up 18% from 2023. Its January 2025 count was 745,652, down 3%, mainly from fewer people in emergency shelters.
  - Unhoused families with children fell 11%.
  - About 22 of every 10,000 Americans are homeless on a given night.
  - Homelessness is up 27% since 2013.

  ([[Maryland Homelessness Up 17 Percent While Nationwide Count Declined -- HUD 2025]]) Community Solutions had projected about 755,300 and called it stabilization at a high floor. Of the people counted in 2024, 912,807 were homeless for the first time ([[2025 Homelessness Estimates -- Stabilization After Record Surge]]).
- **Maryland.** Maryland rose 17% in HUD's 2025 count, the third-largest increase nationally after North Carolina (+33%, largely Hurricane Helene) and Oregon (+19%) ([[Maryland Homelessness Up 17 Percent While Nationwide Count Declined -- HUD 2025]]).

## The federal fight

- **Housing First under attack.** The Trump administration has cited the long-term rise in homelessness as proof that Housing First failed. It announced a shift from permanent supportive housing toward transitional housing with work and treatment requirements ([[Maryland Homelessness Up 17 Percent While Nationwide Count Declined -- HUD 2025]]).
- **The Continuum of Care ruling.** HUD's November 2025 funding notice for Continuum of Care Builds would have made these changes:
  - cut the share available for permanent supportive housing from about 90% to at most 30%
  - required binary sex definitions
  - barred "racial preferences"
  - restricted programs in sanctuary jurisdictions

  On March 31, 2026, U.S. District Judge Mary McElroy ruled that HUD acted arbitrarily and capriciously under the Administrative Procedure Act, calling the changes a "slapdash imposition of political whims." A related appellate ruling followed on April 1. About 200,000 people were at risk, and 20+ state attorneys general had sued ([[Federal Housing Policy -- HUD Homelessness Grant Rules Struck Down]]).

## What's straining supportive housing

- **A correction, not a whole system.** Clinical psychologist Harvey Lieberman argues that Housing First was a 1990s correction to programs that demanded sobriety or psychiatric stability before housing, and that it worked for that purpose. Supportive housing has since become the default destination for people leaving hospitals, jails, shelters, and emergency rooms. Psychiatric beds, rehabilitation, and addiction treatment have all shrunk. Residents now bring co-occurring mental illness, addiction, cognitive decline, and frailty that programs weren't staffed for ([[Housing First Was Designed as a Correction, Not a Complete System]]).
- **The failure pattern.** Lieberman describes a cycle:
  1. an intervention works and expands
  2. it absorbs the caseload of failing adjacent systems
  3. it deteriorates and gets blamed
  4. it's replaced by something more coercive that can't work either

  Utah's mandatory treatment campus collapsed over cost, civil liberties, and the lack of a clinical plan ([[Supportive Housing Is Being Asked to Absorb System-Wide Failures]]).
- **Shelter as infrastructure.** Journalist Kevin Fagan's *The Lost and the Found* frames homelessness as a consequence of treating housing as a speculative asset. He argues for permanent supportive housing with services built into the building. He points to the conversion and demolition of SRO hotels from the 1970s to the 1990s as removing the bottom rung of the market ([[Housing as Speculative Asset vs Shelter as Civic Infrastructure -- The Fagan Framing]]).

## Rehousing models

- **Cleveland's rapid rehousing.** Mayor Justin Bibb's A Home for Every Neighbor began in 2024 with $2 million from the general fund and $2.7 million in federal COVID-era rental aid.
  - **Who it serves:** unsheltered people who avoid congregate shelters, such as those with pets, partners, or trauma histories.
  - **How it works:** outreach workers build a list at camps, a unit acquisition specialist finds matching apartments, participants choose on an iPad, and a caseworker moves them in. It takes 11 days on average.
  - **Results:** it housed more than 200 people and cut the unsheltered count from about 300 to about 100. It met its 150-person goal nearly a year early and won two more years of funding at $1.9 million a year.
  - Of about 90 exits, 8 returned to homelessness.
  - Providers ruled out arrest threats as a tool.

  ([[Cleveland Home for Every Neighbor Rapid Rehousing Model]])
- **Landlords are the bottleneck.** Cleveland's first 12-person pilot took six weeks because units were hard to find. After the program built standing landlord relationships, the next 40 people were housed in three weeks.
  - The landlord pitch includes rent coverage, case management, and a fund for unusual costs.
  - Most programs hand participants a voucher and leave the search to them.
  - The average rent paid is $1,225 a month, and rising rents limit what relationships can deliver.

  ([[Landlord Engagement as the Bottleneck in Rapid Rehousing]])
- **Shared housing without new money.** Camden County, New Jersey, where homelessness rose 21% in a year, paid for about 80 providers to complete a shared housing training program. It then funded pilots from its existing Homeless Trust Fund for youth and for women re-entering after incarceration.
  - Master leasing is required for the highest-acuity programs: a nonprofit, not the tenant, holds the lease.
  - Landlords respond to guaranteed rent and help with turnover, and repairs or paint can matter as much as cash.
  - The county's role is catalyst, and nonprofits implement.

  ([[Camden County Shared Housing -- Tackling Homelessness Without New Funding]])

## Prevention

- **Systems thinking.** Ireland's housing charity Threshold argues that family homelessness comes from interacting failures rather than single events. Its framework:
  - make prevention a core outcome, with shared definitions of risk
  - track pathways and act on early signals like rising rent arrears before evictions spike
  - intervene upstream in private rentals
  - fund accessible advice
  - make local authorities the integrators across agencies
  - apply a child-impact lens

  Allegheny County, Pennsylvania links housing, child welfare, mental health, justice, and income data to identify households at risk of eviction, with external fairness reviews ([[Systems Thinking in Homelessness Prevention -- The Threshold Framework]]).

## Criminalization and zoning against shelters

- **Louisiana.** HB 211 would make "unauthorized camping" a crime: up to six months in jail and a $500 fine for a first offense, and up to two years plus unpaid labor for repeat offenses. Treatment programs offered as an alternative can charge participants, with unpaid labor for those who can't pay. The National Homelessness Law Center called it one of the most punitive bills in the country. It was advancing as of April 2026 ([[Louisiana Homelessness Criminalization Bill -- Unpaid Labor and Jail Time]]).
- **Zoning against shelters: Ocean City.** St. Paul's-by-the-Sea Episcopal Church runs an overnight shelter for 30–35 guests after the town told it to move people indoors from tents. It sued the town under the First Amendment and the Religious Land Use and Institutionalized Persons Act (RLUIPA) when Ocean City moved to close the shelter on zoning grounds. The town counterclaimed for an injunction ([[Ocean City vs St Pauls Homeless Shelter Zoning Case]]).
