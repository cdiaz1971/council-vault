# Resident Services Generate 26 Percent Higher Net Operating Income in Affordable Housing Properties

## Summary

Research from Stewards of Affordable Housing for the Future and Abt Global across 248 properties and 19 affordable housing organizations finds that service-enriched affordable housing generates 26% higher net operating income than comparable properties without resident services, with every $100 invested returning $259 in additional NOI and $397 in total revenue.

## Key Points

The financial case is strong. Properties with on-site resident services see 24% lower rent-arrears rates at both the $100 and $500 thresholds compared to similar properties without services. Eviction-related move-outs at service-enriched SAHF properties run at 1.2 per 100 households versus 7.3 eviction filings per 100 renters nationally.

The economic mobility findings are also striking. Between 2023 and 2024, working-age residents in SAHF properties with financial wellness and employment programs increased labor force participation by 5%, compared to 1% in properties without those services. Residents in service-enriched properties are 22% more likely to have a regular healthcare source and 14% more likely to have health insurance. Seniors in properties with on-site food pantries experience 27% lower food insecurity.

The research is based on 248 properties across 19 nonprofit affordable housing organizations. Capital One integrated $25 million in philanthropic capital into its $265 billion Community Benefits Plan specifically to fund resident services and demonstrate the case for permanent underwriting models.

The policy prescription: resident services must be treated as core housing infrastructure eligible in qualified allocation plans and preservation programs, not as discretionary programming that gets cut when margins tighten.

## Relevance

This research matters for Frederick in two ways. First, for the Lucas Village LIHTC thread and any future LIHTC preservation or redevelopment discussions: the case for requiring resident services as a condition of public subsidy is now financially grounded, not just mission-driven. Service-enriched properties perform better financially. Lenders and investors have reason to require them, not just advocates.

Second, for the broader affordability agenda: resident services reduce evictions, increase income, and improve health outcomes. All three connect to costs that Frederick ultimately absorbs in emergency services, school instability, and code enforcement. The case for investing in or requiring resident services is a fiscal case, not just a housing justice case. That framing matters for reaching Shackelford and Brehm.
