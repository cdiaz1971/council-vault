# States Can Put the Brakes on Landlord Collusion and Junk Fees

## Summary

State attorneys general have robust antitrust and consumer protection tools to pursue landlord collusion and junk fees even as federal enforcement weakens -- the RealPage case established the template, and algorithmic price-fixing is still price-fixing under the Sherman Act.

## Key Points

**The core argument:** Even if the FTC pulls back from housing antitrust enforcement, state AGs can act independently under the Sherman Act, which explicitly empowers them to bring antitrust claims on behalf of harmed residents. The existence of an algorithmic rent-setting platform matters less than the anticompetitive behavior it enables.

**The RealPage precedent:** The DOJ and eight states sued RealPage for enabling landlords to share competitively sensitive pricing data and collude on rents. A similar suit was brought in New Jersey under the Sherman Act against RealPage and ten of the state's largest landlords. Separately, the DOJ and six states sued Agri Stats for analogous information-sharing in the livestock industry -- establishing that the pattern is not housing-specific.

**What state AGs should do:**
- Monitor RealPage post-settlement and watch for new entrants into rental data aggregation
- Oppose mergers of corporate landlords, housing data aggregators, and homebuilders
- Pursue junk fee enforcement under consumer protection statutes; junk fees are charges that are deceptively presented or exceed the value of the service claimed

**The junk fee landscape:** Maryland signed onto a 26-state AG letter to the FTC in April 2026 urging a federal junk fee rule for rental housing. Colorado's fee transparency law (effective Jan 1, 2026) requires all mandatory fees to be included in the advertised total price. Virginia and Connecticut enacted similar disclosure requirements.

**Federal context:** A federal junk fee ANPRM was submitted in January 2026; public comment period closed April 13, 2026. Congress has proposed the Preventing Algorithmic Collusion Act and the Preventing the Algorithmic Facilitation of Rental Housing Cartels Act -- neither has passed.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick operates in a rental market affected by the same corporate landlord consolidation and algorithmic pricing dynamics described here. The city cannot bring antitrust suits -- that's AG territory -- but this piece frames the policy landscape shaping what renters in Frederick are actually experiencing. If renter protections are on the agenda in the [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] or LMC Review, the junk fee and collusion context is directly relevant: affordability is not just about supply, it's about what landlords extract on top of rent.

Maryland is already in the coalition pushing for federal action. Worth tracking whether the Maryland AG moves on any RealPage-adjacent enforcement.
