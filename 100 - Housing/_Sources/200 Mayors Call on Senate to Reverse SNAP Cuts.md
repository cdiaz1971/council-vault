# 200 Mayors Call on Senate to Reverse SNAP Cuts

## Summary

More than 200 bipartisan mayors signed a letter to the Senate Agriculture Committee demanding reversal of SNAP cuts in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act, which will push millions off food assistance and require states to fund a portion of benefits starting fall 2027.

## Key Points

The U.S. Conference of Mayors -- representing cities with populations of 30,000 or more -- sent a letter signed by 210 Democratic and Republican mayors asking the Senate Agriculture Committee to reconsider the deep SNAP cuts enacted in the One Big Beautiful Bill Act. Signatories include mayors of Baltimore, Las Vegas, Oklahoma City, and St. Louis, as well as smaller cities.

The law's key changes:
- New eligibility restrictions and work requirements for recipients
- Beginning fall 2027, states must for the first time fund a portion of SNAP benefits themselves -- a structural shift from fully federal funding
- States with payment error rates above 6% face a penalty requiring them to fund 5% to 15% of benefit costs (previously federal-only)
- Agriculture Secretary Brooke Rollins has characterized the error rate issue as a state accountability failure

Already, more than 4 million Americans have lost SNAP benefits. Food banks and pantries are under increasing pressure. Mayors argue that philanthropic organizations cannot substitute for a federal safety net, and that city and state budgets cannot fill the void.

The state cost-sharing mechanism beginning in 2027 is particularly consequential: it is not tied to fraud but to payment error rates, a technical federal calculation of overpayments and underpayments. States need time to improve error rates and budget for the new costs -- which experts project will exceed $9 billion nationally.

## Relevance

This directly compounds Frederick's SNAP penalty trap dynamics already documented in the vault. Maryland's elevated error rate puts it at risk of the new cost-sharing penalty exactly when the state will also be absorbing the 2027 state-funding obligation. The bipartisan nature of the mayors' coalition is notable -- this framing is available to César when discussing food access with constituents or council colleagues who are skeptical of federal spending arguments.

The 210-mayor letter also offers a model: local elected officials speaking collectively to federal agricultural policy. This is a posture César could consider through USCM engagement or through Frederick joining any Maryland municipal coalition response.
