# Conduit Bond Financing for Affordable Housing Primer

## Summary

A plain-language primer on conduit bond financing as a tool for lowering developer borrowing costs to make affordable housing financially viable, including the LIHTC connection and a path to deeper learning.

---

## The Problem This Tool Solves

Building affordable housing is structurally expensive. Construction costs, labor, land, and permitting add up to the same dollar amount whether a developer is building market-rate or affordable units. The difference is on the revenue side: affordable rents, by definition, are capped below what the market would otherwise charge. That gap between what it costs to build and what affordable rents can support is real and persistent, and it doesn't go away because a developer has good intentions.

Interest cost on a construction loan is one of the few variables that public policy can actually touch. Lower interest means lower debt service, and lower debt service means a project can be financially viable at lower rents than it otherwise would require.

---

## What a Conduit Bond Is

A conduit bond program is a mechanism where a government entity issues tax-exempt bonds on behalf of a private developer. The developer borrows at the government's lower tax-exempt interest rate instead of at private equity rates. The government entity is the legal issuer but not the economic risk-taker. The developer repays the bonds from project revenue. If the project fails, the bondholders take the loss. The city's general fund is not on the hook.

Why does this lower the interest rate? Because the bonds are tax-exempt. Investors who buy these bonds pay no federal income tax on the interest they earn. Because they don't owe taxes on that interest, they accept a lower rate than they would demand from a taxable private borrower. That tax exemption is effectively a federal subsidy that flows through the government issuer to the developer and, ultimately, to the tenants in the form of lower rents.

Critically, conduit bonds do not appear on the issuing government's balance sheet. They are not the city's debt. Rating agencies do not count conduit bonds against a municipality's debt ratios. The city's credit rating and debt capacity are not affected.

The risk the city does take on is reputational. If a project financed through a city-conduit bond fails publicly, there can be political blowback even though the city has no financial exposure. That's a real consideration, just a different category of risk than fiscal.

---

## The Bonus: How Bonds Unlock Federal Tax Credits

This is where the tool becomes significantly more powerful. There is a federal program called the Low Income Housing Tax Credit, universally abbreviated as LIHTC (pronounced "lie-tech"). It is the largest source of affordable housing financing in the country.

LIHTC comes in two varieties. The 9% credit is the most common form, awarded competitively through a state allocation process. The 4% credit is awarded "as of right" -- meaning non-competitively and automatically -- when a project is financed with tax-exempt private activity bonds and meets program requirements.

Investors, typically large banks or corporations, purchase the tax credits from developers in exchange for an equity share in the project. This equity reduces the amount of debt the developer needs to carry, making the project financially feasible while keeping rents affordable. Tax credits function as a dollar-for-dollar reduction in federal tax liability for the investor.

To put it plainly: the conduit bond lowers the developer's borrowing cost, and simultaneously triggers an equity injection from private investors who buy the tax credits. Both effects reduce the financial burden on the project. Together they can make an affordable housing development viable that would otherwise never get built.

To receive the full 4% LIHTC allocation, the project must be financed at least 50% with bond proceeds. Note: 2025 federal legislation reduced this threshold to 25% for bonds issued after December 31, 2025, which significantly expands access.

---

## What Affordability Comes With This

The affordability requirements are federal and non-negotiable. A developer using tax-exempt bond financing must either make 20% of units available to households at or below 50% AMI, or make 40% of units available to households at or below 60% AMI. These requirements run with the project for the compliance period.

---

## The Limits and Risks

**Volume cap.** Each year, the federal government authorizes each state to issue a specified volume of tax-exempt private activity bonds. That cap is finite and shared across all qualifying uses. Maryland manages its own allocation through DHCD.

**Project risk.** If the developer defaults, bondholders take the loss, not the city. But the city's name is on the bonds. Reputational risk is real even when fiscal risk is contained.

**Complexity.** Bond counsel, tax counsel, underwriters, investors, and state agencies are all involved. The administrative capacity to run a conduit program is not trivial.

**The gap persists.** Even with bonds and 4% credits, most affordable projects still have a financing gap. The 4% credit generates roughly 30% of project equity, compared to about 70% for the competitive 9% credit. Additional subsidy -- often from a local housing trust fund -- is typically needed to close the remaining gap.

**Start here (accessible):**

- Local Housing Solutions, "Increased Use of Multifamily Private Activity Bonds to Draw Down 4% Low Income Housing Tax Credits" -- localhousingsolutions.org. Written for a policy audience, not a finance audience. Explains the bond-to-LIHTC connection clearly.

- Hennepin County's Conduit Financing Guide (PDF) -- hennepin.us/en/business/work-with-henn-co/conduit-financing. A real jurisdiction's plain-language explanation of how they run their program.

**Go deeper:**

- Maryland DHCD's Multifamily Bond Program page -- dhcd.maryland.gov. Maryland's own version of this instrument, with current interest rate and structure information.

- Novogradac & Company -- novoco.com. Publishes extensively on LIHTC and bond financing. Start with any piece explaining the 50% test and the bond-credit relationship.

- UNC School of Government, "Local Conduit Issuances of Affordable Housing Bonds in North Carolina: The Basics" -- canons.sog.unc.edu. Written for local government officials. The clearest legal-structural explanation of the conduit mechanism from a government perspective, even though it is North Carolina-specific. The legal concepts translate.

- Westmont Advisors LIHTC glossary -- westmontadvisors.com. Covers the terminology heard in any real conversation with a developer or bond counsel.

- Apers.app, "4% LIHTC with Tax-Exempt Bonds: Deal Modeling" -- apers.app/learn/deal-structures/lihtc/4-percent-lihtc-tax-exempt-bonds-modeling. Walks through how a deal is modeled financially.
