# Puerto Rico -- Community-Led Food Sovereignty After Disaster

## Summary

A Grist longform piece traces how Hurricane Maria's devastation exposed Puerto Rico's extreme food insecurity -- about 85% of food imported, one-third of the population food-insecure -- and how community organizations rather than government stepped in to build durable alternatives: cooperatives, mutual aid kitchens, school farms, and agroecological networks. The piece was updated in 2026 to note that Trump-era FEMA cuts are threatening those same community systems.

## Key Points

Puerto Rico's food vulnerability is structural. The Jones Act (1920 Merchant Marine Act) effectively raised shipping costs and made it harder for local farmers to compete with mainland producers. Combined with a 20-year recession and agricultural sector decline -- farms down 30% from 1992 to 2007 -- the island arrived at Maria with almost no food buffer.

The community response was grassroots and durable. Organizations like Comedores Sociales de Puerto Rico ran mutual aid kitchens weekly for months after both Maria and Fiona. Other groups built out school-based agricultural programs and cooperatives. The thesis of the piece is that these weren't emergency stopgaps -- they became infrastructure that improved everyday life and built real resilience.

The current threat: the Trump administration's termination of FEMA grants and roughly $10 million in federal funding for two programs supporting Puerto Rican farmers and forest stewards (Smart Ag Puerto Rico and a related workforce program). Federal disaster preparedness infrastructure is being stripped at the same time as it's most needed.

## Why This Matters for César

The piece is relevant to César's interest in immigrant community resilience and his council work on housing and social infrastructure. Frederick's immigrant communities -- particularly Central American and Puerto Rican residents -- face analogous dynamics around food access, economic precarity, and distrust of government systems. The mutual aid kitchen model, and the framing of community-built resilience as infrastructure rather than charity, is worth holding as a reference for how to think about community investment. Also relevant to any federal funding disruption discussions at the council level.
