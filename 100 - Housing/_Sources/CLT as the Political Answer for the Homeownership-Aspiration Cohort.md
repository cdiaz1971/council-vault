# CLT as the Political Answer for the Homeownership-Aspiration Cohort

## The Problem Both Housing Models Miss

Frederick's housing policy sits between two dominant frameworks, and neither one reaches the voters who will ultimately decide whether housing policy has durable political support.

The abundance model -- supply-side, market-oriented, zoning reform focused -- makes efficiency arguments. It talks about units, supply curves, and filtering. It has no answer for why institutional investors owning single-family homes feels wrong to most voters, because its framework treats all supply as equivalent regardless of who owns it. It does not speak the language of belonging.

The Vienna model -- social housing, public provision, non-market tenure -- makes equity arguments. It has a compelling diagnosis of why markets fail and who gets left out. But it offers no compelling path to homeownership for working people. In its social-democratic flavor it can read as a professional-class lifestyle preference. In its socialist flavor it is perceived as nostalgia. Neither flavor speaks to the aspiration of owning something.

The emerging cohort that neither model reaches is not ideologically incoherent. They are organized around a specific set of preferences: homeownership as security and aspiration, hostility to corporate and institutional investors in residential markets, skepticism of both large-scale market development and large public housing programs. They want housing for people like them, not abstract supply or state provision. Their politics are organized around belonging, not efficiency or equity.

This cohort is not peripheral. In Frederick they are the residents who show up to oppose large multifamily developments -- not because they are against affordability, but because corporate-scale development feels imposed rather than grown from within the community. They are winnable on housing policy if the frame shifts from supply and equity to ownership and community stability.

## What CLTs Actually Offer

A community land trust permanently separates land ownership from building ownership. The trust owns the land in perpetuity. Residents own the building -- a house, a condo unit -- at a price calibrated to what working households can afford. When they sell, a resale formula keeps the home affordable for the next buyer. The household builds equity. The community retains permanent affordability without ongoing subsidy.

This is not rental. It is not public housing. It is not corporate development. It is homeownership -- with the land cost removed and the affordability permanence built in.

For the homeownership-aspiration cohort, this is the answer to the question neither model was asking: how do people like me actually get to own something in this city, and how does the community keep that ownership from being extracted away by the next market cycle?

## The Pluralism Connection

[[Pluralism Is Infrastructure -- Three Conditions for Democratic Pluralism]] argues that pluralism requires three types of infrastructure: rules infrastructure (who can participate), coalition infrastructure (organized civic actors who sustain themselves as political actors), and relational infrastructure (the habits and capacity to disagree productively across difference).

CLTs are all three simultaneously.

As rules infrastructure: a CLT changes who can participate in homeownership. It expands the set of households for whom ownership is possible -- working families, immigrants, essential workers, artists, the people who make a community function but are priced out of it. In District 2, that expansion is the Municipal ID's equivalent in the housing domain.

As coalition infrastructure: a CLT is a civic institution. Residents who own through a CLT have a shared stake in the trust's governance and long-term viability. That shared stake is the basis for ongoing organized political presence -- a constituency that did not exist before the CLT and that has durable reasons to remain engaged in local housing politics. This is the kind of coalition infrastructure the Anti-Authoritarian Playbook argues is missing from most progressive movements: organized actors with ongoing stakes, not episodic mobilizers.

As relational infrastructure: CLT governance requires working through disagreement about land use, resale formulas, eligibility criteria, and community priorities. It is a school for democratic practice in the most literal sense -- people who disagree about specifics, working together across time because they share a commitment to the institution's survival.

## The Political Frame for Frederick

Frederick's housing advocacy has so far framed the challenge primarily in supply and equity terms. That framing reaches the people already convinced. It does not reach the homeownership-aspiration cohort, and it does not build the coalition that survives the next electoral cycle.

The CLT frame says something different: this city is for people who live and work here, not for investors and developers who extract value and leave. If you want to own a home in Frederick, we are going to build the infrastructure that makes that possible and keeps it possible for the next generation. That is not a progressive argument. It is a belonging argument. It is the kind of argument that crosses the left-right divide that both the abundance model and the Vienna model are losing.

The concrete case for Frederick:

The MPDU program produces affordable units tied to market-rate developments. Those units are affordable as long as the MPDU period lasts, then they filter into the market. A CLT produces permanently affordable units. The difference is durable versus temporary affordability, and that durability is the political argument: we are not subsidizing a temporary fix, we are building something that stays.

The development community is a complicated partner here. Market-rate developers are not natural allies of CLTs because CLTs remove land from the speculative market. But workforce housing developers, LIHTC developers, and mission-driven CDFIs are. The political coalition for CLT expansion in Frederick runs through those partners, through faith communities that hold land, through the immigrant serving organizations in District 2, and through the homeownership-aspiration cohort that currently opposes market-rate density because it has no alternative frame to organize around.

## The Open Policy Questions

Three questions that need answers before this frame becomes a council initiative:

First, land. CLTs need land to put into trust. In Frederick, the most realistic sources are city-owned parcels, faith community land, and negotiated contributions from large developments as a community benefit. Which parcels are candidates? What is the city's current posture on land disposition?

Second, governance model. CLTs can be standalone nonprofits, city-sponsored entities, or hybrid models. The governance question determines who controls the trust and how community voice is built in. The Frederick Community Land Trust, if it exists, or a new entity -- what is the right structure for District 2's community?

Third, the MPDU intersection. Can CLT units satisfy MPDU requirements? If so, a policy change allowing MPDU compliance through CLT contribution rather than individual unit set-asides would dramatically expand the pipeline. This is a zoning and policy question that belongs in the LMC conversation.
