## What Single-Stair Reform Does

Current building codes in most US states require apartment buildings above a certain height to have two stairwells. This requirement was designed for fire safety in an era before modern sprinkler systems, but it has a major unintended consequence: it forces apartment floor plates to be wide enough to accommodate two cores, which pushes building footprints larger and eliminates many infill sites too narrow for a full double-stair building.

Single-stair reform -- allowing taller buildings with one stairwell when paired with modern fire suppression and life safety systems -- unlocks:

- **More flexible apartment layouts:** Single-stair buildings allow units on all sides of the core, creating better light, cross-ventilation, and larger family-sized units.
- **Smaller footprint infill:** Narrow urban lots become buildable. These are exactly the infill sites cities already have in their walkable neighborhoods.
- **Lower per-unit construction costs:** Smaller footprints mean less structural steel and concrete per unit.

## NC Context

CITYBUILDER has partnered with Single Stair NC over the past year. NC SB492 (Senators Moffitt and Mayfield) proposed modifying NC's building code to allow taller single-stair buildings. It did not cross over in the 2025 long session but advocates are pushing for incorporation into 2026 session negotiations.

The federal Road to Housing Act includes single-stair reform provisions -- a signal that this is no longer a niche urbanist idea but mainstream bipartisan housing policy.

## National Momentum

Several states and cities have already adopted or are actively debating single-stair reform: Washington state, New York City, and others. The concept has moved from architecture Twitter to state legislatures in under three years.

## Why This Matters for Cesar

Frederick's LMC work is focused on missing middle production. Single-stair reform is one of the highest-leverage building code changes for enabling small apartment buildings on urban infill sites -- exactly the type of development that fits Frederick's existing walkable neighborhoods without requiring large land assemblies.

Maryland has not yet passed single-stair reform at the state level. If NC passes SB492-equivalent legislation, Maryland and Frederick advocates will have a direct peer-state model to point to. This is worth tracking as a future LMC or state legislative advocacy item.
