# Zoning Reform

## Frederick and Maryland

- **What the LMC allows.** As of LMC Supplement 13 (March 2025):
  - **R4 and R6** allow only single-family detached homes, on minimum lots of 8,000 and 7,000 sq ft. They're Frederick's lowest-density residential zones.
  - **Duplexes and townhouses** are permitted by right in R8 through R20 and the downtown districts, at 4,000 sq ft per duplex and 1,400 sq ft per townhouse unit in most residential districts.
  - **Multifamily** isn't permitted in R4, R6, or R8.
  - **Triplexes, fourplexes, and sixplexes** aren't defined dwelling types in the dimensional tables.
  - **ADUs** are allowed by right in most residential zones. They're limited by an owner-occupancy requirement, an 800 sq ft cap on detached units, and a 10% cap in TNDs, PNDs, and MU zones.
  - **Inclusionary zoning.** Chapter 19 (MPDU) requires 12.5% affordable units in developments of 25 or more units, with a density bonus of up to 22% for going to 15%. The payment-in-lieu fee is $2.00 per sq ft.

  ([[Frederick Housing Policy Baseline]])
- **Since then.** The Mixed Use Vertical amendment passed 6-0 on August 20, 2026, effective August 30. It allows first-floor residential in MU-1 and MU-2 mixed-use buildings (Notion roadmap, checked 2026-09-24).
- **Recent and pending Frederick items** (Notion roadmap, checked 2026-09-24):
  - **ADU ordinance (PC25-614ZTA, Nash), at workshop.** It would:
    - remove owner-occupancy
    - reduce ADU parking
    - raise the detached cap to 1,200 sq ft, subject to Planning Commission approval

    No detached ADUs have been permitted since the 2021 amendment. The Planning Commission's 4-0 recommendation differs from the draft on owner-occupancy and parking. Maryland HB 1466 requires a compliant local ADU ordinance by October 1, 2026 and bars deed restrictions that unreasonably limit ADUs ([[ADU Ordinance Brief 2026-08-13]]).
  - **G-26-11, implementing the Maryland Housing Certainty Act (passed).** Introduced September 3, 2026, and passed 5-0 on September 17, 2026 (per César, 2026-09-24; Notion updated). It's an interim ordinance, ahead of a fuller cleanup of LMC conflicts, and it covers complete-application definitions, a 30-day completeness determination, five-year vesting, and impact-fee timing.
  - **Major site plan final approval authority (Brehm), at workshop.** Council could choose to take final review of major site plans from the Planning Commission, including residential projects of 5+ units ([[Workshop Brief 2026-09-24]]).
  - **LMC Review Commission (draft resolution, still in draft per César, 2026-09-24).** An 11-to-15-member advisory commission, appointed by the Council President with Council confirmation, to review the whole LMC over 24 months. Its scope includes barriers to housing diversity and the "predictability, effectiveness, and equity" of approval processes ([[LMC Review Commission -- Draft Resolution Analysis -- 2026-04-12]]). A companion proposal by Council Member Taylor would:
    - hire a Legislative Project Manager reporting to the Council President
    - spend about $1.39 million over three years
    - impose a 270-day moratorium on new annexations, master plans, preliminary plats, and private text amendments while the final code is before Council

    ([[LMC Modernization -- Libby Taylor Proposal Analysis -- 2026-04-12]])
- **State law in 2026.** The Starter and Silver Homes Act (HB 239 / SB 36), Middle Housing Options (HB 778), and the Generational Housing Act ADU mandate (HB 1538) all died. R4 and R6 stay single-family-only unless the City changes them. The Housing Certainty Act (HB 548 / SB 325) passed. It vests complete applications for five years, so later LMC changes don't reach applications already on file ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]).
- **Frederick's planning-stage zoning work** is tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]]. Parking minimums are covered in [[Parking Reform]].

## Supply evidence

- **Permissiveness and cost.** The New York Times' May 2026 interactive map of housing costs relative to area median income shows lower costs in cities that permit more construction. It joins a larger body of research on the same relationship ([[NYT Housing Cost Map -- Supply Permissiveness Predicts Affordability]]).
- **Million-dollar starter homes.** Zillow counts a record 242 U.S. cities with starter homes at $1 million or more; 105 are in California. The national starter-home median is just under $200,000. Sun Belt markets that added supply saw price growth moderate, while the more restrictive Northeast didn't ([[Starter Homes Over $1M in 242 Cities -- Supply Restriction Is the Variable]]).
- **National production.** May 2026 housing starts were 8.5% below the prior year, the lowest since April 2020. Builders blamed material and financing costs. The declines were steepest in the South (−15%) and West (−11%), where much of the recent deregulation happened. Annual completions were down 14.2% ([[Housing Starts at Pandemic-Era Low -- Supply Shock Deepening]]).
- **Upzoning producing projects.** San Francisco approved its first project under a new upzoning law: an eight-story mixed-income condo building at 230 Anza Street, replacing a two-story building. The Planning Commission approved it over neighborhood opposition ([[San Francisco Upzoning Produces First Approved Tower]]). Asheville legalized duplexes in residential zones, ending single-family-only zoning citywide ([[Asheville Ends Single-Family Exclusionary Zoning]]).
- **Near-campus supply.** Rents near NC State rose 30.5% and near UNC Chapel Hill 23.3% since 2020. When campus housing falls short, students and local renters compete for the same units ([[Student Housing Belongs Near Campus]]).
- **The suburban frontier is closing.** NAHB summarizes research by economists Edward Glaeser and Joseph Gyourko. Using Census tract data from the 1970s to the 2010s, they find that the link between rising prices and new construction has weakened or reversed in many metros. Supply growth slowed most in low-density suburbs of higher-priced markets, including Sun Belt metros like Dallas, Atlanta, and Phoenix. They attribute this to stricter zoning and longer permitting, and find that regulatory barriers tend to rise as incomes rise ([[Housing Digest 02-14-26]]).

## Reform tools

- **The basic menu.** Donald Elliott argues that zoning is a primary driver of housing cost. The one direct capture from his April 2026 Planetizen piece: larger minimum lot sizes are among the provisions studies link to higher prices. The clipping summarizes his broader framework as:
  - smaller minimum lots
  - missing-middle types by right
  - lower parking minimums
  - less discretionary review for conforming projects
  - density bonuses rather than payment-in-lieu

  ([[Housing -- Five Simple Zoning Changes for Affordability]])

  His book *An Even Better Way to Zone* adds administrative flexibility: letting staff approve small, justified deviations from setbacks or parking on constrained infill sites without a full variance ([[An Even Better Way to Zone Book Reference Donald Elliott]]).
- **Housing in commercial zones.** In many places housing is still illegal on commercially zoned land, including aging strip-mall parcels that already have roads, water, and sewer. North Carolina's SB 499 (2025) would have allowed housing by right there. It didn't pass, and advocates pushed its provisions for 2026 ([[Housing by Right in Commercial Zones -- NC SB499 and Corridor Revitalization Model]]).
- **Faith-owned land.** Virginia's HB 1279 made it the third state, after California and Florida, with a "Yes in God's Backyard" law. It allows housing on land owned by religious institutions and nonprofits without rezoning, effective January 1, 2027, with a sunset on January 1, 2031 unless renewed. In Northern Virginia, 65% of faith-owned properties are zoned against multifamily housing. Preservation Maryland counts more than 37,000 acres of faith-owned land in Maryland ([[Housing -- Virginia YIGBY Law Passes, Faith-Based Housing By Right]]). Site studies show 5 units fitting on a 0.15-acre lot and 11 on 0.31 acres around a shared courtyard ([[Housing -- Faith-Owned Land as Infill Housing Opportunity]]).
- **Surplus schools.** School-to-housing conversion is the fastest-growing kind of adaptive reuse. Nearly 2,000 apartments were completed in former schools in 2024, four times the prior year, and 74 projects are underway. Atlanta is converting eight former school sites, with priority for teachers ([[Housing -- School-to-Housing Adaptive Reuse]]).
- **Office-to-housing conversions.** The American Planning Association notes that nearly 20% of office buildings sit empty, and only about 25% are suitable for conversion, depending on location, floor plate, and walkability. Suburban examples include Amber Union in Falcon Heights, Minnesota (125 attainable units) and Row on Merchant outside Cincinnati. Tax credits, subsidies, and preservation incentives help offset renovation costs, alongside zoning adaptations, lower parking requirements, and faster permitting ([[Housing Digest 02-14-26]]).
- **Density that fits.** A CNU Charter Award-winning Cape Cod study by Union Studio Architecture was a $450,000 state-funded project. It showed that traditional missing-middle types can reach up to 20 units per acre at the 1.5–2.5 stories residents prefer. It used community surveys on height, documented historical building patterns, and built demonstration projects ([[Housing Digest 02-14-26]]).
- **Household definitions.** Many codes define "family" to limit how many unrelated people can share a home. Architect Alli Thurmond Quinlan argues these rules are legally weak and practically unenforceable. When she wrote Fayetteville, Arkansas's ADU code, residents asked to keep the definition even though the city attorney said it couldn't be enforced. She recommends regulating noise, maintenance, parking, and infrastructure-based density instead ([[What Zoning Codes Get Wrong About Families]]).
- **Inclusionary zoning design.** DC's IZ program has produced fewer than 300 affordable units a year over 17 years. The DC Policy Center describes a cycle in which set-aside costs thin margins and push production toward fewer, smaller, higher-end units. It proposes three fixes:
  - align IZ rents with LIHTC rents
  - let affordability float between 30% and 80% of median income while averaging 60% or less
  - suspend IZ automatically when multifamily permits fall below 625 a quarter for two quarters

  ([[Inclusionary Zoning Underperforms Without Structural Design -- DC Policy Center Analysis]])
- **Funded inclusionary zoning: Baltimore.** Baltimore's program (Ordinance 24-308, operational July 21, 2024) replaced a 2007 program that produced only 34 units in 17 years because of widespread developer waivers.
  - **Who it covers:** market-rate multifamily projects of 20 or more units that receive City financial help or a significant land-use approval.
  - **The requirement:** 10% affordable units for 30 years, half at or below 50% AMI and half at or below 60% AMI.
  - **The funding:** the City gives an annual property tax credit equal to the rent forgone on each affordable unit, so the credit adjusts to the market.
  - Sightline counts it among only three genuinely funded inclusionary programs in the US, with Portland and Shoreline, Washington.

  ([[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]])

## Land value, displacement, and design responses

- **The land-price critique.** Patrick Condon argues that upzoning raises land prices about as much as it raises allowable density, canceling the affordability gain. His case is Vancouver: its housing stock tripled since 1960, yet by his account it has the world's third-highest prices. Michael Lewyn responds with three points:
  - The proportional land-price assumption looks arbitrary.
  - Land costs also rose sharply in cities that didn't upzone.
  - Condon ignores whether Vancouver's suburbs kept pace.

  ([[Condon's Land Price Capture Critique of Upzoning]])
- **Displacement risk.** In Denver's lower-income, majority-Latino west-side neighborhoods, upzoning led to teardowns replaced by large, expensive homes. The Urban Displacement Project counts more than 50,000 low-income Denver households in areas at risk of gentrification, and the city paused rezoning in affected neighborhoods until affordability tools are in place ([[Upzoning's Gentrification Paradox -- When More Housing Displaces the People It Should Help]]).
- **Size-for-units zoning.** Denver's Unlocking Housing Choices plan caps the size of a single unit but allows more total floor area for more, smaller units. Since 2019, Denver infill has mostly replaced $300,000–700,000 homes with $3–4 million ones, and the cap is meant to make missing middle pencil out against that. Denver's roughly 264-day permitting for larger projects may eat the bonus ([[Zoning Math as Gentrification Intervention -- Denver Unlocking Housing Choices]]).
- **A test for neighborhood change.** Strong Towns proposes two rules. Change should improve conditions for current residents rather than replace them, and it should build a durable tax base rather than depend on growth to service infrastructure debt ([[Two Rules for Neighborhood Change That Serves Everyone]]).
- **Social housing and supply.** New York's Creedmoor senior-housing project was delayed for years and cut by more than 800 units to satisfy local land-use vetoes. Jacobin's argument: publicly financed housing can't scale where zoning blocks construction, so social-housing advocates also need land-use reform ([[Social Housing Requires Zoning Reform to Scale]]).

## Environmental case

- **Density and emissions.** A new high-rise multifamily unit is 82% more energy-efficient than a new single-family home, partly because single-family homes average 2.5 times the size. Compact development also produces less runoff and slows sprawl into rural land. The CityBuild piece notes that the Sierra Club formally recognizes density as environmentalism ([[Density Is Environmentalism]]).
- **Housing reform as climate policy.** The Sightline Institute cites an RMI estimate: if every state legalized more compact, walkable housing, the carbon reduction by 2033 would equal half of all states adopting 100% EV-sales mandates ([[Housing Production as Climate Action -- The Sightline Case]]).

## Fiscal case for infill

- **Costs scale with land, not people.** Urban3's Joe Minicozzi argues that stormwater, road, and utility costs track the land a development consumes. Dense downtowns generate the most tax value per acre and end up subsidizing lower-yield outlying growth that no council explicitly voted to fund. His example is a city that spent $180 million more on stormwater than it needed to ([[Urban3 Tiny Buildings Infrastructure Cost Per Land Area Providence Rhode Island Case]]).
- **Lane-miles and yield.** Chattanooga (about 180,000 people) maintains about 2,500 lane-miles; Jersey City (about 250,000) maintains about 250. A downtown acre can yield around $2 million in tax value against about $40,000 for a suburban acre, with the same per-foot road costs. The same piece treats parking mandates and sprawl as costs developers don't pay. It suggests pricing those costs or fast-tracking preferred building types, and warns that any single target metric, including a production target, can distort behavior ([[Goodhart's Law and Externalities in Urban Policy]]).
- **Maintenance and aid dependence.** Strong Towns describes Fairhaven, Massachusetts, which draws 40% of its revenue from state and federal aid while carrying suburban-era maintenance obligations. It recommends adding housing on existing infrastructure ("thickening") and shrinking the maintenance footprint through narrower lanes, less asphalt, and rain gardens ([[Make Caring Cool Again -- The Strong Towns Case for Maintenance Over Novelty]]).
- **Frederick framing.** César's note frames infill as revenue on streets, pipes, and service areas the City already maintains, and greenfield growth as the opposite. It's an argument for production targets aimed at colleagues who don't respond to the affordability case ([[Densification as Fiscal Strategy -- Housing Production as Revenue Without New Infrastructure]]).

## Origins and system-level planning

- **Exclusion from the start.** New York's 1916 zoning resolution separated noxious uses from other uses. It also kept the immigrant garment workers whose factories were encroaching on Fifth Avenue out of wealthier districts. Ryan Puzycki argues that the shift from "incompatible uses" to "incompatible people" came almost immediately ([[Zoning Was Originally a Tool of Exclusion Not Just Land Use Management]]).
- **Parcel-level deferral.** As zoning grew more granular, city-scale tradeoffs moved into parcel-by-parcel hearings. Puzycki's example is an Austin rezoning for an autonomous-vehicle depot, where the question he poses is "if not here, where?" Necessary but unwanted uses end up wherever resistance is weakest, often lower-income neighborhoods ([[Cities Need Space for Unglamorous Infrastructure]]). César's synthesis draws the same pattern across zoning, research, and community engagement: institutions with power define the problem for communities that weren't included ([[Institutions Plan for Communities Without Them -- A Pattern Across Housing Research and Zoning]]).
