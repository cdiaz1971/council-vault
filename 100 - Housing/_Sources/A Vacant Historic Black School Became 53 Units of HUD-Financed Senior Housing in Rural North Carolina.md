# A Vacant Historic Black School Became 53 Units of HUD-Financed Senior Housing in Rural North Carolina

## Summary

DreamKey Partners converted the vacant, historic Caswell County Training School/Dillard School -- the county's first Black 11-grade school, closed since 2002 -- into 53 income-based senior apartments using HUD Section 202 funding layered with state and federal historic tax credits, addressing a county where 42% of renters are cost-burdened and few senior housing options exist.

## Key Points

- The Dillard School Apartments in Yanceyville, Caswell County, NC opened last year (leasing began 2025) in a building originally built in the early 1950s as the Caswell County Training School, the county's first school offering 11 grades to Black students. It was renamed for longtime principal Nicholas Longworth Dillard after desegregation, closed in 2002, and was placed on the National Register of Historic Places -- then sat vacant over 20 years, with broken windows and a partially gone gym roof, before conversion.
- The project delivered 53 studio and one-bedroom apartments for seniors, with rent based on income (residents pay up to 30% of income; the remainder is covered by federal housing funding). It is not assisted living -- residents must live independently -- but the building includes elevators, accessibility features, and community spaces.
- Financing layered HUD's Section 202 program (for seniors) with state and federal historic tax credits. DreamKey Partners representative Alex Walker: "Through layering all those financing pieces, we were able to, especially with the significant grant from HUD, put this project together to restore the building and make it possible for residents to live here."
- Local need: Caswell County Training School/Dillard Property Manager Charity James said the community has few affordable complexes and even fewer for seniors; when leasing opened, more applicants applied than units were available, producing long waitlists. In Caswell County, about 42% of renters and 20% of homeowners are cost-burdened (spending over 30% of gross income on housing).
- Statewide context: North Carolina has an estimated shortage of 215,000 affordable rental homes for extremely low-income residents (NLIHC); of the state's 348,000 renter households in that income category, 29% are headed by seniors. Statewide median monthly rent is about $1,338 (NC Housing Finance Agency) and has been rising.
- The project won a 2026 national award from the Affordable Housing Tax Credit Coalition in its rural housing category. Coalition CEO Emily Cadik cited federal tax credits like the low-income housing tax credit as increasingly important given rising cost-of-living pressure on families.
- Resident Cassandra Henderson, a 1968 graduate of the school, returned to Yanceyville and moved into the building in 2025 after being unable to find housing there to move back near family: "My sister said, 'Why don't you move back home?' I said, 'There's nowhere for me to move.'"

## Relevance

A concrete adaptive-reuse model for pairing historic preservation with senior-specific affordable production, relevant to [[Housing Production Strategies]]'s public-land/public-asset production channel and to any future Frederick discussion of reusing a vacant or underutilized public building (school, civic, or historic structure) for income-restricted senior housing. The financing structure -- HUD Section 202 layered with state and federal historic tax credits -- is a specific, named mechanism worth flagging if Frederick identifies a comparable vacant historic property, and the waitlist/undersupply pattern documented here (few senior-specific units, applicants exceeding availability) mirrors the kind of demand signal that would support a similar project locally.
