# Zoning Was Originally a Tool of Exclusion Not Just Land Use Management

## Summary

Zoning's origin story is more complicated than the neutral land-use management narrative suggests. From its earliest codification in New York in 1916, zoning was used simultaneously to separate genuinely incompatible uses and to keep immigrant workers away from wealthier commercial and residential districts. The leap from "incompatible uses" to "incompatible people" happened almost immediately, and it set the template for single-family exclusion that persists today.

## Key Points

Ryan Puzycki traces the political history of zoning through what is typically presented as a planning success story. New York's 1916 zoning resolution is often cited as the origin of American zoning, framed as a rational response to the chaos of industrial-era cities. Puzycki notes that it was also, from the start, a response to garment factories encroaching on Fifth Avenue's retail palaces -- and an instrument for keeping the immigrant workers who staffed those factories out of more genteel neighborhoods.

The jump from "incompatible uses" to "incompatible people" happened almost immediately. Once zoning proved useful for separating undesirable people as well as noxious industries, the logic was hard to reverse. Zoning became progressively more granular -- from broad industrial/commercial/residential zones to ever-finer subcategories -- until the parcel became the effective unit of planning. At that scale, city-wide tradeoffs cannot be made. They are deferred indefinitely, reappearing as housing shortages, infrastructure crises, and equity failures.

The "sanctuary suburb" mindset Puzycki identifies -- wanting the benefits of urban proximity without any of its costs -- is the direct descendant of this exclusionary tradition. It operates through the same instruments: rezoning fights, conditional use hearings, and NIMBY organizing that uses the planning process as a shield.

## Relevance

This is essential context for Frederick's LMC review and any zoning reform argument César is making. The standard defense of restrictive zoning is that it protects neighborhoods from incompatible uses. Puzycki's history shows that "incompatible uses" has always been a proxy for "incompatible people," and that the granularity of the current system is what prevents city-scale planning from happening at all.

It also reframes the YIMBY/NIMBY debate. The NIMBYs aren't just being parochial -- they are operating within a system that was designed, from the beginning, to let them win those fights. Reforming zoning isn't just a technical adjustment; it's a structural challenge to a deliberately exclusionary framework.
