# Mercatus State-Level Housing Reform Menu 2026 -- ADUs Vested Rights Duplex Legalization

## Summary

A Mercatus Center brief catalogs proven state-level housing reform tools for 2026 legislative sessions: ADU streamlining, commercial-to-residential conversion, duplex legalization (with HOA statute fix), vested rights protection, and zoning noncompliance remedies. All are relevant to Maryland's Annapolis session.

## Key Points

Published September 2025, aimed at 2026 state legislative sessions. Organized into four categories: reversing regulatory overreach, streamlining procedures, improving legal frameworks, and updating construction standards.

**ADUs:** 18 states have passed ADU permitting laws. California's experience shows that legal authority alone is insufficient -- it took a series of later laws eliminating excessive fees, owner-occupancy requirements, and parking minimums to activate the market. Iowa and Oregon learned from California and passed more complete stand-alone bills. AARP has published a model ADU law.

**Commercial-to-residential conversion:** Post-pandemic office vacancy creates a natural opening. States can pass legislation allowing residential uses on commercially-zoned sites, removing a barrier that keeps vacant office and retail from being repurposed as housing.

**Duplex legalization:** Nashville is the standout case. Tennessee's HOA statute enables simple two-unit HOAs, unlocking conventional mortgage financing for duplex construction. Cities that have legalized duplexes in theory but not practice often have HOA statute problems that prevent financing from flowing to small-scale projects. The legal authorization and the financing mechanism must both be present.

**Vested rights:** Tennessee enacted legislation in 2025 securing a developer's entitlement to current zoning at the time of development application. This removes the political risk that localities change zoning requirements after permits are arranged and financing is committed -- a pattern that prices risk into financing terms. Many states still allow localities to pull the rug after permits are issued.

**Zoning noncompliance:** Boston's audit found less than 1 percent of residential buildings citywide complied with its own zoning code. When existing buildings are mostly noncompliant, the code becomes a barrier to redeveloping vacant sites. States can address this by invalidating restrictions on blocks where at least a quarter of existing buildings do not comply.

## Relevance

Maryland's 2026 legislative session is the relevant action horizon. Frederick's council and its Annapolis allies should know which of these tools Maryland has not yet adopted. ADU streamlining, vested rights protection, and commercial-to-residential conversion are all mechanisms that could ease Frederick's housing production pipeline without requiring the city to fight state law constraints alone.

The vested rights question is directly relevant to any development agreements Frederick enters with HACF or private developers on Lucas Village. If Maryland law does not currently provide vested rights protection, developers face political risk that is priced into their financing -- and ultimately into what they build and at what cost.
