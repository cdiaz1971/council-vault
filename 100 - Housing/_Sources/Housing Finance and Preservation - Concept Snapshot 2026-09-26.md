# Housing Finance and Preservation

## Frederick

- **The City Housing Fund.** Chapter 19 (§19-18) authorizes the fund for a broad list of uses:
  - MPDU operations
  - supportive housing
  - homebuyer and foreclosure counseling
  - emergency mortgage loans
  - financing LIHTC and other affordable projects

  Its only revenue sources are the MPDU payment-in-lieu fee ($2.00 per sq ft) and MPDU foreclosure proceeds ([[Frederick Housing Policy Baseline]]). The fund holds roughly $4.3 million. Payment-in-lieu yields about $2,000 per unit, against an affordability gap of roughly $60,000–80,000 per unit at 80% AMI over a 40-year control period. MPDU in-lieu revenue is projected to reach only $1.83 million by 2030 ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]). The proposed rental licensing fee cut would also affect fund revenue ([[Tenant Protections]]).
- **Council funding items in the vault roadmap** (not in the Notion roadmap database; [[Legislative Roadmap FY2027]]):
  - **Housing Accelerator Fund, $250,000 CIP** (Whole Council, Pre-Legislative). Intended to fund projects identified through the Housing RFI; a transfer of MPDU fund dollars is to be discussed.
  - **Housing Funding Mechanism** (Government Operations, Pre-Legislative). Aimed at funding housing at deeper affordability through an ongoing investment mechanism.
- **Presented to Government Operations, September 14, 2026 (workshop, no votes)** ([[Government Operations Committee Brief 2026-09-14]]):
  - **A $15 million Frederick Housing Accelerator Fund (HAF).** Proposed by Ryan Trout, Rockville's Director of Housing and a Frederick resident, in a September 6 memo and a September 14 presentation ([[A.1 Frederick Housing Fund Memo]]; [[A.2 Frederick Housing Revolving Loan Fund]]; [[Government Operations Committee Brief 2026-09-14]]). A general obligation bond, explored for FY2028, would capitalize a revolving public lender for mixed-income rental housing. It's modeled on Montgomery County's Housing Opportunities Commission and Invest Chattanooga.
    - **Mechanism.** Subordinate construction loans would cover 15–30% of project cost at roughly 5–6%, replacing private mezzanine debt or preferred equity at 12–20%, in exchange for a binding affordability set-aside. Projects refinance in 3–6 years, so the capital turns over 3–4 times during the 20-year bond. After the bond is retired, repayments and interest stay in the fund.
    - **Worked example (Trout's).** A $60 million, 100-unit project is financed with $42 million in senior debt (70%), a $12 million HAF loan (20%), and $6 million in developer equity (10%).
      - That $12 million costs about $2.4 million a year at 20% and about $720,000 at 6%, a saving of about $1.68 million. Trout calls it a conservative blended estimate.
      - Setting aside 40 units (20 at or below 30% AMI and 20 at or below 50% AMI) forgoes about $276,000 a year in rent. The savings cover that about 6.1 times, leaving about $1.4 million a year for the developer.
      - Putting all 40 units at or below 30% AMI still fits, at about 3.9 times coverage.
      - The memo notes this isn't a full pro forma: it doesn't model senior debt sizing, operating costs, vacancy, or developer returns.
    - **Cost.** Estimated debt service is $1.0–1.2 million a year over 20 years, under 0.7% of the FY2027 general fund. About $900,000 a year in loan interest would bring the net general fund cost to $100,000–300,000.
    - **Governance.** The fund would be an independent nonprofit affiliate of the Housing Authority of the City of Frederick (HACF) with its own board and lending guidelines. It would have audited financials, open board meetings, regular reports to Council, and public tracking of units, affordability levels, private money leveraged, repayment, and defaults.
    - **Timeline.**
      1. Council resolution of intent (late 2026)
      2. enabling legislation (FY2027)
      3. bond issued and lending opens (late 2027)
      4. first loans close (2028)
      5. evaluation and a larger second round of funding with Frederick County and philanthropy (5–7 years out)
    - **Trout's case for it:**
      - Frederick County needs 31,853 new units by 2035, about 14,000 of them in the City, and more than 10,000 of the County's units must be below 60% AMI. Production is running at less than half that pace, and County planners warn the pipeline could dry up by 2032.
      - LIHTC uses DC-area income limits, so its rents sit close to market in Frederick.
      - Citing an Associated Press report (September 7, 2026): about 4 million affordable units exist for 11 million extremely-low-income renter households nationally, and only 12% of new LIHTC units in 2024 were affordable at 30% AMI.
    - **Distinct from the CIP line.** The brief notes the HAF differs from the $250,000 CIP item.
  - **Charter amendment on City property (Nash).** It would lengthen the public notice for disposing of City property from 20 to 30 days and add a 30-day right of first refusal for nonprofits serving City residents to buy at a "market-competitive price." It would also explicitly authorize eminent domain for blighted or underused property.
  - **Next step:** following the workshop, Nash is going to write a draft policy (per César, 2026-09-25).
- **City land.** "RFPs for City Land," a process to issue RFPs that accelerate affordable housing on City land, is in Committee Review at Government Operations (Notion roadmap, checked 2026-09-24).
- **Income limits.** Frederick's AMI is based on the HUD Washington MSA, which pools Frederick with DC and Northern Virginia. FY2026 limits at 50% AMI are $58,150 for one person, $66,450 for two, and $83,050 for four. The uncapped 80% limit for four is $132,900 ([Frederick County Division of Housing, FY 2026, effective July 1, 2026](https://www.frederickcountymd.gov/DocumentCenter/View/256909/Income-Limits-Table---All-Programs)).
- **A LIHTC project in the pipeline.** Conifer plans 145 apartments with community buildings and commercial space on Himes Avenue next to the Prospect Center, around 2028, depending on a LIHTC award. It committed to a traffic study and to outreach to the NAC and Golden Mile Alliance ([[Himes Ave -- Conifer LIHTC Development Meeting Notes (2026-03-16)]]). Its parking reduction from 1.5 to 1.25 spaces per unit was granted ([[Parking Reform]]).
- **Planning-stage finance and preservation items** are tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].

## Why the gap is structural

- **The math.** A worked model for a three-bedroom unit at 50% AMI shows costs exceeding what affordable rents support, even with LIHTC and a property tax exemption. The gap persists with zero developer profit and with free land. It's driven by construction costs, financing costs, and the rent ceiling ([[Affordable Housing Cannot Pay for Itself The Math Behind the Gap]]).
- **Rising operating costs.** Insurance, debt service, utilities, and maintenance are rising faster than covenant-capped rents and subsidies. The results are deferred maintenance, cut services, refinancing trouble, and some owners letting covenants expire ([[Affordable Housing Finance Under Structural Stress -- Insurance Rates and Operating Costs]]; [[Affordable Housing Finance Model Under Structural Stress]]).
- **Fragmentation.** The Terner Center finds each additional funding source adds about four months and $20,460 per unit to development. Colorado's per-unit costs rose from about $325,000 to nearly $500,000 between 2021 and 2025, and it's building a statewide common application ([[Housing Finance -- Fragmentation Costs and Common Application Reform]]).

## Financing tools

- **LIHTC and tax-exempt bonds.** César's primer:
  - In a conduit program, a government issues tax-exempt bonds for a private developer. The developer borrows at lower rates, repays from project revenue, and bears the default risk. The debt stays off the issuer's balance sheet.
  - Bond financing triggers the non-competitive 4% LIHTC, which yields about 30% of project equity, against about 70% for the competitive 9% credit. It requires 20% of units at 50% AMI or 40% at 60% AMI.
  - The primer reports that 2025 federal legislation lowered the bond-financing threshold for the full 4% credit from 50% to 25% for bonds issued after 2025.
  - The limits: finite state volume cap (Maryland DHCD), complexity, reputational risk, and a remaining gap that usually needs local funds.

  ([[Conduit Bond Financing for Affordable Housing Primer]])
- **Bank equity.** The ROAD to Housing Act (§203) raises banks' public-welfare investment cap from 15% to 20% of risk-adjusted capital, adding demand for LIHTC and New Markets credits. It isn't self-executing: the OCC and Fed must issue guidance, and some states must amend banking statutes ([[PWI Cap Increase to 20 Percent Unlocks Bank Equity for LIHTC and NMTC]]).
- **Production funds.** Montgomery County's Housing Opportunities Commission capitalized a $100 million fund with an HOC bond and a matching county appropriation.
  - It takes equity positions in market-rate deals at about 5%, instead of the 15–20% private equity expects.
  - It's projected to produce 6,000 units over 20 years, 1,800 of them permanently affordable, with interest flowing back to the county.
  - A companion $14 million Affordable Housing Opportunity Fund, matched 3-to-1 by private lenders, buys at-risk properties quickly.

  ([[Montgomery County Housing Production Fund as State Model]])
- **Revolving funds.** Marks and Coale propose a $250 million Maryland fund administered by DHCD. It would make subordinate below-market loans covering 15–30% of a capital stack and recycle principal. They argue for mixed-income eligibility with a required affordability set-aside rather than deep-affordability-only terms ([[Maryland Statewide Housing Revolving Fund Proposal]]). The track record elsewhere is start modest, then return for more ([[Revolving Fund Infrastructure Compounds Over Time]]):
  - Austin: $250 million in 2018, then $350 million.
  - Columbus: $50 million in 2019, $200 million in 2022, and $500 million in 2025, after helping create or preserve 7,000 homes.
  - New York's $100 million fund drew $115 million in private capital.
- **Peer revolving lenders.** As cited by Trout ([[A.1 Frederick Housing Fund Memo]]):
  - **Invest Chattanooga:** $20 million city seed money (2022), about 20:1 leverage over 20 years, 30% of units permanently affordable, first loans closed in 2025.
  - **MassHousing's Momentum Fund:** $50 million from the state (2024), at least 20% of units at or below 80% AMI, about 500 homes financed since 2025.
  - **Montgomery County's HOC:** about 8× leverage on $100 million in bonds (2021–22).
- **Federal Home Loan Banks.** The eleven regional FHLBs' targeted programs supported nearly $8 billion in loans in 2024, comparable to or larger than CDBG. FHLB New York's 0% Development Advance added housing in 2025 ([[Federal Home Loan Banks as Underutilized Housing Finance Tool]]).
- **Pension capital.** Maryland cut Rental Housing Works, its main gap-financing program, by $20 million. Tom Coale proposes letting state and local pension trustees invest in affordable housing, as New York City's funds do. Maryland's State Retirement and Pension System manages about $67 billion ([[Pension Funds as Affordable Housing Capital -- The Maryland Gap]]).
- **Multi-state authorities.** Shelterforce proposes joint state housing authorities to pool bonding and administration as a hedge against reduced HUD support ([[State Housing Joint Authorities as HUD Hedge]]).

## LIHTC's limits and expiring affordability

- **Rents tied to AMI, not tenants.** LIHTC costs about $13.5 billion a year. Its rents are set against 50–60% AMI, but nearly half of tenants earn at or below 30% AMI, so about 37.5% are rent-burdened.
  - When AMI spikes, as in 2022, rents can jump by double digits.
  - Eleven state agencies cap increases, from 2% to 10%. Federal rules don't.
  - In California, only 15% of 2011–2023 LIHTC units were affordable to extremely low-income households.

  ([[LIHTC -- Structural Tension Between Tax Credit Program and Tenant Housing Security]])
- **The expiration wave.** More than 440,000 federally assisted homes reach subsidy or restriction end dates between 2026 and 2030, including about 329,000 LIHTC units. Each is a refinancing decision point, not an automatic loss. Project-based rental assistance contracts usually renew ([[440,000 Federally Assisted Homes Face Affordability Restriction Expirations by 2030]]).
- **Early exits.** The "Qualified Contract" process lets some LIHTC owners exit restrictions early if the state agency can't find a buyer within a year at a formula price. Advocates say that price almost always exceeds the property's value as affordable housing, which prices out preservation buyers ([[Qualified Contract Exits Let LIHTC Owners Escape Affordability Restrictions Early]]).

## Preservation

- **Losing the existing stock.** Units renting below $1,400 fell by 9.3 million between 2014 and 2024, while units above $1,400 grew by 11.8 million. LIHTC fits new construction and full rehabilitation, not ongoing capital needs. The National Housing Trust proposes a "shallow subsidy": low-cost capital in exchange for maintaining properties and keeping residents stable ([[Housing Preservation Gap in the Supply-Plus Agenda]]).
- **Durability.** In older cities, most affordable housing already exists. Repairs done in the wrong order, such as energy work before structural stabilization, can speed up deterioration. Shared "archetype" guidance would help CDCs, lenders, and contractors sequence repairs ([[Housing Preservation -- Durability as Affordability Policy]]).
- **Buying unsubsidized affordable housing (NOAH).** Mecklenburg County lost more than 77% of its low-cost housing from 2015 to 2024. Charlotte's business-backed Housing Impact Fund responded:
  - It raised $225 million in impact capital at returns as low as 6%.
  - It has bought nearly 2,000 units, with rents capped at 80% AMI and a third at or below 30% AMI, for 20 years.
  - The city and county rebate each property's taxes into the fund for its lowest-income units.

  ([[Private Sector Housing Impact Fund Preserves Affordable Housing in Charlotte]])
- **Retrofits.** Maryland's utility geothermal pilot under the WARMTH Act stalled after the Public Service Commission rejected the proposals as too costly. Del. Lorig Charkoudian noted that retrofitting naturally occurring affordable housing is the hardest version and the equitable one, while new construction tends to serve higher-income communities ([[Retrofitting Affordable Housing for Geothermal Is the Hardest and Most Necessary Problem]]).
- **Resident services pay.** Across 248 properties, service-enriched affordable housing earned 26% higher net operating income. Each $100 invested returned $259 in added NOI, rent arrears were 24% lower, and eviction move-outs ran 1.2 per 100 households ([[Resident Services Generate 26 Percent Higher Net Operating Income in Affordable Housing Properties]]).
