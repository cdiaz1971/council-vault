# Housing Preservation Gap in the Supply-Plus Agenda

## Summary

National Housing Trust CEO argues the supply-side housing agenda is missing a preservation component: 9.3M low-rent units lost 2014-2024, operating costs for affordable properties outpacing rental income, and no systemic funding mechanism for maintenance.

## Key Points

**The scale of loss:** Units renting below $1,400/month declined by 9.3 million between 2014 and 2024 (Harvard JCHS data). Units above $1,400 grew by 11.8 million over the same period. This is not just market filtering -- it is net loss of the affordable stock at the low end.

**Why affordable housing operators can't keep up:**
- Rents have risen but rental income for income-restricted properties is capped by design. Owners cannot pass rising costs to residents without violating affordability covenants.
- Operating costs (utilities, insurance, labor) have seen double-digit increases.
- The result: owners drawing on reserves to cover mortgages with nothing left for repairs. Leaky roofs, pest infestations, and deferred maintenance accumulate until the building is beyond saving.

**Why existing tools don't fill the gap:**
- LIHTC is a deep subsidy best suited to new construction or comprehensive renovation -- not ongoing maintenance and regular-replacement capital needs.
- Private preservation funds focus on middle-income housing because it generates sufficient returns. Housing affordable to low-wage workers does not.

**The proposed fix -- a "shallow subsidy":** Low-cost capital to affordable housing owners in exchange for maintaining properties in good condition and providing resident stability. Deep subsidies reserved for new or currently-offline units. Preserving existing affordable stock so new supply is truly additive, not offsetting.

**The argument:** Zoning and permitting reform alone is not a complete housing strategy. Filtering -- the process by which market-rate housing trickles down to lower-income households -- takes decades. In the meantime, the housing most affordable today is disappearing.

## Relevance

Directly relevant to Frederick's housing production agenda and the Housing Production Target Resolution. The 1,400 units/year target addresses the supply gap but does not account for losses in the existing affordable stock. If Frederick is losing affordable units to deterioration, conversion, or market pressure while building new ones, the net gain is smaller than the headline number suggests.

The shallow subsidy argument is also relevant to the Rental Licensing Ordinance debate: the fee rate discussion is downstream of whether affordable housing operators in Frederick can afford to maintain properties at all. The proposed fee cut from $240 to $150 needs to be weighed against what the Housing Fund is actually being asked to support.

The LIHTC limitation noted here connects to the Frederick AMI and LIHTC discussion and the Lucas Village redevelopment -- LIHTC is powerful but narrow, and the city's reliance on it as the primary tool leaves preservation funding structurally unaddressed.
