# Federal Housing Bills ROAD Act and H21 -- Incentive Structures and Their Weaknesses

## Summary

The ROAD Act (Senate) and H21 (House) both use incentive grants to nudge localities toward permissive zoning, but the Urban Institute finds the mechanisms too weak -- median bonus of $55K/year -- and recommends conditioning federal transportation aid on housing production as the stronger lever.

## Key Points

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act (Senate) and the Housing for the 21st Century Act (House) are the clearest federal signal in years that local land-use policy is a national problem. Both recognize that restrictive local zoning is a primary driver of the housing shortage. Both use similar approaches: incentive grants to localities that improve housing supply growth, planning assistance grants, and HUD guidance to support production across income levels. H21 goes further by directing HUD to create guidelines for single-stair apartment buildings, which reduces floor area dedicated to internal circulation and cuts per-unit construction costs.

The Urban Institute identifies two structural weaknesses. First, the CDBG-linked bonus is too small: the median community receiving a bonus would get roughly $55,000 per year -- not enough to fund a single staff position or build a single housing unit in most markets. That is not a credible incentive against local political resistance to upzoning. Second, both bills direct HUD to implement significant new mandates while the agency is undergoing major staffing cuts. Without reversing those cuts, the legislative mandates are largely unfunded.

The more powerful lever the Urban Institute recommends: conditioning a share of federal transportation assistance on housing supply growth. Transportation funding is orders of magnitude larger than CDBG and touches nearly every community. Cities and counties with housing shortages that are also pursuing transit investments would face direct financial consequences for exclusionary zoning.

## Relevance

Frederick receives federal categorical grants including CDBG and HOME funds. If either bill passes, Frederick's development trajectory -- including Lucas Village and HACF projects -- will need to demonstrate housing supply growth to remain competitive for federal bonuses. That tracking should begin now, before any bill passes, so Frederick can document its production trajectory.

More consequentially: if Congress eventually conditions transportation aid on housing production, Frederick's Route 15 corridor and any regional transit planning would become linked to zoning decisions. That is an argument for proactive LMC reform and the Housing Production Target Resolution -- getting ahead of the federal requirement rather than being forced into it.

The Urban Institute also cites Seattle's social housing program as a model for direct public investment in new housing supply -- worth tracking for potential HACF-level application.
