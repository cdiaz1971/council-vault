# Pension Funds as Affordable Housing Capital -- The Maryland Gap

## Summary

Maryland's 2026 legislative session addressed housing supply through regulatory tools but failed to solve the underlying capital gap. The state cut Rental Housing Works -- its primary gap financing program -- by $20 million, while neighboring states are deploying housing trust funds worth hundreds of millions. An op-ed by housing attorney Tom Coale proposes enabling legislation for Maryland pension trustees to direct state and local pension assets into affordable housing, citing NYC Comptroller Brad Lander's model as evidence that the investment case is sound.

## Key Points

The core diagnosis: housing in Maryland fails to pencil because construction costs are high, interest rates remain elevated, and rents in most submarkets cannot support what new construction costs to deliver. Local requirements -- design standards, infrastructure obligations, slow approvals -- add cost without adding units. The zoning reforms and PILOT expansions coming out of this session are necessary but not sufficient without a serious capital strategy.

**Rental Housing Works gap.** Maryland cut the program by $20M this year and faces further cuts in FY2028. The state is short roughly 90,000 units for households at 60% AMI or below -- a number that grows annually. PILOT (Payment-In-Lieu-Of-Taxes) authority is expanding, which is meaningful for LIHTC deals, but without gap financing the math still fails for many projects.

**The NYC pension model.** NYC Comptroller Brad Lander directed a portion of New York City's $270 billion public pension funds into affordable housing through targeted allocations to CDFIs and housing construction vehicles. The argument: well-structured LIHTC-anchored affordable housing deals generate steady, predictable returns that match pension fund liability profiles. Fiduciary duty and public benefit are not in conflict when the investment is structured correctly.

**Maryland's pension assets.** Maryland's State Retirement and Pension System manages roughly $67 billion on behalf of teachers, state employees, and public safety workers. County and municipal funds hold billions more. All of it is actively seeking returns. The enabling legislation proposal would allow pension trustees to treat affordable housing as a legitimate asset class rather than a social mission incompatible with fiduciary duty.

## Relevance

This is directly relevant to how Frederick thinks about the Housing Fund, the CLT proposal, and any future LIHTC-supported development. If Maryland enables pension investment in affordable housing, it opens a new capital pathway that doesn't depend on Rental Housing Works or federal CDBG flows. Worth monitoring as a potential state legislative track for next session.

The PILOTs argument is also relevant to Frederick: the fee structure coming out of the Rental Licensing rewrite (fee cut from $240 to ~$150) has downstream implications for Housing Fund capacity. The capital gap at the state level makes the Housing Fund more, not less, important for local leverage.
