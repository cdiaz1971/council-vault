# Parking Reform

## What Frederick's code requires

Parking minimums are set in **LMC §607, Table 607-1** ([[Frederick Housing Policy Baseline]]):

| Use | Minimum | Maximum |
|---|---|---|
| Single-family | 2 per unit | none |
| Duplex | 1.5 per unit | 2.5 per unit |
| Townhouse | 2 per unit | 3.0 per unit |
| Multifamily | 1.5 per unit | 2.5 per unit |

- **ADUs** need one additional space only if existing parking is at or below the code minimum.
- **Downtown:** requirements are relaxed in the Carroll Creek Overlay (§420) and the DB district. There's no citywide elimination outside downtown ([[Frederick Housing Policy Baseline]]).

## The cost case

- **Cost per space.** A surface space costs roughly $5,000–10,000 to build and a structured space $25,000–50,000. Those costs pass through to renters and buyers whether or not they own a car ([[LMC Zoning Amendments -- Personal Strategy Brief (2026-05-13)]]). One analysis puts them at the upper end: about $10,000 surface and $40,000+ structured ([[Parking Minimums as a Housing Cost Driver]]). North Carolina's bill sponsor cited $5,000–10,000 per surface space and $25,000–65,000 per deck space ([HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/north-carolina-parking-reform-law/)).
- **Land.** Minimums also consume land. On infill lots the land needed for required parking can cost more than the construction, which effectively sets a floor on density and a ceiling on affordability ([[Parking Minimums as a Housing Cost Driver]]).
- **Missing middle.** Parking is often what decides whether a small multifamily building on an infill lot pencils out ([[LMC Zoning Amendments -- Personal Strategy Brief (2026-05-13)]]).
- **Reform removes the mandate, not parking.** Developers can still build as much as they want ([[Parking Minimums as a Housing Cost Driver]]).

## Reform models

- **Washington State (SB 5184, 2025): use-case exemptions.** Rather than tie relief to transit proximity, which invites fights over whether service is "good enough," Washington exempted:
  - homes under 1,200 square feet
  - commercial spaces under 3,000 square feet
  - ground-floor retail in mixed-use buildings
  - childcare centers
  - subsidized affordable and senior housing
  - changes of use

  ([[Parking Minimums as a Housing Cost Driver]]) César's April 2026 brief names this the recommended model for Frederick, because it sidesteps the transit question ([[Affordable Housing Policy Brief -- 2026-04-17]]).
- **Dallas (2025).** Dallas removed parking minimums. One early result: a senior-housing project expanded without adding 200 spaces the old rules would have required, freeing capital and preserving two trees on site ([[Dallas Parking Reform Produced Concrete Results -- Peer City Evidence]]).
- **North Carolina (statewide).** The Parking Lot Reform and Modernization Act ran as two bills:
  - **HB 369** passed the House unanimously in 2025 and stalled in the Senate. It paired an end to parking mandates with stormwater changes for redevelopment ([[NC Parking Reform HB369 -- Stormwater Housing Affordability Linkage]]; [[CITYBUILDER Brief -- NC Parking Reform and Housing Density]]; [NC General Assembly, H369](https://www.ncleg.gov/BillLookup/2025/h369)).
  - **HB 162** carried the act in 2026, with a coastal-county exemption added to secure passage. The Senate passed it 44-1, the House concurred 111-2 on June 30, and Gov. Josh Stein signed it **July 6, 2026** as **Session Law 2026-39** ([NC General Assembly, H162](https://www.ncleg.gov/BillLookup/2025/h162); [HousingWire](https://www.housingwire.com/articles/north-carolina-parking-reform-law/)).

  It bars local governments from requiring minimum off-street parking for most new residential and commercial development, making North Carolina the first state to do so nearly statewide. It covers about 90% of the population and takes effect **January 1, 2027**. It lets localities offer incentives, including fee waivers and tax benefits, for redevelopment that captures more stormwater, and the Catawba Riverkeeper was a coalition partner ([[NC Statewide Parking Minimum Repeal as Peer City Model]]).
- **Asheville, North Carolina.** In April 2025 Asheville removed parking minimums in certain districts, mainly along transit-supportive corridors ([City of Asheville](https://www.ashevillenc.gov/news/information-on-newly-adopted-udo-text-amendments/)). In 2026 it proposed going citywide as part of a package that also enlarged ADUs, allowed duplexes in all residential zones, and would have added a "vulnerability index" to track displacement risk ([[Asheville Could Eliminate Parking Minimums]]). Council adopted the package on **August 25, 2026**: residential parking requirements were eliminated, duplexes are now allowed in every residential district and some mixed-use districts, and the ADU size limit went up ([Asheville.com](https://www.asheville.com/news/2026/08/asheville-expands-duplex-and-accessory-dwelling-rules/)).
- **Illinois.** The People Over Parking Act (SB 2111), signed by Gov. JB Pritzker on December 16, 2025, bars local parking minimums within a half mile of a public transportation hub or an eighth of a mile of a transit corridor. That made Illinois the fourth state to preempt parking mandates, after Oregon (by rule), California, and Colorado ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illinois_People_Over_Parking_Act); [[CITYBUILDER Brief -- Parking Reform in NC and IL]]).

## Maryland: HB 894

Maryland's Transit and Housing Opportunity Act (HB 894, signed May 2026) restricts local parking minimums and density limits near transit ([[Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act -- Parking Minimums Near Transit]]):
- **Parking minimums** are barred within **a quarter mile** of a qualifying rail station, and mixed use must be allowed within **a half mile**.
- **Qualifying stations** must have at least hourly weekday service, on average, from 8 a.m. to 6 p.m. They are on WMATA heavy rail, the Purple Line, Baltimore Light Rail and Metro, and the MARC **Penn** Line between New Carrollton and Baltimore Penn Station.

([National Center for Smart Growth](https://www.umdsmartgrowth.org/news/tod-preemption-passes-the-legislature/); [[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]])

Frederick County's four MARC stations are all on the **Brunswick Line**: Point of Rocks and Brunswick on the main line, and Monocacy and Frederick on its Frederick branch ([[Housing Policy Brief -- 2026-04-04]]; [Wikipedia, Brunswick Line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunswick_Line)). None is on a qualifying line, so **HB 894 doesn't reach Frederick today**. Lines could qualify later if service frequency is upgraded. Until then, any parking relief in Frederick has to come from the City's own code.

## Parking and stormwater

Required parking means more impervious pavement, so minimums raise runoff and heat as well as cost. Two reforms work together: cutting how much parking is mandated shrinks future lots, and improving surface materials (permeable paving, bioswales, shading) improves the lots that already exist ([[Parking Lot Reform as Multi-Benefit Infrastructure Policy]]; see [[Frederick Water, Sewer, and Stormwater]]). North Carolina's law built this link in, with an environmental group in the coalition ([[NC Statewide Parking Minimum Repeal as Peer City Model]]).

## Frederick: pending items and pressure points

- **City proposals in planning.** Planning-stage City parking proposals, which haven't been introduced, are tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].
- **ADU ordinance (PC25-614ZTA).** The draft reduces ADU parking with a transit-proximity exemption at 1,320 feet, the distance already used for downtown DB and DBO parking reductions, plus smaller stall dimensions ([[ADU Ordinance Brief 2026-08-13]]). The Planning Commission's 4-0 recommendation (March 17, 2026) included a parking exemption for the DB, DR, and DBO districts that the draft doesn't contain. The ordinance was discussed at the August 13, 2026 work session with no vote (Notion roadmap, checked 2026-09-24).
- **Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force.** Its July 2026 final report endorses parking-minimum reduction for affordable housing and rates it high urgency and near-term, alongside missing middle, ADU reform, and faith-based by-right zoning ([[MAHTF Final Report Policy Brief -- 2026-07-16]]).
- **Variance pressure.** Conifer's Himes Ave affordable project (145 LIHTC units) sought to reduce parking from 1.5 to 1.25 spaces per unit to maximize unit count ([[Himes Ave -- Conifer LIHTC Development Meeting Notes (2026-03-16)]]). The variance was granted (per César, 2026-09-24). The April brief treats that as the visible edge of a code problem that affects every affordable project in the pipeline ([[Affordable Housing Policy Brief -- 2026-04-17]]).
