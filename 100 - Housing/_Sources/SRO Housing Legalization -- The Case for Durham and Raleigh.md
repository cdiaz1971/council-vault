# SRO Housing Legalization -- The Case for Durham and Raleigh

## Summary

An argument for legalizing single-room occupancy housing in Durham and Raleigh by removing special-use permit requirements -- directly applicable to Frederick's LMC reform and housing production work.

## Key Points

SROs are small private rooms with shared kitchens and bathrooms -- the cheapest non-subsidized housing type available. They were largely eliminated through post-WWII zoning reform across American cities, taking the bottom rung of the housing ladder with them.

The case for SROs rests on three pillars. First, they are the cheapest housing available without subsidy -- historically hundreds of dollars below even modest apartments. Second, they serve a wide range of people: students, young workers, people between jobs, single adults. Third, they unlock existing housing stock (spare rooms, underused buildings) without requiring subsidized construction.

In Durham, SROs are only permitted without special-use approval on campuses and downtown. Raleigh has a hard cap of four unrelated persons per dwelling, which effectively bans SROs outside a designated Co-Living district. Both cities are sitting on unused housing capacity the law won't let activate.

The author's proposal: add SRO-permitting language to Durham's RD/RX residential districts without requiring special use approval. Occupy standards should align with the Fair Housing Act. For Raleigh, drop the "four unrelated persons" rule and move SRO out of a special district into standard residential zoning.

The framing is "gentle density" -- SROs produce upzoning effects without high-rises, activating existing buildings along transit corridors and near mixed-use zones.

## Relevance

Frederick is deep in LMC reform and housing production work. SRO legalization fits directly into the missing-middle conversation -- it's a zoning change that doesn't require new infrastructure or subsidies, just removing a regulatory barrier. The transit-corridor argument is directly applicable to Frederick's East Street and Golden Mile corridors.

The North Carolina context is peer-city territory. Durham in particular has been tracked repeatedly in this vault ([[CITYBUILDER Brief -- NC Parking Reform and Housing Density]]). The existing [[SRO thread]] in the vault captures earlier research on this topic -- this piece provides fresh H1 2026 legislative context and a specific policy proposal worth referencing in LMC reform discussions.
