# ROAD to Housing Act Enacted Without Presidential Signature

## Summary

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became law without President Trump's signature after nearly a year of negotiations, enacting a suite of federal housing reforms including manufactured home cost reductions and local incentive structures.

## Key Points

The act crossed the finish line at midnight on Friday, July 11, becoming law through congressional passage without a presidential signature. After decades of federal inaction on housing supply, the law introduces a broad set of policy tools aimed at increasing production.

The CITYBUILDER newsletter, which covers North Carolina housing policy closely, highlighted two main levers in the act: reducing the price of manufactured homes, and creating financial incentives for communities that allow more housing. Their focus is on how state and local governments in NC can implement the reforms to unlock more supply.

The same issue flagged that the NC Senate is advancing a separate regulatory reform bill, suggesting momentum at the state level to complement the federal action.

## Relevance

This is a significant update to the existing vault note [[21st Century ROAD Act Passes -- Federal Housing Incentive Structure and Its Limits]] from June 26, which was written before enactment. That note covered the legislative structure and limits; this one confirms it is now law and that implementation planning is beginning.

For Frederick, the manufactured housing provisions are the most directly actionable piece -- manufactured homes are central to the Golden Mile corridor housing mix and to the Real Rent Ordinance work. The incentive structures for communities that allow more housing also connect directly to the LMC reform track and the Housing Production Target Resolution.

The NC implementation framing from CITYBUILDER is a useful peer-city model: if NC is moving quickly to adapt its zoning and regulatory posture in response to the ROAD Act, Frederick should be tracking what those state-level moves look like and whether Maryland has analogous reform levers.
