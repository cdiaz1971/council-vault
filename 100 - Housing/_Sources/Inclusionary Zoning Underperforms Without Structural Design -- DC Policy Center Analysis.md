# Inclusionary Zoning Underperforms Without Structural Design -- DC Policy Center Analysis

## Summary

DC's IZ program produced fewer than 300 affordable units per year over 17 years. The DC Policy Center identifies a scarcity-subsidy cycle that suppresses overall production and recommends three structural fixes: LIHTC rent alignment, floating affordability bands, and automatic suspension during permitting downturns.

## Key Points

Testimony by Emilia Calma, Director of The Wilkes Initiative for Housing Policy, delivered at DC Council's Performance Oversight Hearing for DHCD on March 5, 2026. Draws from an upcoming DC Policy Center report proposing 24 recommendations for a new housing framework.

The core diagnosis is a scarcity-subsidy cycle. When a developer sets aside below-market units, the income shortfall gets absorbed through higher market-rate rents and thinner margins. Thinner margins make projects harder to finance, biasing production toward larger buildings, smaller units, and high-end amenities -- the opposite of what IZ intends. After 17 years, DC's IZ program produced fewer than 300 affordable units per year citywide. It also only applies where multifamily zoning exists (a small portion of DC), and it tracks market activity -- producing least when need is greatest.

Three recommended reforms:

1. Align maximum allowable rents in IZ units with LIHTC rent levels. This does not change affordability outcomes but reduces the operating income restriction that makes IZ units financially difficult to manage as insurance, taxes, and utilities rise over time.

2. Allow affordability designations to float between 30 and 80 percent of median family income, as long as the combined average stays at or below 60 percent MFI. This gives developers flexibility to make projects financially viable while maintaining the overall target.

3. Build in an automatic suspension trigger: if quarterly multifamily permits fall below 625 units (2,500 annually) for two consecutive quarters, IZ requirements automatically suspend for one year. This protects supply during downturns -- when the program produces least and need is greatest.

## Relevance

This analysis is directly relevant to any inclusionary requirements Frederick considers tying to new development approvals. Frederick's housing market operates at lower margins than DC's, which means the perverse effects of poorly designed IZ would be more acute here, not less.

If the council moves toward any affordability requirement on new residential development -- through a density bonus, voluntary set-aside ordinance, or condition on HACF properties -- the DC Policy Center's reform framework provides a ready-made design template. The LIHTC rent alignment, floating affordability bands, and automatic suspension trigger are three specific parameters to build into any Frederick IZ proposal from the start rather than retrofit later.

The Lucas Village redevelopment and any future HACF projects are the most immediate context. Affordability conditions attached to those projects should be designed to not suppress overall unit counts -- which means building in the flexibility mechanisms the DC Policy Center recommends.
