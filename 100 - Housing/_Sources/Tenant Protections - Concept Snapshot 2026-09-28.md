# Tenant Protections

## Frederick and Maryland

- **Real Rent Ordinance (pending).** César's working draft of a new Chapter 12.5, Article VI is an **all-in pricing and fee** ordinance, enforced through rental licensing. It expressly doesn't cap or limit rent ([[Real Rent Ordinance]]). As drafted:
  - **All-in price.** Landlords must advertise, quote, and charge a single **Total Rent** that includes every mandatory fee, including mandatory pet rent.
  - **Mandatory-fee presumptions.** A fee is presumed mandatory if more than 25% of a property's tenants pay it, or if refusing it delays or worsens the lease.
  - **Anti-circumvention.** It bars disguising mandatory fees as optional, requiring services as a condition of the lease, and routing fees through third-party vendors.
  - **Prohibited separate fees.** Administrative, move-in/out, technology/portal, payment-processing, separately billed trash/pest/common-area, utility-administration, renewal, inspection, and non-refundable pet fees.
  - **Allowed charges.** Application fees are capped at actual screening cost and refunded if no screening is done. Security deposits and late fees stay governed by state law, and pet deposits must be refundable.
  - **Utilities.** They can be billed separately only by actual metered usage or a written, consistently applied allocation formula disclosed at signing. Flat utility fees and any fee added to a utility charge are prohibited.
  - **Licensing enforcement.** Compliance is a condition of the rental license, with civil penalties of $500, $1,000, then $2,500 per unit, and each month counts as a separate offense.
  - **Private right of action.** Tenants can sue for treble damages, at least $1,000 in statutory damages, and attorney's fees, with the burden on the landlord.
  - **Effective date.** 120 days after adoption, for new leases and renewals.

  It was workshopped July 9, 2026 at the council-feedback stage before introduction ([[Workshop Brief 2026-07-09]]). Per the Notion roadmap (checked 2026-09-24) it's **In Committee**. The text above is **César's working draft**. The City Attorney's comments would remove the private right of action (*Aleti v. Metropolitan Baltimore*) and the security-deposit provisions, and raise the anti-circumvention provision for legal review. César is weighing those comments, and a final draft hasn't been prepared yet (per César, 2026-09-24).
- **Rental licensing (pending).** Frederick licenses about 7,800 rental properties. A rewrite would add a revocation path after three formal violations ([[What Happens After License Revocation -- The Missing Piece in Frederick's Tenant Protection Stack]]). Council Member Brehm separately proposed codifying the license fee at 150% of program operating cost, about $150 versus $240 today. At $240 the program raised $912K in FY24 and $971K in FY25 against operating costs of $281K and $268K ([[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-05-26]]). The Rental Licensing Program Review is In Committee (Notion roadmap, checked 2026-09-24).
- **State law.** Maryland's Good Cause Eviction bill (HB 774 / SB 462) died in the 2026 session. As drafted, it would have authorized counties, not municipalities ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]).
- **What state law already provides.** The Renters' Rights and Stabilization Act (HB 693, 2024):
  - created an Office of Tenant and Landlord Affairs at DHCD
  - gave tenants in 1–3 unit properties a right of first refusal: landlords must notify qualifying tenants before selling to a third party, and tenants get 30 days to make an offer (effective October 2024)
  - required a Maryland Tenants' Bill of Rights, which took effect July 1, 2025. It must be attached to every new residential lease and is updated by DHCD each September. Maryland is the first state to mandate one.

  State law doesn't provide just cause eviction, rent stabilization, or relocation assistance. Buildings with more than three units aren't covered by the right of first refusal, unlike Washington, DC's Tenant Opportunity to Purchase Act, which covers all rentals ([[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]]).
- **Rockville's Chapter 18 rewrite (effective January 1, 2027).** After work sessions from 2024 to 2026, Rockville became the first Maryland city to **ban algorithmic rent pricing**. New §18-148 bars using an "algorithmic device" drawing on non-public competitor data to set rents or fees, bars price coordination, and exempts single-unit owner-landlords. The rewrite also limits payment-processing and administrative fees, expands relocation assistance for tenants displaced by code violations, and requires transparency on lease terms, utility costs, and mandatory fees ([City of Rockville](https://www.rockvillemd.gov/news/2026/07/07/rockville-updates-rental-housing-code-becomes-first-maryland-city-to-ban-algorithmic-rent-pricing/); [[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]).
- **Frederick's planning-stage tenant work** is tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].

## Rent stabilization

- **Evidence on landlord returns.** Research by the UK's Joseph Rowntree Foundation found private landlords earning supernormal returns from rent and capital gains, even in a high-interest environment. That undercuts the claim that caps would trigger a sell-off. Its modeled cap (CPI within tenancies, CPI + 2% between) combined with tax reform would leave **fewer** landlords in loss by 2030 than today, while renters gain about £1,200 a year. New supply can be exempted, as Ireland does for new apartments ([[Making Rent Controls Work -- JRF Research on Landlord Returns, Tax Reform, and the Supply Compatibility Argument]]).
- **Design: Massachusetts.** A proposed ballot measure, backed by more than 124,000 signatures and headed for the November 2026 ballot, would cap annual increases at 5% or CPI, whichever is lower. It exempts small owner-occupied buildings and buildings under 10 years old. It would be the first statewide measure since the 1994 repeal (51–49). By 1998, rents on formerly controlled units had risen 54% ([[Massachusetts Rent Control Ballot Measure -- 5 Percent Cap With New Construction Exemption]]).
- **Legal exposure: New York City.** After the Rent Guidelines Board voted a 0% increase on stabilized lease renewals, landlords sued. They challenged the board's composition and its handling of costs, not the power to set rents. Tenant advocates pointed to a 6% rise in landlord net operating income ([[NYC Rent Freeze Legal Challenge Landlord Litigation as Counter-Strategy]]).

## Fees, utility billing, and algorithmic pricing

- **Algorithmic rent-setting.** New Jersey's FAIR Act, signed by Gov. Mikie Sherrill, made it the first state to ban AI rent-setting software, treating it as an antitrust violation. The state is separately suing RealPage ([[New Jersey FAIR Act Bans AI-Driven Rent Setting]]). Rockville's local ban is the Maryland precedent (above).
- **State enforcement.** State attorneys general can pursue algorithmic collusion under the Sherman Act even as federal enforcement recedes. DOJ and eight states sued RealPage, and New Jersey sued RealPage and ten large landlords. For junk fees, Maryland joined a 26-state AG letter (April 2026) urging a federal rental fee rule. Colorado (effective January 1, 2026), Virginia, and Connecticut require fee disclosure ([[States Can Put the Brakes on Landlord Collusion and Junk Fees]]).
- **Utility billing (RUBS).** Ratio utility billing splits a building's utility bill among tenants by formula rather than meter, often with a markup. That raises effective rent and removes the landlord's incentive to fix inefficiency. Tenants at Virgil Square in Los Angeles have held a year-long RUBS rent strike and are seeking a citywide ban ([[RUBS Strike and Utility Billing as a Tenant Protection Gap]]). Frederick's Real Rent draft regulates rather than bans the practice: it permits a disclosed, consistently applied allocation formula but prohibits flat utility fees and any billing or administrative fee added to utility charges ([[Real Rent Ordinance]]).

## Eviction prevention

- **The toolkit.** Shelterforce's April 2026 overview lists right to counsel, emergency rental assistance funds, good cause eviction (which research endorsed by 54 housing economists found doesn't suppress new construction), and mediation or payment-plan requirements ([[Eviction Prevention as Local Housing Stability Policy]]).
- **Diversion: Alexandria, Virginia.** Tenants behind on rent can stay by paying 10% of the balance upfront and the rest in installments. The program targets one-time shocks such as car repairs or medical bills, and landlords avoid filing costs and vacancy ([[Alexandria Eviction Diversion Program Lets Tenants Pay Back Rent in Installments]]).

## Source-of-income (voucher) protections

- **Coverage.** Twenty-four states and 150 localities bar discrimination against voucher holders, covering about 60% of voucher holders ([[Fourth Amendment Challenge Threatens Source-of-Income Voucher Protections Nationwide]]).
- **The Fourth Amendment challenge.** In March 2026 a New York appellate court held the state's 2019 law unconstitutional, reasoning that Section 8 inspections require landlord consent. The ruling is stayed on appeal and the law remains enforceable. At least 12 similar challenges are underway nationally, including *Moore v. Mac Property Management* in Illinois, and one in Lawrence, Kansas was rejected in 2025 ([[Fourth Amendment Challenge Threatens Source-of-Income Voucher Protections Nationwide]]).
- **Preemption: Kansas City.** Kansas City's January 2024 ordinance was enjoined within a month on a Fourth Amendment theory. A May 2025 Missouri law then barred every locality from enforcing source-of-income protections, ending the ordinance regardless of how the litigation came out ([[State Preemption Killed Kansas City's Voucher Nondiscrimination Ordinance]]; *same article as the preceding note*).
- **Mobility.** Vouchers are nominally portable, but billing between housing authorities, delays, payment-standard limits, and weak landlord outreach discourage moves. Families who move to higher-opportunity areas see better school and earnings outcomes for their children ([[Housing Voucher Portability -- Urban Institute]]).

## Older renters

- **Scale.** One in five older adults rents. About half are cost-burdened, and most of those spend more than half their income on housing. Fixed incomes, accessibility needs, isolation, and the near-impossibility of moving make their precarity different in kind. People 50 and older are the fastest-growing group experiencing homelessness ([[Older Renters Face the Most Severe Consequences of the Housing Crisis]]).
- **Targeted tools:**
  - New York's Senior Citizen Rent Increase Exemption (SCRIE) freezes rent for eligible tenants 62+ in rent-regulated units. Eligibility requires income under $75,000 and rent above a third of income. Landlords receive a property-tax abatement instead of the increase.
  - Los Angeles is developing a similar program at 80% of area median income, with a companion program for disabled renters.
  - Both programs apply only to regulated units.
  - Chicago's Senior Safety Ordinance adds habitability requirements during public-health emergencies.

## Organizing, tools, and landlord pressure

- **Organizing.** Democratic Socialists of America (DSA) chapters use tenant organizing for power-building and political education. In New Haven, organizers revived a fair rent commission that had sat largely unused since 1984 ([[DSA Tenant Organizing as a Political Power-Building Strategy]]).
- **Tenant-side technology.** Tools include ownership lookups (Landlord Mapper, Who Owns What) and guided eviction-response tools (Tenant Power Toolkit, Greater Boston Legal Services' MADE). They help but don't substitute for legal protections or a right to counsel ([[Tenant-Side Tech Tools for Landlord Accountability]]).
- **Landlord legal pressure.** In Chicago, tenants who settled their eviction cases on condition of public silence were then accused of breaching the settlement over interviews given to Shelterforce before it existed ([[Landlord Legal Pressure Against Tenants as Suppression of Journalism]]).
