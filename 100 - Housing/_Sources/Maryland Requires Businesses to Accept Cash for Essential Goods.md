# Maryland Requires Businesses to Accept Cash for Essential Goods

## Summary

HB 191, effective October 1, 2026, requires Maryland businesses to accept cash for in-person purchases of food, fuel, and health and hygiene products under $300 between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m., with no cash surcharge. It protects unbanked households, a group concentrated among immigrant and ALICE residents.

## Key Points

HB 191, sponsored by Del. Greg Wims (D-Montgomery), took effect October 1, 2026. Businesses must accept cash for in-person purchases of essential goods, defined to include food, fuel, and health and hygiene products. The mandate is bounded in three ways. It applies only between 6 a.m. and 10 p.m., only to purchases under $300, and only to in-person sales. Within those limits, businesses cannot charge more to customers paying in cash.

The law targets the drift toward cashless retail, where a store that accepts only cards or app payments effectively excludes anyone without a bank account or credit card. For essential goods, that exclusion is a basic access problem rather than an inconvenience.

## Relevance

This belongs to the administrative and affordability dimensions of food access. A household can live next to a grocery store and still be shut out if the store will not take its money. The people most likely to rely on cash are the same residents at the center of César's affordability work: ALICE households, seniors, and immigrant families without access to mainstream banking.

The connection to the Municipal ID work is direct. One of the core equity arguments for a municipal ID is that lack of accepted identification keeps residents out of the banking system. HB 191 protects those residents at the point of sale in the meantime, and the two policies address the same gap from opposite ends.

It also extends the consumer protection frame already developing around the surveillance pricing ban. Both laws stop retailers from using payment and data systems in ways that fall hardest on lower-income shoppers.

Open question worth checking: whether the City or County has any role in fielding complaints about noncompliance, or whether enforcement sits entirely with the state.
