# Starter Homes Over $1M in 242 Cities -- Supply Restriction Is the Variable

## Summary

New Zillow data shows a record 242 U.S. cities now have starter homes priced at $1 million or more. California accounts for 105 of those cities, and 26 states have at least one. The divergence between Sun Belt and Northeast markets reveals supply response as the key variable: where new housing was added, price growth moderated; where it was not, prices climbed without check.

## Key Points

The national median for starter homes remains just under $200,000 -- which means million-dollar starter homes are not a national condition, but a concentrated regional failure. The Northeast has not experienced the supply-side relief that Sun Belt markets have. The Zillow analysis is direct: eliminating barriers to building, including restrictive zoning, is the most direct path to improvement.

The Sun Belt pattern is the control case. States that permitted new supply -- through zoning reform, ADU legalization, or reduced permitting friction -- saw price growth moderate. The Northeast, with its more restrictive land use environments, saw prices continue climbing. This is not about regional demand differences; it is about whether housing supply can respond to demand.

California's 105-city concentration reflects decades of restrictive local zoning layered on top of high demand and geographic constraints. New Jersey and the broader Northeast corridor show the same dynamic at a smaller geographic scale.

For the starter home category specifically, the millennial and Gen Z ownership cohort is the most affected. The home that would historically have been the entry point into the ownership market is now priced out of reach in a growing share of American cities.

## Relevance

This is direct evidence for the Housing Production Target Resolution. The 1,400 units/year target is not an abstract number -- it is a response to a market where insufficient supply is pricing out the workforce, the young, and the moderate-income household. Frederick does not yet have $1M starter homes, but it is operating in a regional market where Maryland inventory is down 16.4% and prices are up 4.7% year-over-year (see [[Maryland Housing Supply Crisis -- Inventory Down 16.4 Percent, Prices Up 4.7 Percent in 2026]]). The trajectory is set; the question is whether Frederick acts before the gap widens further.

The Sun Belt vs. Northeast divergence is also a useful rhetorical frame for the LMC reform conversation. Opposition to zoning reform often comes with the implicit argument that supply doesn't affect price. This data refutes that directly: the markets that added supply got relief, and the ones that didn't got none.
