# Making rent controls work -- JRF research on landlord returns, tax reform, and the supply compatibility argument

A guest post by Rosie Worsdale of the Joseph Rowntree Foundation, published in *The Week in Housing* (June 26, 2026), before the newsletter goes on summer break. The analysis draws on new JRF-commissioned research into landlord rates of return in England's private rented sector (PRS).

## The central argument

Rent control opponents have long relied on the threat of a landlord sell-off as the decisive objection. JRF's research takes that argument seriously and then systematically dismantles it. The key finding: landlords have been making **supernormal rates of return** -- from a combination of rental income and capital gains -- even in the recent high-interest-rate environment. A majority continue to outperform both economy-wide comparable investments and the broader real-estate sector.

That finding has a direct policy implication: modestly capping returns through a rent control would not create widespread unprofitability. For most landlords, rental investment would remain competitive.

## Proposed mechanism

JRF models a cap structured as:
- CPI within tenancies
- CPI + 2% between tenancies

Under this model, renting households would be roughly £1,200/year better off by 2030/31. The near-term affordability gain is precisely the point: even if new supply eventually solves the structural imbalance, OBR forecasts show rents rising in lockstep with wages through at least the end of the decade. Supply-side solutions don't reach today's renters.

## Tax reform as the pairing mechanism

The vulnerable minority -- heavily mortgaged landlords hit hard by rising interest rates -- could be protected through tax reform rather than diluting the rent cap. JRF models reinstating full mortgage interest relief while applying National Insurance Contributions to rental income. This rebalances the tax burden from squeezed landlords to those making the largest profits.

The counterintuitive result: the combined package of rent control + tax reform would see **fewer landlords in loss by 2030** than the current system with no controls.

## Supply compatibility

The standard political framing pits rent control against new supply. Worsdale rejects this as a false binary. New rental supply can be exempted from caps -- Ireland's model exempts newly built apartments in perpetuity from the 2% annual cap -- preserving investment incentives for new development while protecting existing renters.

## Frederick relevance

This piece provides strong comparative framing for the Real Rent Ordinance work. Key arguments directly applicable to the Frederick context:

- The sell-off threat is empirically weak when landlord returns are supernormal
- Short-term rent stabilization and long-term supply production are complementary, not competing
- Tax reform (if state law permits) can address the legitimate concerns of small, mortgaged landlords without abandoning the stabilization mechanism
- The supply/rent-control false binary is a rhetorical move from opponents, not an analytical finding

The England context is different from Maryland law and market conditions, but the underlying economics -- particularly on landlord return rates and the framing of supply vs. stabilization -- transfers directly.
