# Seattle Ordinance 127497 - Rental Fee Disclosure, Prohibited Fees, and Enforcement

## Summary
Seattle Council Bill 121254, signed as Ordinance 127497, regulates rental housing fees and rebuilds enforcement of Seattle's rental agreement code (SMC Chapter 7.24). Passed August 11, 2026, approved by Mayor Katie B. Wilson August 18, 2026, with Sections 1 through 17 effective **July 1, 2027**. It applies to rental agreements and renewals entered into after that date. It was a Mayor's Office and Seattle Department of Construction and Inspections (SDCI) bill, briefed to committee by Central Staff analyst HB Harper on July 22, 2026. Copies of the ordinance, fiscal note, and presentation are in Google Drive under City council / 02 - Legislation / Tenant Protection.

## Key Points

**Findings the Council adopted (recitals)**
- Inflation-adjusted average rents rose 32% from 2012 to 2022, from $1,430 to $1,897 (CoStar and BERK).
- 38,365 Seattle renter households are cost burdened and 33,795 severely cost burdened (2025 Comprehensive Plan).
- 89% of renters pay at least one fee on top of rent (National Consumer Law Center, 2023). Renters of color (67%), especially AAPI (74%) and Hispanic (66%), report paying fees more often than White renters (63%). Renters 18 to 29 report it at 82% (Zillow, 2025).
- Fees add 10% to 30% to the total monthly payment (Urban Institute, 2025), which at Seattle's June 2026 average rent of $2,038 is $203 to $609 a month.
- A $100 rise in median rent is associated with a 9% rise in homelessness (GAO, 2020).
- **Market failure finding:** no landlord has an incentive to advertise an all-in price, because it looks more expensive than competitors with lower base rent and unbundled fees (Stanford research, Mahoney in the Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2025). The Council called junk fees a collective action problem that will not self-correct.
- Building-wide and company-wide investigation is a more efficient use of City resources than case-by-case enforcement.

**Fee disclosure (SMC 7.24.039)**
- Every advertisement, listing, and rental application, and a form of no more than two pages placed before the first page of the lease, must show: monthly rent, any discounts or concessions and how long they last, utilities the tenant pays, utilities included, every mandatory and optional fee, and the **total monthly cost** including estimated utilities and the estimated total with variable fees. Short-term concessions and optional fees are excluded from the total.
- Online listings may use a clearly labeled hyperlink to the disclosure if space is limited. Landlords are not liable for listings they did not authorize, such as aggregator sites.
- An updated two-page disclosure is due every 12 months and whenever a non-variable fee changes.
- Variable fees and utilities: disclose the prior 12-month monthly average, or a labeled estimate for new buildings, and send a monthly statement of each variable charge.
- No fee may be charged for preparing the disclosure or monthly statements.
- SDCI publishes a model disclosure form translated into the five most common non-English languages in Seattle, updated annually using the Office of Immigrant and Refugee Affairs list.

**Permitted mandatory fees (SMC 7.24.040.A to B).** Any fee not authorized by Chapter 7.24, Chapter 7.25 (utilities), or state law is an "unfair or excessive fee," regardless of its name. Any fee not disclosed under 7.24.039 is also prohibited. Allowed:
- Late rent fees under the existing $10 cap (SMC 7.24.034; see [[Seattle Ordinance 126803 - Late Fee Cap and Notice Fee Ban]]).
- Security deposits and nonrefundable move-in fees, screening fees, and pet damage deposits under existing law.
- Utility fees under Chapter 7.25.
- Replacement key: lesser of actual cost or $50.
- Lockout: $50 during weekday business hours (none if the landlord is on site), $150 after hours.
- Returned payment: lesser of actual bank cost or $31.
- Documented repair of tenant-caused damage under state law.
- Liability for default or abandonment under state law, with no separate lease-break fee.

**Optional fees (SMC 7.24.040.B.11)**
- Must be disclosed as optional, affirmatively opted into in writing, accompanied by written opt-out instructions, and cancelable at any time without penalty.
- If a third party provides the service, the fee may not exceed the landlord's actual cost.
- **Bundles must be unbundled:** each component of a bundle, such as cable and internet, must be available separately.

**Fees banned even as optional (SMC 7.24.040.C)**
- Use of an in-unit appliance or feature (except portable cooling device rental under 2026 state law).
- Access to common areas, except temporary exclusive use such as renting a clubhouse.
- Accepting rent by personal check, money order, cashier's check, or ACH.
- Receiving mail, packages, or mailbox access.
- Performing any landlord duty under the housing code or state landlord-tenant act.
- Adding or removing a roommate (screening fees still allowed).
- **Any pet fee** other than a pet damage deposit, however named.
- Landlord-provided insurance unless it meets the opt-in rules, the tenant gets the policy in advance with clear disclosure of whether personal property and loss of use are covered, changes are noticed within five days, and the policy meets Director's minimum standards.
- Any other optional fee the Director bans by rule. The rule factors are: real tenant benefit, relation to cost, whether it exceeds minimum legal standards, whether it is truly optional, similarity to banned fees or to rent, eviction risk, and prevalence.

**Records (SMC 7.24.045).** Landlords keep three years of compliance records per tenant: ads, listings, applications, offered and signed leases and renewals, disclosure forms, variable fee statements, rent and fees charged, and vendor receipts. If records are missing, there is a **rebuttable presumption of violation**, overcome only by clear and convincing evidence.

**Defenses and private remedies (SMC 7.24.050, 7.24.060)**
- Illegal fee or disclosure terms are a defense in eviction and collection actions, with attorney fees to the prevailing tenant. In a nonpayment eviction, the rent claimed is reduced by all prohibited fees charged.
- Private right of action: actual damages plus interest, **double** any prohibited fee or deposit unlawfully charged or withheld, double the citation penalty per violation, and attorney fees. Interest runs at 12% a year from the date the fee was imposed.
- Up to $4,000 for including prohibited provisions in a lease. Up to $6,000 plus damages for retaliation.
- Tenants may sue on behalf of similarly situated tenants affected by the same policy or practice of a common defendant.
- **Cure defense:** if, within 30 days of a prohibited-fee notice, the landlord stops the fee, repays every similarly situated tenant in the building the full fee plus interest plus double the fee, and reports the cure to SDCI, it has a defense against those tenants' suits for that fee. The defense is lost for a repeat of the same fee within 24 months.
- All liability amounts index to the Seattle-area CPI-U housing component starting January 1, 2028. Tenants may not recover twice for the same harm in different forums.

**Administrative enforcement (SMC 7.24.120 to 7.24.240)**
- First and second violations proceed by citation. Later ones may go to notice of violation or criminal enforcement at the Director's discretion.
- Citation penalties: **$750 first, $1,500 for each subsequent violation within five years, $5,000 for retaliation**, indexed to CPI-U from 2028. A warning is allowed for a first-time violator. Unpaid penalties go to collections with costs added.
- **Investigations (7.24.170):** SDCI may investigate on its own initiative or on complaint, including **building-wide and company-wide** investigations. It may demand documents and serve interrogatories. Reporter identity is kept confidential. The landlord can be ordered to post or deliver notice of the investigation in tenants' primary languages. Investigations must begin within three years. Civil limitation periods are tolled during investigation.
- **Subpoenas:** the Hearing Examiner issues them on SDCI's affidavit without a hearing. A landlord who ignores a subpoena cannot later use the withheld records on appeal. The City Attorney may seek court enforcement.
- **Interim relief:** the Director may order a deposit or bond covering estimated penalties and tenant remedies while the investigation runs.
- **Voluntary compliance agreements** must include full reimbursement plus interest to all harmed tenants, and cannot be offered to a landlord who has had two in the past three years.
- **Findings (7.24.180):** a written Determination of No Violation or an Order of Investigative Findings stating amounts due, corrective action, and monitored compliance.
- **Remedies (7.24.190):** for prohibited fees, full reimbursement plus interest plus **double the fees**. If records are inadequate, the Director may estimate amounts by "just and reasonable inference," including representative tenant testimony. Civil penalties $750 first and $1,500 subsequent per violation, $5,000 for retaliation, and $5,000 for obstructing the Director or Hearing Examiner. Penalties may be cut **75% if the tenant remedy is paid within 10 days** or **50% within 15 days**, and not at all after that. Settlements should not fall below the tenant remedy. The City may recover its enforcement costs, including attorney fees.
- **Appeals:** to the Hearing Examiner within 15 days. De novo hearing with SDCI bearing the burden by a preponderance. Failure to appear means the order stands. Judicial review by writ in King County Superior Court within 30 days.
- **Collection (7.24.230, 7.24.240):** for noncompliance, the Director may require public notice of the landlord's failure to comply, refer to collections, or refer to the City Attorney. All amounts due are a **debt owed to the City**. Tenant remedies the City collects are held in trust and paid to tenants. Final orders can be entered as judgments in Municipal Court with the order as prima facie or conclusive evidence.

**Fiscal note (Summary and Fiscal Note, SDCI)**
- 3.0 new FTE (one senior planning and development specialist, two code compliance analysts) for intake, investigations, and landlord assistance.
- Estimated other-fund costs: $915,203 in 2027, then $795,697 a year. Total 2027 cost about $1.03 million excluding overhead.
- Funded by a **new rental regulatory fee of about $11 per rental unit**, collected with Rental Registration and Inspection Ordinance billing every two years. Projected revenue: $1.36M (2027), $791K (2028), $1.44M (2029), $874K (2030).
- $60,000 of 2026 General Fund underspend covers IT changes to bill the fee.
- $100,000 one time for translated materials and in-language outreach (renter's handbook, ethnic media, bus ads, website refresh), plus $65,000 a year for ongoing outreach.
- Hearing Examiner absorbs subpoena review and appeals within existing resources.
- Equity data cited: 78% of Black, 71% of Hispanic or Latino, 66% of Native American, and 56% of AAPI households rent, against 51% of White households. Median renter income is $74,580 against $151,430 for owners.

**Regulatory context (presentation).** Builds on the state Residential Landlord-Tenant Act (RCW 59.18, 1973), Ordinance 125222 (2016, deposit and move-in fee limits and installment payment), Ordinance 125337 (2017, information packet), and Ordinance 126803 (2023, late fee cap).

## Relevance
- **Real Rent design.** Seattle's structure lines up with Real Rent's goals but goes further in four places: a closed list of permitted mandatory fees with any unlisted fee presumptively unfair, a short standardized disclosure form placed before the lease, a requirement to unbundle bundled services, and a banned list that covers pet fees, roommate change fees, mail and package fees, and payment-method fees. Compare with [[Rockville Chapter 18 Rewrite - Adopted Ordinance Text]] § 18-163, which uses an enumerated list plus informed opt-in.
- **Enforcement without depending on a private lawsuit.** Seattle has a private right of action because Washington law allows it, but the core of this ordinance is administrative: investigations, subpoenas through a hearing officer, orders of restitution plus double fees, penalties, and conversion of unpaid amounts into a debt owed to the City and held in trust for tenants. That administrative path is the part most relevant to Frederick after *Aleti v. Metropolitan Baltimore*. Whether Frederick's Charter and Maryland's municipal infraction framework support each element is an open question.
- **Records presumption.** The three-year records rule with a presumption of violation when records are missing is a strong, low-cost enforcement tool not in Frederick's draft.
- **Cure-and-repay incentive.** The 30-day cure defense and the 75%/50% penalty waiver for fast restitution are a practical way to get money back to tenants without litigation.
- **Funding model.** A per-unit regulatory fee billed through existing rental registration pays for dedicated staff. Frederick's rental licensing revenue already ran well above projection in FY2026 (see [[Frederick Affordability Agenda 2026-2029]]).
- **Language access.** The model disclosure form translated into the five most common languages is directly relevant to Frederick's immigrant renters.
- **Not directly portable:** Washington law, Seattle's Hearing Examiner, and its Municipal Court have no exact Frederick equivalents. Late fee and deposit rules differ because Maryland regulates both at the state level.
