# Make Caring Cool Again -- The Strong Towns Case for Maintenance Over Novelty

## Summary

Maintenance and care are the unglamorous core of local government work, but culture and media incentives systematically devalue them in favor of novelty -- meanwhile the suburban experiment has left towns with aging, expensive infrastructure and a revenue structure too dependent on state and federal aid to be stable.

## Key Points

**The core argument:** Local government is fundamentally in the business of maintenance and care -- educating kids daily, preventing incidents, keeping roads drivable. None of that generates clicks. Failures do. That negativity bias, amplified by social media incentives, distorts the public's understanding of what government actually does and what it costs to do it well.

**The fiscal trap:** The author's town of Fairhaven, MA gets 40% of its revenue from state or federal aid -- high enough that the town's financial fate is tied to forces outside its control. The suburban infrastructure build-out created long-term maintenance obligations that current revenue structures cannot reliably fund. Tax overrides are band-aids, not solutions.

**The two-sided response Strong Towns recommends:**
- Revenue side: add to the tax rolls through incremental neighborhood densification ("thickening") -- more housing on existing infrastructure without adding new infrastructure to maintain.
- Infrastructure side: shrink the maintenance footprint where possible. Replace underused asphalt with cheaper materials. Extend tree buffers. Install rain gardens. Narrow lanes (which also improve safety). Stop maintaining infrastructure that serves almost no one.

**The political reframe:** Officials making hard budget cuts are "sorting out the mess made by decision-makers long dead" -- people who cut ribbons on shiny projects that looked good at the time but created generational maintenance obligations. Novelty was rewarded; the bill is now due.

## Why This Matters for César

This is a direct articulation of the Strong Towns framework applied to a town in fiscal stress -- and Frederick is not immune to the same dynamics. The piece makes two moves that are directly useful in the housing production conversation:

First, it names incremental densification as a fiscal strategy, not just a housing strategy. More units on existing streets generate tax revenue without adding infrastructure the city must maintain. That's an argument for housing production targets that doesn't lead with affordability or equity -- it leads with financial resilience. That framing may land differently with skeptics like Peter Brehm.

Second, it frames maintenance as the unglamorous but essential work of government -- which is a useful frame for budget conversations where capital projects get attention and operating costs get cut. Worth keeping in the back pocket for the FY2027 budget season.
