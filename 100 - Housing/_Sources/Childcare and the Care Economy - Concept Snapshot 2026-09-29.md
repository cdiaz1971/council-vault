# Childcare and the Care Economy

## Frederick

- **Cost.** Full-time care for an infant and a preschooler averages $23,808 a year in Frederick County, 16.9% of the median family income of $140,657.
  - Weekly full-time rates for infants (0–23 months) are $410 in family child care and $287 in centers. For ages 2–4, they're $328 and $262.
  - At Maryland averages, the ALICE survival budget puts childcare at $1,538 a month for a family with an infant and a preschooler, more than housing.

  ([[Frederick Childcare statistics 2025.pdf]]; [[ALICE Report -- Frederick County 2025 (2023 Data) -- Reference Note]])
- **Supply.**
  - The County has 260 registered family child care providers (capacity 1,991) and 116 licensed centers (7,563).
  - Only 36 providers are licensed for infants, with 392 infant slots, against 9,874 children aged 0–2.
  - There are 7 Head Start programs (130 slots) and no Early Head Start.
  - The City's ZIP codes (21701–21704) hold 146 of the family providers and 59 of the centers.
  - Family providers fell from 297 in 2020 to 260 in 2024 and are projected to keep declining, while full-day centers are projected to grow.
  - The Maryland Family Network/Upfront map does not classify Frederick County as a childcare desert.

  ([[Frederick Childcare statistics 2025.pdf]]; [[Frederick County Childcare Landscape -- Background Reference]])
- **Demand.** Of 101 children whose families used Maryland Family Network's LOCATE referral service in 2023–24:
  - 62 sought infant or toddler care
  - 88 needed care because of a parent's job
  - 83 needed full-time care

  A 2023 study cited in the ALICE report found that 80% of County families rely on childcare to work, 38% say it adds more than 30 minutes to their commute, and 46% spend more than $1,200 a month ([[Frederick Childcare statistics 2025.pdf]]; [[ALICE Report -- Frederick County 2025 (2023 Data) -- Reference Note]]).
- **Scholarships.** The state Child Care Scholarship program spent $11.0 million on 1,365 County children in FY2024. 61 family providers (23%) and 42 centers (36%) accept scholarship children. State funding rose to $218 million in FY2024 and $270 million in FY2025 ([[Frederick Childcare statistics 2025.pdf]]).
- **Workforce pay.** Family child care providers average $37,015 in gross income, center teachers $29,733, and center aides $17,400, against $77,537 for County public school teachers ([[Frederick Childcare statistics 2025.pdf]]).
- **What the City can do.** The City doesn't set rates, license providers, or run the subsidy program; those sit with the state and County. César's synthesis identifies City tools anyway:
  - **Direct:** zoning for center siting and home providers, permit-fee waivers for new facilities, and its own employee benefits.
  - **Partner or fund:** buying reserved slots from licensed providers, lending City facilities to nonprofit providers, convening employers, coordinating with the County, and helping eligible families enroll in scholarships.
  - **Advocacy:** state pre-K, provider subsidy rates, and federal childcare funding.

  ([[Childcare and Care Economy -- Frederick Peer City Research and Council Options]]) Planning-stage childcare items are tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].

## Childcare and housing stability

- Children under five are the most commonly evicted group in the US. Young parents early in their careers face low wages and high childcare costs at the same time, so lowering childcare costs frees income for rent ([[Childcare as Housing Stability Issue Universal Childcare Momentum]]).

## The public-good and funding case

- **A policy failure, not a market failure.** Demos puts average US childcare costs at $13,128 per child a year. Providers run on thin margins while the mostly Black and Brown women in the workforce stay among the lowest-paid workers. It argues only federal investment can make care universal ([[Child Care as a Public Good -- Demos on the Case for Sustainable Federal Funding]]).
- **Affordability of a Nordic system.** A People's Policy Project paper estimates a Nordic-style US childcare system would cost 1.17% of GDP today, down from 3.05% in the 1970s, because of wage growth and lower fertility ([[US-Nordic Childcare Gap -- A Nordic System Would Cost 1.17 Percent of GDP Today]]).
- **State action.** New Mexico launched universal childcare in November 2025, saving families an estimated $12,000 a year. New York leaders are pushing for similar programs, and four states and DC offer universal pre-K ([[Childcare as Housing Stability Issue Universal Childcare Momentum]]).
- **Childcare as infrastructure.** Seward, Alaska had 20 slots for more than 80 children after closures. It put a $1 million cruise-line donation into childcare capacity instead of docks or roads, and is now pursuing a cruise tourism tax as permanent funding. Experts caution that private windfalls don't replace sustained public money ([[Treating Child Care as Infrastructure Funding]]).

## Care workers

- **Access depends on jobs.** Demos argues that care affordability and care-job quality are the same problem. Public investment needs to be paired with worker power: wage and quality standards, labor rights, and voice. Michigan, California, and Minnesota link care funding to worker standards ([[Worker Power as Care System Infrastructure -- Demos Explainer 2026]]).
- **Frederick's version.** César's synthesis notes that care workers in District 2 are disproportionately women of color, many of them immigrants, who face the same ALICE squeeze as the families they serve. It proposes making provider wage standards a condition of any City-funded slots or facility partnerships ([[Childcare and Care Economy -- Frederick Peer City Research and Council Options]]).

## Peer models

- **Austin.** The city's Economic Development Department runs a Workforce Development and Child Care division that invests in childcare supply, especially in childcare deserts. It treats childcare as workforce infrastructure and supports home-based providers through a Family Child Care Educator Network ([City of Austin](https://www.austintexas.gov/economic-development/divisions/workforce-development-and-child-care)).
