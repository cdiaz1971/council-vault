# The Radical Simplicity of Housing Abundance

## Summary
Lubeck responds to Matthew Yglesias's YIMBY essay, arguing housing abundance is fundamentally about restoring the right to build on already-zoned land, and that academic NIMBY opposition survives mainly by keeping land use politicized rather than offering a credible alternative.

## Key Points
Yglesias's core claim: if land is zoned for development and the owner wants to build housing on it, they should be allowed to, subject only to genuine safety and environmental review. Lubeck frames this as a property rights restoration, not a novel policy idea, and notes no US jurisdiction actually works this way today.

He argues opposition to housing abundance is concentrated among academics who have little practical building experience and therefore find their influence tied entirely to keeping land use decisions political. Serkin and Sharfstein's proposal that government simply build more housing is dismissed as ignoring a century of evidence that governments are poor at construction, cost control, and long-term property management.

Lubeck highlights a recurring contradictory pattern: chronic NIMBYs argue simultaneously that reforms are too radical (will "turn cities into Manhattan") and too modest (inconsequential), and treats the contradiction itself as proof the objections are not substantive. He frames the underlying stakes as binary: either people have the right to build on their own zoned land or they do not.

## Relevance
This lands directly in the middle of the Housing Production Target Resolution and LMC review work. The property rights framing (depoliticizing land use rather than treating each project as a political fight) is a useful rhetorical tool for the LMC Review Commission conversation, and the "too radical / too modest" contradiction pattern is worth watching for in local opposition to the Housing Production Target itself.
