# Residential Mobility as a Progressive Policy Frame

## Summary

Lubeck argues, building on Yoni Appelbaum's book Stuck, that progressives should reclaim "mobility" to mean the freedom to move toward opportunity rather than transit, and that housing abundance, metro densification, and an aligned YIMBY and New Urbanist movement are how to restore it.

## Key Points

In planning circles "mobility" almost always means transportation. Lubeck wants the word to carry Appelbaum's meaning instead. Being stuck means losing choices, and in Appelbaum's account those choices depend on how easily a person can pick up and relocate to a better job, school, or community.

The data behind the frame is stark. Roughly one in three Americans moved each year in the nineteenth century, about one in five as late as 1970, and around one in thirteen now. Those who can still move toward opportunity are increasingly the affluent and well educated. For everyone else, high housing costs in the destination cancel out the better job.

Lubeck offers three levers. First, housing abundance, so that both renters and buyers hold the market power that comes with surplus supply. He points to nineteenth century urban moving days, when large shares of a city relocated at once and usually traded up. Second, densification at the metro scale, which he treats as the same project as unit level abundance viewed from farther out. He argues that current planning practice is effectively suburban planning and that anti-density environmentalism harms the environment. Third, institutions committed to mobility, with YIMBY and New Urbanism as the leading candidates that he thinks should merge or at least align.

He also claims that modern gentrification is a product of artificial scarcity. In his view, price allocates scarce housing and money wins, so abundant supply where people want to live would prevent displacement in the modern sense. On transportation he argues that once a place is dense, mobility infrastructure can be cheap, citing simple bicycle boulevard networks in Portland, Seattle, and Berkeley.

Politically he sees "mobility" as a more specific and actionable message than the broader Abundance thesis. It appeals to an American tradition of choosing one's own community, and he notes Appelbaum's observation that opposition to mobility clusters in progressive jurisdictions.

## Relevance

This frame fits the housing production and tenant protection work and gives a values language that links housing supply to opportunity and agency rather than to developers. It pairs with [[The Radical Simplicity of Housing Abundance]], Lubeck's earlier piece on restoring the right to build.

Two tensions are worth holding. The gentrification claim conflicts directly with [[Upzoning's Gentrification Paradox -- When More Housing Displaces the People It Should Help]], which documents displacement following upzoning, and the market-led approach sits on the opposite side of [[Abundance Model vs Vienna Model -- Housing Politics Typology]] from social housing. The vocabulary also collides with [[Mobility vs Accessibility -- The Urban Planning Distinction]] in Parks and Infrastructure, where mobility means travel speed. Adopting Lubeck's sense would require stating clearly which meaning is in play. [[Access to Opportunity Is Civic Infrastructure -- Transit, Housing, and Micromobility as Democratic Preconditions]] is the closest existing bridge between the two.
