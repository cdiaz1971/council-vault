# Cleveland Home for Every Neighbor Rapid Rehousing Model

## Summary

Cleveland's A Home for Every Neighbor program moved over 200 unsheltered people from street to apartment in an average of 11 days by building direct landlord relationships and providing wraparound support, exceeding its goals nearly a year ahead of schedule.

## Key Points

**Program origin and structure.** Mayor Justin Bibb launched the program in February 2024 with $2M from the city general fund and $2.7M in federal COVID-era rental assistance. Goal: house 150 unsheltered people within 18 months. The program deliberately targets people who won't use shelters -- those with pets, opposite-sex partners, mental illness, addiction, or trauma histories that make congregate shelter unworkable. It launched as a special project in the mayor's office and has since moved to the Department of Public Health. Partners include the Northeast Ohio Coalition for the Homeless (NEOCH) and Downtown Cleveland Alliance.

**The 11-day process.** Outreach workers approach camp sites with water and basic supplies, explain the program, and build a list of interested participants. A unit acquisition specialist then finds rental options matching each person's needs (solo or shared, pet-friendly, no-stairs, etc.). Participants choose from options via iPad at the site, sign the lease with guided support, and a faith-based charity (I'm In Ministry!) furnishes the unit. A caseworker then drives them to their new home. Average time from first engagement to keys: 11 days.

**Results as of early 2026.** The program housed over 200 people. Unsheltered population in Cleveland dropped from ~300 to ~100. The goal of 150 people was hit in April 2025 -- nearly a year ahead of schedule. City Council unanimously approved two more years of funding at $1.9M/year. Private donations added $530,000.

**Exit outcomes (March 2026 progress report):** 35 in permanent supportive housing with EDEN vouchers; 21 in jail or prison when leases expired; 18 have unknown whereabouts but have not re-entered shelter or street systems; 8 returned to homelessness; 6 found other housing independently; 6 died (mostly natural causes). The 8 returns to homelessness out of ~90 exited participants is a strong outcome by national standards.

**The no-criminalization choice.** Service providers in Cleveland explicitly ruled out the use of arrest threats as a tool to move people off the streets -- a choice that distinguishes this program from approaches in other cities. The program relies entirely on voluntary participation.

**Ongoing challenges.** Expected federal funding restrictions starting in 2026-27 could shrink the exit options available when 12-month subsidies end. The program is also wrestling with flexible subsidy length -- some people may need only 3-6 months, others 18-24. And the underlying driver remains unsolved: rents in Greater Cleveland are rising at more than twice the national average.

## Relevance

Frederick doesn't have a visible unsheltered homelessness problem at Cleveland's scale, but the program's design principles are transferable: voluntary engagement, pet/family-inclusive eligibility, landlord relationship-building as the operational core, and wraparound case management. The cost point is notable -- Ward 7 Council Member Austin Davis described it as "the equivalent of paving four streets" for the city budget.

The program also reframes the shelter-first vs. housing-first debate. People who are shelter-resistant are not necessarily services-resistant -- they often have specific legitimate reasons for avoiding congregate settings. Cleveland's model meets them where they are rather than requiring them to conform to an intake system designed for a different population.
