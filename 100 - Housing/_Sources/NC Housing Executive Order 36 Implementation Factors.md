# NC Housing Executive Order 36 Implementation Factors

## Overview

North Carolina Governor Stein issued Executive Order 36, "Increasing Housing Opportunities for All North Carolinians," in response to the state's housing shortage driven by population growth. The CityBuild NC newsletter (written by someone with firsthand experience in the Cooper administration) argues the EO has real potential but that its impact hinges entirely on implementation — not the policy text itself.

## Three Factors That Determine EO Impact

### 1. Internal Agency Buy-In

State executive agencies report to the Governor, but rely heavily on permanent civil servants who predate the current administration. New top-down directives can face legitimate wariness: concerns about overstretching limited resources, unclear fit with existing priorities. When staff are genuinely excited about a policy, it moves faster and more ambitiously. Housing and land use policy is particularly difficult because it doesn't "live" anywhere at the state level outside the NC Housing Finance Agency's financing mandate — making cross-agency coordination harder to sustain.

### 2. Resource Allocation for Oversight

Governor's Office staff have limited time to coordinate with agencies and track EO implementation. New crises always emerge. The appointment of a Senior Advisor on Housing with dedicated bandwidth to focus on this issue is a positive signal — it suggests the EO won't get buried when priorities shift.

### 3. Sustained External Political Pressure

A key governing truth: internal support for a policy is not enough. For any initiative to be prioritized, political staff must hear about it from the outside — from advocates, organizations, and electeds. The writer notes that Governor Stein's willingness to issue the EO is partly a response to the organized pressure that already exists, and that sustained advocacy is what keeps implementation on track.

## Relevance to Frederick

This framework maps directly to how housing policy works at the local level. Executive or legislative action (like the Housing Production Target Resolution) creates the mandate, but implementation success depends on whether staff understand and own it, whether leadership is tracking it, and whether advocates keep the pressure on after the vote. The pattern holds regardless of scale.
