# What Zoning Codes Get Wrong About Families

## Summary

Zoning codes across the country attempt to define what a "family" is -- typically as a way to prevent groups of unrelated adults from sharing a home. These definitions are not only outdated but legally questionable, practically unenforceable, and rooted in exclusionary zoning history. Alli Thurmond Quinlan, an architect and incremental developer, argues governments should abandon this approach entirely and focus on things that actually affect neighborhoods.

## Key Points

The ostensible purpose of family definition in zoning is to prevent overcrowding or nuisance -- typically aimed at preventing college students from clustering in residential neighborhoods. But enforcement is a practical impossibility: proving whether people are or aren't family requires invasive documentation (DNA tests? marriage certificates? family trees?) that governments cannot and should not demand. The rule exists to make people feel comfortable, not because it functions.

The definition also fails on its own terms. Real families are fluid: foster children, blended families, unmarried couples, foreign exchange students, great-aunts who move in when their eyesight fails. These are common living situations that many codes declare illegal. The nuclear family assumption encoded into these rules was not historically normal and is increasingly out of step with how people actually live.

Quinlan's experience writing Fayetteville, Arkansas's ADU code is illustrative: family definition protections were retained because residents wanted to see them, even though the city attorney acknowledged they couldn't be enforced. That's the core critique -- these rules provide psychological comfort to neighbors who want exclusion, not legal or practical protection.

The alternative is to focus code enforcement energy on things that actually matter: noise, property maintenance, parking, density limits tied to infrastructure capacity -- not on who is or isn't legally a family.

## Relevance

Directly relevant to Frederick's LMC review. If Frederick's zoning code includes family definitions, this is a candidate for removal or reform -- it creates legal exposure and enforces exclusion without producing any legitimate land use benefit. The framing is also useful for broader zoning reform conversations: the argument is not "anything goes" but "regulate what you can actually regulate and what actually matters."
