# The Week in Housing -- Should We Oppose ADUs (Seomraí)

## Summary
Michael Byrne's weekly housing newsletter examines the Irish government's proposal to legalize accessory dwelling units (ADUs, called "Seomraí") without requiring planning permission. The piece is a careful cost-benefit analysis that ends up broadly supportive of the idea, while naming serious concerns around tenant rights and wealth inequality. The framing -- that imperfect supply-side measures can still improve outcomes even within a flawed system -- is useful for the Frederick housing context.

## Key Arguments

**The Case For ADUs**
The proposal would exempt ADUs from planning permission and extend rent-a-room tax relief to them. Supporters argue this could produce up to 350,000 new units by opening large gardens in established, infrastructure-rich areas. Byrne is persuaded by "abundance" thinking (Ezra Klein style): the instinct to block imperfect solutions because they fall short of systemic change is dangerous. Some housing is better than no housing. ADUs add typological diversity and supply responsiveness. A small unit beats living with parents or commuting long distances if that's the realistic alternative.

**The Concerns**

- *Landlord-tenant proximity*: When landlords and tenants share a site, privacy and autonomy suffer. Landlords may be intrusive or conflictual; tenants may feel surveilled or infantilized. Proximity also makes landlords more risk-averse and selective -- research shows they already screen tenants by employment, race, nationality, and benefit receipt, and close proximity amplifies this.
- *Licensee reclassification*: If ADU residents are classified as "licensees" rather than tenants, they lose all protections under the Residential Tenancies Act, including minimum tenure rights. This is a serious concern and a live political question.
- *Tax and wealth inequality*: ADU landlords would receive untaxed rental income (up to €14,000) while tenants pay taxed earnings to access housing. ADUs also increase property values for homeowners, widening the wealth gap. If residents have no security of tenure, you get a policy that simultaneously increases supply and entrenches housing inequality.

**The Theoretical Frame**
Byrne argues that political-economic critique (which reveals structural inequality) should inform long-term thinking but shouldn't be a veto on incremental improvements. Policies can work within an unjust system and still matter in people's daily lives. The goal is to advocate for systemic change while supporting measures that help people now.

## Relevance
The ADU framing translates directly to Frederick. The city has discussed accessory dwelling units and infill development as part of housing production. The analysis here -- supply gains vs. tenant protection vs. wealth effects -- is exactly the tension in the local debate. The licensee/tenant rights distinction is particularly important if Frederick ever considers ADU policy: what protections would apply?
