# Lancaster HPP 2024-2029 -- Comparative Reference for Housing Target Resolution

## Summary

Lancaster, MA adopted a Housing Production Plan (HPP) in November 2024 covering 2024-2029. Required under MGL Chapter 40B (Massachusetts state law), the plan establishes a data-driven production target of 295 units over five years, with detailed analysis of household growth, cost burden by income group, development constraints, and a pipeline-vs-goal gap analysis. The Massachusetts regulatory framework does not apply to Maryland, but the methodology, framing, and income-stratified structure offer strong drafting ammunition for Frederick's Housing Production Target Resolution.

## Key Points

### The Production Target Methodology

Lancaster derives its 295-unit target from three components: 210 units from projected household growth (2.3% annually since 2010), 77 units to correct a dangerously low vacancy rate of 2.4% (healthy range is 5-8%), and 8 units for replacement housing. This methodology is transparent, defensible, and grounded in data rather than aspiration. Frederick's resolution would benefit from adopting a similar build-up approach -- a specific number tied to specific drivers is harder to defer than a vague production goal.

### The 30-60% AMI Gap as a Structural Policy Failure

The plan identifies the 30-60% AMI band as the fastest-growing income group and among the most cost-burdened, yet the least served by existing programs. Lancaster's pipeline at the time of adoption was overwhelmingly tilted toward 60-80% AMI and market-rate households, leaving the 30-60% group with only 8% of 40R project units as support. This framing directly applies to Frederick: the workforce housing gap -- households earning between roughly $44,000 and $88,000 annually -- is where cost burden is concentrated and where production is weakest. Any resolution that sets income-stratified targets should call this band out explicitly.

### The Pipeline-vs-Target Gap Analysis

The plan compares active and tentative development projects against the production target, showing Lancaster at 6.3% subsidized housing inventory in 2024 and on a path to 10.2% by 2030 only if The Landing (a 146-unit 40R project) is completed. This pipeline dependency is a political vulnerability, and the plan names it clearly. Frederick's resolution should include an equivalent gap analysis: what is currently permitted or in review, what income levels does it serve, and how far does it fall short of the target?

### Safe Harbor as a Local Control Mechanism

Under Massachusetts law, a municipality with an adopted and state-approved HPP that is making measurable progress toward 10% subsidized housing can claim Safe Harbor -- the ability to deny comprehensive permits from developers. Maryland has no equivalent statute, but the underlying logic is applicable: a locally adopted production target gives the Council a policy basis for evaluating development proposals proactively rather than reactively. The resolution can function as Frederick's internal Safe Harbor equivalent, establishing a framework before the next contested project lands on the agenda.

### Development Constraint Mapping

Lancaster maps four categories of hard development constraints (protected open space, wetlands, floodways, government-owned land) and one advisory category (core habitat). The unconstrained buildable area is only 6,393 acres -- 36% of the town's total 17,910 acres. This constraint mapping is essential to making a production target credible: you cannot set a unit number without understanding where those units can actually go. Frederick's resolution process should include or reference a comparable constraints analysis, particularly given the Golden Mile corridor's infrastructure limitations and the mix of privately held, county-owned, and city-owned parcels in the housing opportunity areas.

### Strategies Worth Borrowing

Lancaster's seven strategies offer a useful checklist against which to evaluate Frederick's existing and proposed tools:

- Inclusionary zoning with a deeper affordability option at 60% AMI (analogous to Frederick's existing IZ provisions and any LMC Review Commission recommendations)
- Walkable mixed-use district zoning at a specific underutilized corridor (analogous to Golden Mile rezoning possibilities)
- Affordable Housing Trust capitalization through multiple sources including fee-in-lieu payments, surplus property sales, and free cash
- Community Preservation Act funding (Maryland has no direct equivalent, but PILOT agreements and developer community benefit contributions fill a similar function)
- Town land disposition via RFP with affordability requirements baked in (directly applicable to any city-owned parcels in the Golden Mile or elsewhere)
- Public utility capacity expansion as a prerequisite to housing production (relevant to any Golden Mile infrastructure gaps)
- Proactive engagement with large redevelopment sites to embed affordable housing goals before entitlements are issued

### Demographic Signals Relevant to Frederick

Lancaster's fastest-growing age cohort is 65+, driving demand for smaller, accessible units. The cost-burdened population is concentrated in the 0-80% AMI range and is racially and age diverse. Households above 100% AMI are predominantly white. These patterns are consistent with national trends and likely mirror Frederick's own demographic dynamics -- particularly in the Golden Mile corridor where lower-income Latino and immigrant households are concentrated and face the most acute cost burden.

## Relevance

This document is directly useful for three purposes in the Housing Target Resolution work. First, the production target methodology provides a template for how to calculate and justify Frederick's specific unit number -- grounding it in household growth projections, vacancy rates, and replacement needs rather than political negotiation. Second, the income-stratified analysis and the 30-60% AMI gap argument give César a framing for why a production target without income distribution requirements is insufficient. Third, the pipeline-vs-goal gap analysis structure can be adapted to show where Frederick's current pipeline leaves the city relative to any adopted target, which is the core policy argument for why the resolution is necessary now.
