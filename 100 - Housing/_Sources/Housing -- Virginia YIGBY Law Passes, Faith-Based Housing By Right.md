# Housing -- Virginia YIGBY Law Passes, Faith-Based Housing By Right

## Summary

Virginia passed HB 1279, making it the third state after California and Florida to enact a "Yes in God's Backyard" (YIGBY) law allowing housing on faith-owned land by right. The law takes effect January 1, 2027, with a sunset provision expiring January 1, 2031 unless made permanent by the General Assembly.

## Key Points

The bill passed the House 54-41 but narrowly failed in the Senate 23-26 in an earlier version. The final conference committee report reconciled House and Senate differences, landing on a January 2027 effective date and a four-year sunset. This built-in expiration is Virginia's signal that the legislature will need to affirmatively reauthorize the policy to make it permanent.

The policy allows religious institutions and nonprofits to build housing on land they own without needing rezoning approval from localities. HousingForward Virginia research found that 65% of faith-owned properties in Northern Virginia currently prohibit multifamily housing under local zoning. The sunset gives the General Assembly four years of data on actual housing production before locking the policy in.

Maryland has not yet acted on YIGBY, though Preservation Maryland published analysis in early 2026 identifying over 37,000 acres of faith-owned land in the state and pointing to HFV's Virginia research as a replication model. Maryland is described as still building the case.

The federal YIGBY Act (S.2720, Warner/Schatz/Young) would layer $50 million annually in grants on top of state laws, plus technical assistance for congregations navigating financing and permitting.

## Relevance

Maryland's proximity and HFV's direct comparison makes this immediately relevant. If Maryland introduces YIGBY legislation in 2027 (which Preservation Maryland's research sets up), Frederick would be an early implementation site. Faith-owned land in Frederick is worth mapping now. The sunset provision is also a useful policy design precedent -- building in a forced reauthorization creates accountability without locking in a policy before evidence accumulates.
