# Affordable Housing Cannot Pay for Itself: The Math Behind the Gap

## Summary

A worked financial model showing why affordable housing development has a structural funding gap that cannot be closed without public subsidy, regardless of developer intent or land speculation. The analysis uses a three-bedroom unit at 50% AMI to demonstrate that construction costs, financing, and operating expenses exceed what affordable rents can support -- even after accounting for federal tax credits and local property tax exemptions.

## Key Points

The central finding is simple: the cost to build affordable housing is greater than the revenue it can generate. For a three-bedroom unit affordable to a family at 50% AMI, the math does not work even before developer profit is considered. This structural shortfall is what the industry calls "the gap."

Closing the gap requires layering subsidies -- federal Low Income Housing Tax Credits (LIHTC), local Housing Trust Fund grants, property tax exemptions, philanthropic contributions, or cross-subsidy from market-rate units in mixed-income projects. Even stacking two of these (LIHTC plus property tax exemption) still leaves a funding shortfall on a typical project.

The implications push back against two common political narratives:

**"It's developer greed."** Remove all developer profit from the equation and the gap persists. The math is structural, not behavioral.

**"It's land speculation."** Even if land is acquired for free -- zero acquisition cost -- affordable three-bedroom units still cannot be financed from affordable rents alone. Land cost explains some of the difficulty in high-cost markets, but it is not the root cause of the gap.

What actually drives the gap: construction costs (materials, labor, permitting), financing costs (interest rates, required reserves), and the hard ceiling that 50% AMI rent places on revenue. All three are largely outside local government's direct control.

## Relevance

This note is foundational for the MAHTF work. Recommendation 2 in the MAHTF work outline calls for City Housing Fund capitalization based on the task force's own numbers -- but the political argument for that capitalization depends on being able to explain clearly why the fund is structurally necessary, not a nice-to-have. The gap analysis provides exactly that argument.

It also matters for the MPDU reform thread: if the in-lieu fee is set at $2.00/sq ft (the current Frederick rate) while the actual subsidy needed to close the gap on an affordable unit runs into the tens of thousands of dollars per unit, the fee is not functioning as a meaningful affordability mechanism. That disconnect is arguable from the gap analysis.
