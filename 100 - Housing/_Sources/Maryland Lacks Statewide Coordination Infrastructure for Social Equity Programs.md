# Maryland Lacks Statewide Coordination Infrastructure for Social Equity Programs

## Summary

Maryland's first Department of Social and Economic Mobility report finds that individual agencies run effective equity programs but the state has no central coordination function, shared data infrastructure, or evaluation framework to align those efforts -- a governance gap that mirrors what happens at the municipal level when equity work is siloed by department.

## Key Points

The Department of Social and Economic Mobility (DoSEM) was established in October 2025, consolidating three existing offices: the Office of Minority Business Enterprises (MDOT), the Office of Small, Minority, and Women Business Affairs, and the Office of Social Equity (Cannabis Administration). The department was championed by then-Speaker Adrienne Jones.

Its first report, released July 1, 2026, surveyed more than two dozen agencies running at least 150 programs with social equity components. The core finding: Maryland does not lack equity initiatives -- it lacks the governance, data, and coordination infrastructure to align and evaluate them across state government.

Four recommendations:
1. One office should oversee equity programs across all state agencies
2. A DoSEM representative should sit on all state boards, commissions, and advisory bodies
3. The existing Office of Social Equity (from the Cannabis Administration) should become the state's permanent statewide equity coordination office, operating under DoSEM's Secretary
4. Regular cross-agency forums to share program updates, flag overlaps, and surface coordination challenges

DoSEM Secretary Walter Simmons explicitly declined to be deflected by federal anti-DEI pressure, stating the department works for Marylanders. A second report is due to the governor and General Assembly by December 1, 2026, with recommendations for law and policy changes.

Six agencies reported no specific social equity programs: Department of Budget and Management, Department of Information Technology, Maryland Department of Planning, Office of Overdose Response, Governor's Appointments Office, and Secretary of State. Simmons pushed back on that framing, noting those agencies do equity-integrated work even if they don't administer direct service programs.

The report's recommended definition of social equity: "The consistent and systematic fair, just, and impartial treatment of all individuals, combined with deliberate and measurable efforts to identify, reduce, and eliminate disparities that have historically limited access to opportunity, resources, and participation in State programs."

## Relevance

The DoSEM diagnosis -- programs exist, coordination doesn't -- is directly applicable to how Frederick thinks about equity work at the city level. César's affordability work spans housing, food access, childcare, and utilities, all administered through different departments and funded through different mechanisms. The risk is the same: each department does its thing, no one has a cross-cutting view of cumulative impact on ALICE households, and programs that should reinforce each other instead operate in silos.

The DoSEM model of a central coordination function with cross-agency representation is worth tracking as a possible template. If Maryland gets the coordination infrastructure right, Frederick could eventually look to the state framework as a structure to align local programs with.

The December 2026 report with policy recommendations is worth monitoring -- it may produce state-level changes that create new municipal authority or funding pathways.
