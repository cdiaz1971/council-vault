# Fourth Amendment Challenge Threatens Source-of-Income Voucher Protections Nationwide

## Summary

A March 2026 New York State appellate ruling found the state's 2019 source-of-income (voucher) nondiscrimination law unconstitutional on Fourth Amendment grounds, because Section 8 requires landlord consent to unit inspections. The decision has emboldened landlords nationwide to mount similar constitutional challenges against source-of-income protection laws, with at least 12 such cases now underway across multiple states.

## Key Points

- The theory originated in a 2023 Cortland County, NY decision (People v. Commons West): the judge found that mandatory Housing Assistance Payment contract inspection consent violates a landlord's Fourth Amendment rights, even though inspections require landlord cooperation to occur and no example exists of forced entry to conduct one.
- The March 2026 Third Department appellate ruling upheld that reasoning, though its direct legal effect is narrow -- it covers only part of upstate New York, and the ruling was stayed after being appealed to the NY Court of Appeals in April 2026, so the 2019 law remains enforceable statewide for now. NY Attorney General Letitia James's office has appealed further.
- National Housing Law Project litigation director Eric Dunn disputes the court's factual premise: landlords control whether an inspection happens at all ("they can't send in a SWAT team"), so it should count as a "reasonable" search under the law.
- Twenty-four states and 150 counties or municipalities have source-of-income discrimination laws on the books; roughly 60% of voucher holders live somewhere covered by such protections (Heather Abraham, University at Buffalo School of Law).
- University at Buffalo has tracked 12 similar Fourth Amendment challenges nationwide since the 2023 decision, including in Lincoln, Nebraska, Chicago/Illinois (Moore v. Mac Property Management, ongoing), and Lawrence, Kansas (landlord challenge rejected in 2025).
- Effect described by Abraham as partly reputational: landlords are telling each other "we won," and legislators elsewhere are now hesitant to spend political capital on new source-of-income protections that could simply be struck down later.

## Relevance

Frederick has no source-of-income protection ordinance yet, but this is exactly the kind of constitutional exposure worth checking with Rachel Gray before drafting one as part of a broader tenant-protection package -- the Fourth Amendment theory is spreading well beyond New York and remains untested locally. See the companion note [[State Preemption Killed Kansas City's Voucher Nondiscrimination Ordinance]] for the other major failure mode facing this kind of ordinance.
