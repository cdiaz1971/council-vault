# Vibes-Based Planning -- When Cities Say They Support Housing but Vote Against It

## Summary

When resident David Modica stood up at a Marblehead, MA public meeting and asked whether the town was "kind of being pricks" by identifying a private golf course as its multifamily compliance zone under Massachusetts's 3A law, he named something that most housing advocates have been circling for years. Aaron Lubeck uses that moment to define "vibes-based planning": the pattern where officials make all the morally correct statements about affordability and sustainability while quietly structuring policy to do the opposite. The piece connects this to the YIMBY movement's origins and to the New Urbanist argument that abundant and quality housing are not mutually exclusive.

## Key Points

**Vibes-based planning defined.** Politicians say popular but unsubstantive things. What they say does not match what they do. Specifically: they claim to care about housing while voting against it. The decoupling of promise from action is the defining feature. Marblehead identified a private golf course -- which no one seriously believes will ever be redeveloped -- as its multifamily zone, technically complying with state law while ensuring nothing would ever be built.

**Why this persists.** Three structural conditions sustain it. First, relatively few people understand zoning and land-use policy well enough to recognize obstruction when it occurs. Second, collapsing local media amplifies vibes-based narratives and rarely holds officials accountable for outcomes like permitting times or units produced. Third, participatory planning processes have no filtering mechanism to distinguish civic concern from organized obstructionism -- and meetings are dominated by people with the time, wealth, and political fluency to resist newcomers.

**The YIMBY origin.** YIMBYism was not originally an ideological project -- it was a reaction against obvious civic hypocrisy. Young citizens attended hearings to point out that cities claiming to support affordability were systematically preventing it. Modica's comment fits squarely in that tradition.

**YIMBY and New Urbanism as complements.** Lubeck argues these are not opposing movements. YIMBYism answers whether housing should be allowed; New Urbanism answers what kind of housing should be built. America built beloved, walkable, working-class neighborhoods at scale -- triple-deckers, courtyard apartments, Baltimore rowhouses -- that are now illegal to replicate under current zoning. The false choice between quantity and quality has done enormous damage.

**Modica's core line:** "Any moment we spend bullshitting each other is just a sin."

## Relevance

This is directly applicable to Frederick's Housing Production Target Resolution and LMC reform. The central risk is that Frederick produces a resolution that performs commitment to housing production while the zoning and approval structures remain unchanged. The test of whether the resolution is vibes-based or not is: does it change what gets built, where, at what density, and at what speed? Numbers without mechanism changes are vibes.

The piece is also useful for how to talk publicly about the housing work. Modica's framing -- "people live in houses; are we trying to make sure we don't build no houses?" -- is more effective public communication than technical affordability arguments. It translates the abstract into the moral.
