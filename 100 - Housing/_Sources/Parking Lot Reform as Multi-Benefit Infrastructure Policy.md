# Parking Lot Reform as Multi-Benefit Infrastructure Policy

## Summary

Two parallel parking reform tracks -- reducing mandated parking quantities and improving parking surface quality -- address the same underlying problem from different angles and should be understood as a coherent package. Together they reduce heat, stormwater runoff, housing costs, and land waste simultaneously.

## Key Points

Cities that have eliminated parking minimums (Buffalo, Austin, Minneapolis) are already reducing how much land is converted to impermeable pavement. But the existing stock of parking -- which in many downtowns occupies 25%+ of land area -- is not going away quickly. Surface material reform addresses the existing stock.

The stormwater and heat island benefits of permeable parking accrue only if the lot exists. The housing affordability benefits of parking minimum elimination accrue only if developers stop building excess parking. The two reforms together produce complementary outcomes: fewer new lots, and better performance from existing ones.

NC Parking Reform HB369 explicitly links parking to stormwater -- noting that parking minimums produce impermeable surfaces that increase runoff costs for cities. This is the same analytical frame.

The Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act (signed May 26, 2026) restricts parking minimums near transit. Frederick's own LMC reform process provides an opportunity to align with that direction and add surface quality standards for any parking that does get built.

## Relevance

Frederick's LMC modernization and parking reform threads can both be advanced with this framing. The argument that parking reform is environmental infrastructure -- not just a housing production or development tool -- broadens the coalition that can support it. Public works staff, stormwater management staff, and environmental advocates are all potential allies for the surface quality piece, even if they are not engaged in the quantity/minimums debate.
