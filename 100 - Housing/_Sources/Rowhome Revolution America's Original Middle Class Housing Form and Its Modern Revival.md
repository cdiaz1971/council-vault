# Rowhome Revolution: America's Original Middle-Class Urban Housing Form and Its Modern Revival

## Summary

A sweeping historical and contemporary argument for the rowhome as America's original middle-class housing form, tracing its disappearance through 20th-century zoning and its potential revival via companies like the American Housing Corporation in Austin.

## Key Points

The rowhome dominated American urban housing from roughly 1790 through the mid-20th century. The Federal style -- codified in Asher Benjamin's widely circulated pattern books -- enabled speculative builders to produce block after block of attached homes on narrow lots, sharing party walls and bulk materials. Philadelphia became "City of Homes" through this model; the 1893 World's Columbian Exposition advertised a model Philadelphia rowhome at $2,500 (under $100K today). Philadelphia's high homeownership rate was credited at the time with producing a more "contented labor force" -- a politically salient observation.

The form's disappearance was not organic. Post-WWII zoning codes imposed setback requirements, minimum lot sizes, parking mandates, and density limits that made the continuous attached street wall illegal in most American cities. Where townhomes are permitted today, they are typically detached or semi-detached and organized around garages rather than sidewalks.

The contemporary revival case study is the American Housing Corporation (AHC) in Austin. AHC is a vertically integrated manufacturer targeting a $750K price point for family-sized rowhomes in walkable Central Austin neighborhoods where detached single-family homes often exceed $1M. AHC fabricates fiberglass-reinforced cement and steel panels, delivers them in shipping containers, and assembles on-site with a five-person crew. Current capacity is 12 homes/year; a larger facility opening in 2027 targets 1,200. JPMorgan Chase estimates this construction method reduces costs 20-30%. Austin's recent zoning reforms -- legalizing three homes by right on single-family lots, eliminating parking minimums, reducing setbacks -- made AHC's model possible. Houston's reduction of minimum lot sizes to 1,400 sq ft produced 30,000+ townhomes and kept it the most affordable large city in America.

Puzycki frames this explicitly in terms of republican political tradition: the rowhome as architecture embodying ordered liberty, wealth-building for ordinary citizens, and the conviction that housing affordability is a political variable, not a natural condition.

## Relevance

This essay does a lot of work for Frederick housing politics. The "contented labor force" framing is a historically grounded version of the argument that homeownership rates and civic stability are linked -- useful for cross-partisan coalition-building without relying on progressive framing. The AHC model (vertically integrated, prefab, targeting the $750K gap) is a direct proof-of-concept for what zoning reform enables. The pattern-book logic -- standardized, replicable, neighborhood-scaled -- maps directly onto the missing-middle and manufactured housing conversations already in the vault.

The essay also implicitly indicts the zoning codes that created the missing middle problem: not just as a housing issue, but as a deliberate dismantling of a working civic ecosystem. That framing connects to [[Populist Planning and Cultural Housing Aspirations]] and the Imagination Problem note.

Worth watching: AHC expansion to Seattle and Reno. If this model scales, it becomes a concrete alternative to large-complex multifamily as the primary supply mechanism -- which matters for Frederick's political geography.
