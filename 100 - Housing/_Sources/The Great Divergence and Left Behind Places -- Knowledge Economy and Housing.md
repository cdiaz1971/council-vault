# The Great Divergence and Left Behind Places -- Knowledge Economy and Housing

## Summary

Second in a series examining how the knowledge economy produces a spatial 'double divide' -- intra-urban inequality and a growing gap between thriving cities and economically stagnant 'left behind places' -- with implications for housing, populism, and democratic resilience.

## Key Points

- The transition to the information economy has widened the productivity gap between high-skill, high-density cities and regions with outdated industry mixes -- an "internal brain drain" compounds this as educated workers leave for opportunity elsewhere
- Moretti argues that the geographic sorting of Americans by education level magnifies socioeconomic divergence in health, life expectancy, and income -- not just wages
- The divergence is not just economic but political and cultural: voting patterns increasingly track the urban/rural, hub/heartland divide; Wilkinson notes there is "no such thing as a Republican city" anymore
- Michael Lind's "new class war" framing -- hubs (knowledge economy cities) vs heartlands (suburbs, smaller towns) -- maps partisan geography as a proxy for class conflict, particularly on trade, immigration, and environmental policy
- The "metropolitan elite" critique (from writers like Goodwin) is largely a caricature, but it points to a real resentment at inequality between globally integrated cities and places left behind
- Next installment in the series will address how housing functions as both cause and consequence of inter-regional inequality

## Relevance

Frederick sits in a complicated position relative to this analysis: close enough to the DC metro economy to benefit from knowledge-sector spillover, but with a working and middle class that feels the squeeze of proximity without the full benefit. The "left behind" dynamics play into why housing production targets matter -- Frederick needs to build for the population that keeps the city's own services running, not just for those who can afford market-rate proximate to DC. The political polarization framing is also useful: understanding why residents resist density is partly about hub/heartland cultural identity, not just NIMBYism. Part III of this series (housing as cause and consequence) should be captured when it arrives.
