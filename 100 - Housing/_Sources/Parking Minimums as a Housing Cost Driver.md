# Parking Minimums as a Housing Cost Driver

Mandatory off-street parking requirements are one of the most direct mechanisms by which local zoning inflates the cost of housing and commercial development. The economics are straightforward and the reform evidence is mounting.

## The Cost Math

A single surface parking space costs roughly $10,000 to construct. A structured garage space runs $40,000 or more. These costs are not absorbed by developers -- they are passed through to tenants and buyers. Every unit of housing built above a parking podium carries embedded parking cost regardless of whether the resident owns a car. For affordable housing, childcare centers, and small commercial tenants, mandatory parking requirements can make otherwise viable projects infeasible.

Parking requirements also consume land. In dense infill contexts, the land cost of satisfying a minimum parking requirement often exceeds the cost of construction. The result is that mandatory parking minimums effectively set a floor on development density and a ceiling on affordability.

## How Requirements Actually Work

Most parking minimums are calculated by use type: residential units get a per-bedroom or per-unit requirement; commercial space gets a per-square-foot requirement; restaurants are sometimes calculated per chair. Madison, Wisconsin requires one off-street space per home as the baseline, with commercial requirements layered on top. These formulas encode assumptions about car ownership and travel behavior that were often wrong when written and are increasingly wrong now, particularly in walkable or transit-adjacent neighborhoods.

## Washington State's Reform Approach (SB 5184, 2025)

Washington passed one of the strongest statewide parking reform laws in the country in 2025. Rather than tying exemptions to proximity to transit -- the approach taken by Oregon and California, which generated endless political fights about whether transit was "good enough" -- Washington reframed the debate around specific use cases:

- Homes under 1,200 square feet (covering most apartments, ADUs, and middle housing) are fully exempt
- Commercial spaces under 3,000 square feet are exempt
- Ground-floor retail in mixed-use buildings is exempt
- Childcare centers, subsidized affordable housing, senior housing, and buildings changing use are all exempt

The transit-proximity framing was politically vulnerable because it required adjudicating the quality of transit service. The use-case framing asked a simpler question: should parking rules prevent a daycare from opening? Should they make affordable housing too expensive to build? Those questions have defensible answers that don't depend on bus frequency.

Critically, Washington's law does not ban parking. Developers remain free to include as much parking as they want. What changed is who decides -- the market, not the code.

## Madison's Situation

Madison is considering similar reform, particularly for missing middle housing. Its current one-space-per-unit residential minimum is a direct constraint on small multifamily development: fourplexes and small apartment buildings become significantly harder to pencil when each unit requires a dedicated off-street space on an infill lot. Reform advocates are framing the issue as a housing production question, not a parking question -- the same reframe that worked in Washington.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick's LMC is under active review. Parking minimums belong on the table. The Washington State model gives Frederick a tested reform template that sidesteps the transit-proximity debate -- which would be politically complicated in a city that lacks robust transit -- and instead targets specific housing types and uses where parking requirements demonstrably constrain production. Affordable housing, childcare centers, and small commercial tenants are all sympathetic cases that frame the issue correctly.
