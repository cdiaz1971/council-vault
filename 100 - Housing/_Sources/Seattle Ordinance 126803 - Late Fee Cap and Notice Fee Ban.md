# Seattle Ordinance 126803 - Late Fee Cap and Notice Fee Ban

## Summary
Ordinance 126803 (Council Bill 120541), sponsored by Councilmember Kshama Sawant, added SMC 7.24.034. It passed the City Council April 18, 2023 and was filed May 1, 2023, effective 30 days after mayoral approval. It replaced an earlier bill, CB 120530, which covered only late fees. The 2026 fee ordinance ([[Seattle Ordinance 127497 - Rental Fee Disclosure, Prohibited Fees, and Enforcement]]) incorporates this cap into its list of permitted fees. Copies are in Google Drive under City council / 02 - Legislation / Tenant Protection.

## Key Points

**What the ordinance does (SMC 7.24.034)**
- A late rent fee may not exceed **$10 per month**. No other fee may be charged for late payment, including for serving any notice required by state law.
- A landlord may not charge **any fee for issuing a notice** to a tenant, including late notices, pay-or-vacate notices, and notices of lease noncompliance.
- SDCI must publish a notice explaining the rule, with links to translations in the five most common non-English languages in Seattle, updated annually, and provide free copies to owners on request.

**Findings the Council adopted**
- Rents in Seattle and King County have risen and affordable vacancies are scarce.
- King County's Regional Affordable Housing Task Force (2018, revised 2019) found renting raises the risk of severe cost burden and set a goal to preserve access for renters through tenant protections.
- *Losing Home* (Seattle Women's Commission and King County Bar Association Housing Justice Project, 2018): eviction is a leading cause of homelessness, 51.7% of evicted tenants were people of color, 86.5% of filings were for nonpayment, and **52% of those were for one month's rent or less**.
- GAO-20-433: a $100 rent increase is associated with a 9% higher homelessness rate.
- Average hourly earnings fell 3% from July 2021 to July 2022 (BLS). Renters are skipping utilities, food, and medical care to pay rent.
- **Peer caps cited:** Redmond and unincorporated King County limit late fees to 1.5% of monthly rent. Auburn limits them to $10 a month. The CFPB had proposed capping credit card late fees at $8.
- Some landlords charge "notice preparation" or "notice service" fees each time a notice issues, required or not, which pile up as further debt for low-income and fixed-income tenants.

**Central Staff analysis (April 5, 2023 memo)**
- No explicit regulation of notice fees existed, and it was unclear how many landlords charged them or how much. The real cost of a notice ranges from printing and postage to sheriff service or a management company's fee.
- Changes from CB 120530: the notice fee ban was broadened from late-payment notices to all notices, and a requirement that pay-or-vacate notices separate unpaid rent from late fees was dropped, though SDCI was still directed to do rulemaking on notice of rights.
- **Alternatives staff laid out:** ban all notice fees, ban only certain ones, limit fees to the landlord's actual cost, or set a dollar ceiling.

**Fiscal note and enforcement capacity**
- No budget amendment. SDCI enforces through its Property Owner and Tenant Assistance (POTA) group.
- POTA intake rose from 2,399 (2019) to 2,799 (2020), 3,271 (2021), and 4,847 (2022). Average complaint wait times rose from 13.6 days (2019) to 15.7, 21.0, and 25.5 days (2022). Emergencies, eviction notices, and lockouts are triaged first, then time-sensitive items such as rent increases, then everything else.
- SDCI said one more code compliance analyst (about $138,000 fully loaded) would help, but staff could not say how much it would shorten response times.
- Equity statement: capping late and notice fees could reduce debt and eviction records that fall hardest on Black, Indigenous, and other communities of color.

**Sponsor's case (Sawant office FAQ)**
- Renter reports included $250 charged the moment rent is late with a second $250 days later, $40 to $50 per day clauses, $100 late fees on a Section 8 household, and late-notice delivery fees on top of late fees.
- A Social Security recipient paid on the second Wednesday reported a perpetual $50 late fee because the landlord refused a mid-month due date, totaling over $3,600.
- **Why a flat $10 instead of a percentage:** the Seattle Times editorial board argued for a 1.5% cap as fairer across rent levels. The sponsor responded that 1.5% exceeds $10 for any rent above $667, that almost no Seattle units rent below $700, only 2% rent below $1,000, and 55% rent above $2,000, so a percentage cap would allow higher fees for nearly everyone.
- Most mortgages have a 15-day grace period, which the sponsor contrasted with fees charged on the second of the month.
- Other reported junk fees: a $75 notice fee for potted plants on a balcony and a $300 administrative fee for signing a new lease.
- In 2021 the City issued only five fines to landlords.
- The FAQ is advocacy material from the sponsor's office, not a neutral analysis. Its claims about landlord income and small-landlord exits should be read in that light.

## Relevance
- **Maryland already regulates late fees.** State law caps them at 5% of the unpaid amount, only on the unpaid portion, with lower limits for weekly leases (see [[Maryland Tenants' Bill of Rights - October 2026 Edition]]). Rockville's staff chose not to go further because of that state rule. A Frederick cap below 5% would need a decision on whether state law leaves room for a stricter local limit.
- **Notice fees are a cleaner local target.** Maryland's Bill of Rights does not address fees for preparing or serving notices. A ban on notice fees fits naturally inside Real Rent's fee rules, because it is a fee for something the landlord must do anyway, the same logic Rockville used to ban fees for landlord duties.
- **The percentage vs. flat-cap argument.** At Frederick rent levels, 5% of a typical rent is well above $10. The sponsor's math shows how a percentage cap can still allow large fees. That point holds even if Frederick cannot change the state cap, and it is useful in testimony to the General Assembly.
- **Capacity data.** Seattle's rising intake and wait times are a reminder to fund enforcement staff when adding protections. Seattle's 2026 fee law did this with a per-unit fee.
