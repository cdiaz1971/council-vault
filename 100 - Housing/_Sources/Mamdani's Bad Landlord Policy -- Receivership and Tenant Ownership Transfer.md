# Mamdani's Bad Landlord Policy -- Receivership and Tenant Ownership Transfer

## Summary

NYC Mayor Zohran Mamdani has announced his administration is targeting "the worst landlords in NYC" by pursuing building ownership transfers to responsible stewards -- explicitly including tenants themselves -- as a consequence for chronic neglect. This goes beyond code enforcement and fines: it treats persistent landlord failure as grounds for a change of ownership, not just a penalty.

## Key Points

The Mamdani approach represents a policy escalation beyond the standard bad-landlord toolkit. Most jurisdictions rely on fines, inspection requirements, and repair orders. These tools are chronically underfunded, slow to move, and easily gamed by landlords with legal resources. Mamdani is proposing to make the ultimate consequence -- loss of the property -- credible and routine for the worst actors.

The mechanism most likely in play is something similar to the Article 7-A receivership process New York already has, which allows a court to appoint a receiver to manage a building when an owner has failed to maintain it. What Mamdani is adding is the political will to use it aggressively and the framing of tenant cooperatives as the preferred receiving entity rather than a new private landlord.

The tenant-ownership-transfer element is the most politically significant piece. If tenants of a neglected building can become its owners -- through a CLT structure, a cooperative, or a similar mechanism -- the outcome of the enforcement process is permanently affordable housing, not just a change of landlord. This converts a punitive tool into a production mechanism.

The policy is still being announced rather than implemented, so the details of the legal mechanism, financing structure, and eligibility criteria are not yet public.

## Relevance

Frederick's rental licensing ordinance (now under rewrite led by Council Member Brehm) includes a revocation escalation pathway: three inspections, then license revocation. But license revocation leaves the building's tenants in a precarious position -- the landlord loses the license, but tenants still need somewhere to live. Mamdani's model asks what happens next: who takes over a building whose owner has forfeited their right to operate it? The [[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]] and the CLT thread in the vault are the right places to develop a Frederick-specific answer. This also connects to the SRO Ordinance: SRO operators who lose their license under the revocation pathway would be exactly the context where a tenant-stewardship transfer mechanism could prevent displacement.
