# Dignity-First Design in Free Food Programs

## Summary

The Goodr free grocery model is built on an explicit dignity principle: participants shop rather than receive. This is not semantic -- it shapes the physical space, the intake process, and the social experience. The design principle generalizes across Frederick's affordability work wherever the council is designing programs for ALICE households.

## Key Points

Goodr's founder distinguishes between "access to meals" (a box of random donated food) and "access to food" (choosing what you cook for your family). The store looks and functions like a grocery store. Participants use a reusable bag, shop on a schedule, and select items. There are no visible markers of charity.

The PG County location specifically targets seniors above the income threshold for SNAP but below the ability to afford regular grocery shopping -- the ALICE gap. The council member who championed it said they were "suffering in silence."

The dignity design element matters politically as well as practically. Programs that feel like charity produce lower uptake and higher stigma. Programs that feel like infrastructure produce higher participation and broader coalition support. This mirrors the Goodr founder's distinction.

## Relevance

César's affordability agenda work across all six ALICE burden categories (housing, childcare, transportation, food, healthcare, technology) is going to require designing or advocating for programs. The dignity-first principle is a frame that applies to all of them -- a Municipal ID program, a childcare subsidy, a food access program. The political framing "this is infrastructure, not charity" is more durable and builds broader coalition support.

This connects to the "what we mean when we say affordable" framing work already in the vault, and to the ALICE baseline data showing the gap between households that qualify for federal programs and those that don't.
