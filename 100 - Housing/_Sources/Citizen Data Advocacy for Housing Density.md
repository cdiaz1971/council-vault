# Citizen Data Advocacy for Housing Density

## Summary

An Arlington economist used self-produced transit, tax, and housing cost analysis to make the public case for approving a 400-unit upzoning at a county board meeting -- a replicable model for citizen data advocacy in local land use fights.

## Key Points

Matthew Kowalyk, a housing advocate with an economics background, produced independent analysis ahead of an Arlington County Board vote on the RiverHouse development in Pentagon City. The proposal would add hundreds of new housing units through upzoning.

His analysis focused on three empirical questions: Is the local transit infrastructure capable of handling additional riders? What is the projected tax revenue impact? What do housing cost trends tell us about the need for supply?

The transit and parking analysis showed existing stations in the corridor are well equipped to absorb additional travelers. The tax revenue estimate projected an $8-11 million annual increase to the county if the upzoning is approved. Housing cost trend data demonstrated significant price increases over two decades, providing a supply-side argument for density.

Kowalyk was explicit about the limitations of his "homebrew econometrics" -- the analysis was not a peer-reviewed economic model. His framing was deliberately modest: not claiming to resolve every objection, but providing empirical grounding to "cut through the noise" around a politically contentious vote.

The underlying principle is that publicly available data (transit ridership, assessed property values, regional housing indices) is sufficient for a motivated advocate to produce credible analysis that shifts the terms of a local land use debate.

## Relevance

Directly applicable to Frederick's housing production debates. The [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] and ongoing LMC reform both involve arguments about capacity, affordability, and fiscal impact. Kowalyk's model -- a citizen economist doing independent analysis ahead of a council vote -- is something Frederick housing advocates or council staff could replicate with Frederick-specific data.

The tax revenue angle is particularly useful: upzoning resistance often centers on infrastructure capacity and neighborhood character, but the fiscal case for density is underused in local advocacy. Worth flagging as a model for any future density votes or rezoning hearings.
