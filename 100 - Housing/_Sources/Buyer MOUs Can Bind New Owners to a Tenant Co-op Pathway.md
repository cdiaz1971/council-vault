# Buyer MOUs Can Bind New Owners to a Tenant Co-op Pathway

## Summary
Without a COPA law, Brooklyn's Dukler tenant union used a legally binding memorandum of understanding with its new owner to lock in honoring the rent strike, repairs, and a co-op conversion pathway after six years. It works, but leaves tenants dependent on the buyer's good faith.

## Key Points
The Dukler Tenant Union organized for years against the Dukler family, a fixture on New York's worst-landlords list, with support from HOPE, a tenant organizing project of the Urban Homesteading Assistance Board (UHAB). When the buildings were finally sold at auction, the buyer was Mark Schwartz, an affordable housing developer.

The tenants did not rely on promises. Working with Brooklyn Legal Services, they negotiated memoranda of understanding that are part of a legally binding contract with the buyer. Unlike a typical real estate MOU, these cover an extended relationship: the new owner honors the rent strike, makes the repairs needed for livable conditions, and after six years brings in someone to walk tenants through converting the buildings to a co-op.

Tenant leaders describe it as a real win and an incomplete one. Anita Foxe, a 26-year resident, puts her satisfaction at 75 percent, because the tenants still do not own the building and the co-op step is six years out and depends on the owner following through.

## Relevance
This is the fallback playbook when no right-of-first-purchase law exists, which is Frederick's situation. A buyer agreement negotiated by organized tenants with legal support can secure repairs, stability, and an ownership path, and it gives tenant organizers something concrete to bargain for at the moment of sale. For council purposes it argues for two things: legal-services capacity for tenant associations, and a structural tool like COPA so tenants are not left negotiating from a weak position one building at a time.
