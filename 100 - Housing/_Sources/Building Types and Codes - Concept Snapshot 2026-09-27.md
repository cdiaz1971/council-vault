# Building Types and Codes

## Frederick

- **Types the code doesn't define.** Triplexes, fourplexes, and sixplexes aren't defined dwelling types in the LMC's dimensional tables. R4 and R6 allow only single-family detached homes, and multifamily isn't permitted in R4, R6, or R8 ([[Frederick Housing Policy Baseline]]).
- **SROs.** Single-room occupancy has no permission structure in Frederick's code ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]). The Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force explicitly endorses SRO and co-living as affordability tools ([[MAHTF Final Report Policy Brief -- 2026-07-16]]).
- **Process.** Frederick's review timelines run 18 to 24 months, against the task force's 8-to-10-month target. There's no pre-approved plan library, so every ADU and small infill project carries full custom design cost. It isn't confirmed whether 2–8 unit buildings are reviewed under commercial rather than residential code ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]).
- **Building code.** Frederick operates under state building code minimums and can't amend them on its own. Single-stair buildings depend on state action ([[Single Stair Reform Policy Brief]]).
- **Planning-stage work,** including César's SRO ordinance draft, is tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]]. ADU zoning changes are on [[Zoning Reform]].

## Missing-middle building types

- **4–20 unit buildings.** Desmond Dunn argues that missing-middle reform stops too soon at duplexes and ADUs. Six-unit buildings from the 1980s already sit unnoticed in older Raleigh neighborhoods. The 4–20 unit range suits small local developers, but it needs matching changes in entitlement, financing, appraisal, and predevelopment support ([[Small Multifamily 4-20 Units as the Next Layer of Missing Middle Housing]]).
- **Local vernacular.** Max Podemski's *A Paradise of Small Houses* traces how each city developed its own type from local lots, materials, and labor. Examples include Boston's triple-deckers, used by immigrant families as wealth-building rentals, New Orleans shotgun houses, and Philadelphia rowhouses. Regulation eliminated these types while the conditions that produced them persisted. Houston's 1990s lot-size reform is cited as the contemporary model ([[Vernacular Housing Typologies as a Framework for Missing Middle Reform]]).
- **Rowhomes.** Ryan Puzycki describes the rowhome as the dominant American urban form from about 1790 until postwar setbacks, lot minimums, and parking mandates made continuous street walls illegal. Philadelphia advertised a $2,500 model rowhome at the 1893 World's Fair. In Austin, the American Housing Corporation builds prefab family rowhomes around $750,000 where detached homes exceed $1 million:
  - It currently produces 12 a year, with a 1,200-a-year plant planned for 2027.
  - JPMorgan Chase estimates the method cuts costs 20–30%.
  - Austin's reforms made it possible: three homes by right, no parking minimums, and smaller setbacks.

  Houston's 1,400 sq ft lot minimum produced more than 30,000 townhomes ([[Rowhome Revolution America's Original Middle Class Housing Form and Its Modern Revival]]).
- **Pocket neighborhoods.** Architect Ross Chapin's clusters of 4–12 small homes around a shared green reach modest density in a form neighbors find less threatening than apartments. He argues that "charm" comes from specific, repeatable design patterns: scale, threshold spaces, and building-to-street ratios ([[Pocket Neighborhoods and the Pattern Language of Charm - Interview with Ross Chapin]]).
- **Cohousing.** Private homes with shared space can lower ongoing costs, but US projects are expensive to build. Roughly 95% of US cohousers are white and two-thirds hold graduate degrees. The barriers are zoning that doesn't recognize the model, construction financing, and developer margins, which often have to be waived. Workarounds include nonprofit partners, resale caps, retrofits, and rental tenure ([[Cohousing Affordability -- Why the US Model Struggles to Scale]]).
- **A project that penciled.** Loft3, a small unsubsidized condo building near downtown Raleigh, shows where the costs are:
  - an elevator adds about $20,000 per unit
  - parking can exceed $30,000 per space
  - a required second stair adds $10,000–15,000 per unit

  It used cold-formed steel framing and met Raleigh's affordable density-bonus terms ([[Housing Production -- Loft3 Middle Housing Case Study Raleigh]]).

## Single-stair buildings

- **What changes.** US codes generally require two stairways above three stories. That forces double-loaded corridors, units with windows on one side, and wide lots. One protected stair allows units on multiple sides, family-sized layouts, and narrow infill lots:
  - Costs fall 6–13%.
  - Up to 95% of floor area goes to housing, against 70–87% with a double-loaded corridor.
  - Eliminating the second stair can save up to $300,000 on a small building.

  ([[Single Stair Reform Policy Brief]]; [[Single-Stair Reform -- NC Legislative Momentum and National Context]])
- **Fire safety.** A February 2025 Center for Building in North America and Pew study found sprinklered single-stair buildings no riskier than two-stair buildings:
  - New York City's modern four-to-six-story single-stair buildings had fire-death rates similar to other residential buildings from 2012 to 2024.
  - Seattle has allowed them up to six stories since 1977.

  Adopted ordinances typically require full sprinklers, pressurized stairs, fire-rated construction, no more than four units per floor, and a short travel distance to the stair ([[Single Stair Reform Policy Brief]]).
- **Small builders and financing.** Buildings of 3–6 stories and 4–12 units on small lots can be financed at human scale. For owner-occupied buildings of up to four units, Freddie Mac-backed loans count about 75% of rental income toward qualifying. Where the type is illegal, there are no rent comparables, so appraisals fall back to single-family values and financing disappears ([[Single-Stair Buildings and Small Lot Zoning for Housing Production]]).
- **State momentum.**
  - Since 2022, 19 states and DC have introduced single-stair bills. Seven passed in 2025, including Colorado, Montana, New Hampshire, and Texas.
  - Tennessee passed reform in 2024, and five of its cities have moved.
  - North Carolina's SB 492 stalled in 2025.

  ([[Single-Stair Building Reform Housing Production]]; [[Single Stair Reform Policy Brief]])
- **Maryland.** HB 489 / SB 436 (2025) directed the Maryland Department of Labor to study single-stair buildings up to six stories and recommend legislation, with a report due December 2026. Baltimore City Bill 25-0062 (Dorsey) would amend Baltimore's building code directly ([[Single Stair Reform Policy Brief]]; [[Single-Stair Building Reform Housing Production]]).
- **Federal.** The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, law since July 11, 2026, directs HUD to set guidelines and model code for single-stair ("point-access block") buildings up to six stories and authorizes pilot grants ([McGuireWoods Consulting section-by-section](https://mwcllc.com/2026/07/17/21st-century-road-to-housing-act-section-by-section-guide/)).

## Single-room occupancy (SRO)

- **The lowest rung.** SROs are private rooms with shared kitchens or baths. They're the cheapest housing available without subsidy and historically buffered against homelessness. Most were zoned out after World War II, which researchers widely link to rising homelessness ([[Housing Policy -- Single-Room Occupancy Legalization Rhode Island ROOM Act]]).
- **Legalization models.**
  - Rhode Island's ROOM Act would make SROs a by-right use in residential and commercial zones statewide. It was held for study after a March 31, 2026 hearing ([[Housing Policy -- Single-Room Occupancy Legalization Rhode Island ROOM Act]]).
  - In Durham, SROs need special-use approval outside campuses and downtown. Raleigh's four-unrelated-persons cap confines them to a co-living district. The proposed fix is to allow SROs by right in standard residential districts and drop the cap ([[SRO Housing Legalization -- The Case for Durham and Raleigh]]).
- **What an ordinance has to settle.** César's February 2026 public thread on a Frederick draft names the choices that decide whether SROs become "dignified housing or a problem":
  - how many residents share a bathroom or kitchen
  - property management standards
  - whether units must be affordable
  - inspection and enforcement
  - tenant protections
  - whether existing apartments can convert
  - design standards
  - whether supportive services are required, optional, or incentivized
  - how results are monitored

  ([[SRO thread]])

## ADUs and factory-built units

- **ADUs, costs and trade-offs.** Ireland plans to exempt ADUs ("seomraí") from planning permission, and supporters claim up to 350,000 units. Michael Byrne supports it while naming the risks:
  - landlord–tenant proximity sharpens screening and intrusion
  - classifying ADU residents as "licensees" would strip tenancy protections
  - untaxed rental income and rising property values widen wealth gaps

  ([[The Week in Housing -- Should We Oppose ADUs (Seomraí)]])
- **Mobile dwelling units.** Sightline describes factory-built, movable units as faster and cheaper than site-built ADUs. It positions them as the answer where ADU legalization succeeds but the economics don't close ([[Mobile Dwelling Units (MDUs) as a Low-Cost Housing Tool]]).
- **Modular at scale.** China's Broad Sustainable Building assembled a 26-story, 208-unit tower in five days from pre-finished, container-sized modules ([[Manufactured Housing Speed -- Broad Sustainable Building 26 Story Modular Tower in 5 Days]]). The ROAD to Housing Act expands the federal definition of manufactured housing to include homes built with or without a permanent chassis ([McGuireWoods Consulting section-by-section](https://mwcllc.com/2026/07/17/21st-century-road-to-housing-act-section-by-section-guide/)).

## Code and permitting fixes

- **Residential code for small multifamily.** Dallas applied the lighter International Residential Code, rather than commercial standards, to buildings of up to 8 units and 3 stories. That removed sprinkler requirements and cut costs ([[Applying Residential Building Codes to Small Multifamily (Dallas Model)]]).
- **Pre-approved plans and electronic review.** Cities certify ADU, duplex, and small-apartment designs in advance so builders pay only for site-specific work. Electronic plan review can cut the time from decision to permit from months to weeks. Neither requires zoning reform, and both multiply its effect ([[Pre-Approved Architectural Plans and Electronic Permitting as Supply-Side Tools]]).

## Construction methods and building lifecycle

- **Deconstruction.** US construction and demolition waste exceeds 600 million tons a year. Taking buildings apart piece by piece recovers materials and embodied carbon. Policy models:
  - Boulder (2020) requires 75% diversion by weight.
  - Portland, Palo Alto, Pittsburgh, and San Antonio have similar rules.
  - Chapel Hill and Orange County, NC require waste plans for demolitions, recyclability assessments above 500 sq ft, and higher tipping fees for mixed debris.

  ([[Building Deconstruction as Housing Policy -- Design for Disassembly and Circular Construction]])
- **Circular design.** Architect Rick Cook argues against "single-use buildings." He calls for adaptive reuse, design for disassembly (fasteners over adhesives, modular parts), and materials chosen for reclamation ([[Circular Architecture and the Case Against Single-Use Buildings]]).
