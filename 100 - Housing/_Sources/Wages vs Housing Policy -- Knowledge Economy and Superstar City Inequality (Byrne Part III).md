## Summary

Michael Byrne's third installment in his knowledge economy and housing series (The Week in Housing, June 12, 2026) makes the case that the housing affordability crisis is primarily a wage inequality problem, not a housing supply problem. In "superstar cities" where the knowledge economy concentrates high earners, non-college-educated workers must compete for housing against residents whose incomes have been amplified by globalization and agglomeration effects. Housing prices track average income growth, but that average obscures deeply unequal income growth -- decades of stagnant wages for many workers drives the gap between prices and affordability.

## Key Points

**The central argument (Buchholz et al., 2026):**
The problem lies not in a constrained housing market but in rising inequality. High-earning college graduates concentrate in the largest, densest cities; housing prices track the average income of those cities; lower-income workers face prices set by high earners while earning stagnant wages.

**Superstar city dynamics:**
- Since 1950, the most expensive cities have gone from 2x to 4x the national average home price
- Six of the seven US superstar cities experience negative internal migration while simultaneously experiencing positive international immigration -- displacement is happening, but lower-income residents who can't leave endure crowding or extreme commutes instead
- Women in these cities sort into jobs below their skill levels to secure shorter commutes (related to care duties); Black workers face a "double disadvantage" of lower earnings plus longer commutes

**Housing as the mechanism of durable inequality:**
Byrne argues that housing is not just a symptom of inequality but the mechanism through which inequality becomes entrenched and spatial. High earners use purchasing power to chase well-located housing, improve local amenities to their tastes, and price out lower-income residents who then pay for "perks" they didn't want. The result is a closed shop: the successful city excludes by price what it claims to welcome by principle.

**Implication for supply-side politics:**
The article implicitly challenges pure supply-side arguments. If inequality is the driver, building more market-rate units in superstar cities does not solve affordability for workers whose wages haven't risen -- it just builds more units priced for high earners.

## Relevance for Frederick

Frederick is not a superstar city but is increasingly a bedroom community for the DC-Baltimore metro knowledge economy corridor. The dynamics Byrne describes -- lower-income workers priced out by the arrival of higher-earning remote workers and commuters -- are directly applicable to the housing production target resolution and MPDU reform work. The racial dimension (Black workers facing double disadvantage in commute burden) maps onto Frederick's equity goals. The core argument that "supply isn't the problem, inequality is" has direct implications for how to frame housing advocacy and what tools actually address affordability vs. what just adds market-rate units.
