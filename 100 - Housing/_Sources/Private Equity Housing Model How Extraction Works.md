# Private Equity Housing Model How Extraction Works

## Summary

Private equity firms use a short time horizon, high debt loads, dividend recapitalization, and the 2-and-20 fee structure to extract maximum profit from housing portfolios -- creating structural incentives for rent hikes, evictions, and deferred maintenance regardless of individual firm behavior.

## Key Points

The private equity model in housing is not primarily about bad actors -- it is about structural incentives that make harmful behavior the rational choice.

**The basic mechanics:**
- PE firms invest as little as 1-2% of their own capital; the rest comes from institutional investors (including pension funds) and debt.
- The "2 and 20" model: 2% annual management fee on committed capital, plus 20% of profits above a 6-8% return threshold. This creates powerful incentives for high-risk, high-return strategies.
- Average hold period is 3-7 years, creating pressure to extract maximum value quickly rather than maintain long-term asset quality.
- PE firms are largely exempt from SEC disclosure requirements, making their operations opaque.

**Debt loading and dividend recapitalization:**
Portfolio companies are often required to take on additional debt to pay investors dividends -- a practice called dividend recapitalization. The PE firm and its investors collect cash; the property company carries the debt for years. This additional debt can damage credit ratings and contribute to bankruptcies, which creates tenant dislocation risk that is entirely disconnected from tenant behavior or lease compliance.

**The Southern Towers case:**
CIM Group acquired this 2,346-unit Alexandria complex in 2020 for $506 million, targeting "urban neighborhoods that will support outsized rent growth/capital appreciation." Within six months of ownership, CIM filed 250 evictions -- during an eviction moratorium. Tenants reported rent hikes and deferred maintenance. This is not aberrant; it is the model.

**Scale:** The private funds market grew from $1 trillion in 2004 to over $16 trillion in 2025. PE firms own approximately 13% of all U.S. apartment units.

**The pension fund irony:** Many of the workers harmed by PE-owned housing have their pension funds invested in the same PE firms collecting their rent.

## Relevance

This is foundational context for the Frederick tenant protection package and for any conversation about algorithmic rent pricing and corporate landlord accountability. The Rockville Chapter 18 algorithmic rent pricing ban is directly responsive to the dynamics described here -- PE firms use algorithmic pricing tools to coordinate rent increases across portfolios in ways that function as price collusion even without explicit coordination. Understanding the debt-loading and short-hold-period mechanics also clarifies why relocation assistance provisions are not overreach: when a PE firm takes on additional debt to pay itself dividends and then triggers displacement through that financial engineering, tenants bear costs created entirely by investor decisions.
