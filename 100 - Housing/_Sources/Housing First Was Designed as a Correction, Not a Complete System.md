# Housing First Was Designed as a Correction, Not a Complete System

## Summary
Harvey Lieberman, a clinical psychologist and co-founder of the Institute for Community Living, argues that Housing First emerged in the 1990s as an important correction to programs that demanded sobriety or psychiatric stability before offering shelter. It worked for that purpose. But it was never designed to manage the escalating complexity that supportive housing now confronts -- severe mental illness, fentanyl-era addiction, cognitive decline, aging-related frailty -- and the current political backlash exploits this mismatch without offering anything better.

## Key Points
Housing First's original insight was simple and empirically sound: people living on the street, especially those with serious mental illness or addiction, were far less likely to improve without secure housing first. Early data showed people placed into housing without treatment preconditions stayed housed at higher rates than expected.

The problem is systemic, not programmatic. Shrinking psychiatric beds, weakened long-term rehabilitation, inadequate addiction treatment capacity, and cuts to community mental health infrastructure left supportive housing as the default destination for people leaving hospitals, jails, shelters, and emergency rooms. The model became overloaded by absorbing the failures of every other system.

Frontline staff -- often peers or counselors -- are expected to coordinate across hospitals, outpatient clinics, treatment programs, criminal justice systems, and public benefits systems while also maintaining housing stability. This is a staffing-to-complexity mismatch that no programmatic model can solve on its own.

The political backlash is dangerous not because it identifies real strain, but because it produces underprepared replacements. Utah's attempt to move homeless residents to a mandatory treatment campus collapsed amid cost concerns, civil liberties issues, and no clinical plan.

Lieberman's conclusion: housing remains essential for stabilization, but housing alone cannot resolve the behavioral, medical, and social burdens that many supportive housing programs now carry. The solution is restoring the surrounding infrastructure -- not replacing Housing First with something more coercive.

## Relevance
This is a useful frame for César's housing policy work. When Frederick eventually engages more directly with homelessness and supportive housing (through the HACF or other mechanisms), the political pressure to move away from Housing First will likely be framed as common sense. This piece gives the intellectual grounding to respond to that pressure -- not by defending Housing First uncritically, but by correctly identifying what actually failed.
