# Community Opportunity to Purchase Gives Mission-Driven Buyers First Claim on Distressed Buildings

## Summary
NYC's Community Opportunity to Purchase Act would let approved nonprofits buy distressed buildings before they reach the speculative market. It now has 30 sponsors and Mayor Mamdani's support but sits laid over in committee, waiting on Speaker Menin.

## Key Points
A Community Opportunity to Purchase Act (COPA) gives qualified nonprofit buyers, drawn from an approved list such as community land trusts and mission-driven developers, a first opportunity to buy multifamily buildings when they are sold, before the open market can. The point is to stop distressed buildings from cycling between speculative owners and to give tenants a path to ownership through a trusted partner rather than through whoever bids highest. San Francisco runs a COPA program that advocates credit with preventing displacement.

New York's version has been introduced three times in five years. Council Member Sandy Nurse's current bill has 30 council sponsors and backing from more than 200 community land trusts, tenant associations, and affordable housing developers. Former Mayor Eric Adams vetoed it in December 2025. Mayor Zohran Mamdani has publicly endorsed it, put it in his citywide housing plan in May, and directed the housing agency (HPD) to work on standing up a program. Even so, the bill was laid over in committee on September 5 and is well past its aging period. Speaker Julie Menin has to bring it to a vote, and her office's statement committed to nothing specific.

The Dukler tenant union case shows the cost of not having it. After years of organizing against a landlord on the city's worst-landlords list, tenants got their buildings sold to an affordable housing developer, but only through an open-market auction and a negotiated side agreement. Organizers say COPA would have let a community buyer step in directly and kept the tenants from depending on another landlord's good faith.

## Relevance
This is a social-ownership tool for the tenant protection package and the CLT thread. Frederick has no right-of-first-refusal mechanism for tenants or nonprofits at the municipal level, and the question of whether a city can impose one in Maryland is a Rachel Gray question before it is a drafting question. COPA also pairs with the license-revocation gap: when a negligent owner loses a rental license or exits, a first-purchase right for a CLT or mission buyer is what keeps the building from going to the next extractive owner. The NYC story is also a reminder that a supportive mayor and a sponsor majority are not enough when the presiding officer controls the agenda, a dynamic César sees from the other side of the gavel.
