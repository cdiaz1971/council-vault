# Maryland Geothermal Pilot Stalled Between Utility Cost and Equity Imperative

## Summary

Maryland's WARMTH Act geothermal pilot program -- targeting affordable apartment complexes in Silver Spring and Baltimore -- has stalled after the Public Service Commission rejected BGE and Washington Gas proposals as too expensive, leaving immigrant working-class tenants who organized for the program in limbo.

## Key Points

The 2024 WARMTH Act required Maryland's two largest gas utilities to propose geothermal pilot programs at select affordable housing sites, funded through customer rates subject to PSC approval. The commission rejected both utilities' proposals in December, describing the costs as "not only exorbitant, but also speculative." BGE proposed two projects totaling roughly $97M; Washington Gas proposed Northwest Park Apartments in Silver Spring, estimated at $40-50M. The commission has since begun hiring a consultant to help both utilities revise proposals.

The target sites are not luxury developments. Northwest Park Apartments in Silver Spring houses a tight-knit immigrant community, many of them workers, who have been conducting nitrogen dioxide testing on their own gas stoves, connecting with advocacy groups, and meeting with Washington Gas directly. One resident's utility bill hit $800 in a single month before she qualified for assistance -- and her power was cut for two days before that assistance arrived.

The delay is felt unevenly. The resident concern is specific: their building is roughly two miles from Flower Branch Apartments, where a gas line explosion in August 2016 killed seven people. The people living at Northwest Park know some of those victims personally.

BGE's rejected proposals covered the Verona at Oakland Mills complex and community centers in Columbia, plus a dormitory at Coppin State and St. Stephen's Court Apartments in Baltimore.

Del. Lorig Charkoudian (D-Montgomery), the WARMTH Act sponsor, argues cost reductions are feasible: utilities may be overbuilding for their first-ever geothermal installation, and creative solutions -- like tapping into an existing geothermal system at a neighboring school -- could lower the Washington Gas number significantly. The Massachusetts utility-owned geothermal precedent came in around $30M.

## Relevance

Frederick is not directly involved in this program, but the political and structural dynamics are relevant. The story illustrates a recurring pattern: environmental and energy-efficiency programs that are technically designed to benefit low-income and immigrant communities get delayed by cost structures set by utilities and regulators who answer primarily to the ratepayer base, not to the specific communities targeted. The residents who organized and did the work are waiting while the consultants draft a new proposal.

For council work, this is useful context for any energy or infrastructure legislation touching affordability, utility rates, or environmental justice. It also connects to the data center thread -- BGE rate pressures are already documented there -- and to the broader pattern of infrastructure disinvestment in immigrant-heavy communities.
