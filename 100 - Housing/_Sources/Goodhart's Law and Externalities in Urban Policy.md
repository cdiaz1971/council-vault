# Goodhart's Law and Externalities in Urban Policy

## Summary

Applies Goodhart's Law and Pigouvian tax theory to suburban development, arguing that American cities are trapped in self-reinforcing cycles of sprawl, fiscal drain, and car dependency produced by optimizing for the wrong metrics.

## Key Points

Goodhart's Law: when a measure becomes a target, it ceases to be a good measure. The author opens with a call center analogy -- Progressive Insurance refused to measure handle time because minimizing call length drove callbacks up. Cities do the same thing when they optimize for traffic flow by widening roads, which induces more driving and sprawl, which makes traffic worse.

Externalities, from Arthur Cecil Pigou's *The Economics of Welfare*, are private decisions with unpriced public costs or benefits. Negative externalities in suburban development are systemic and compounding:

**Lane miles and fiscal drag.** Chattanooga (180k people) maintains 2,500 lane miles; Jersey City (250k people) maintains 250. The same road infrastructure costs the same per foot to build and maintain whether it sits in a downtown that generates $2M/acre in tax revenue or a suburb that generates $40k. Suburbs are structurally insolvent -- low tax yield, high service demand.

**Parking mandates.** Required parking raises construction costs for everyone regardless of how they arrive, heats the urban environment, increases stormwater runoff, and forces businesses apart. The most economically productive neighborhoods in most American cities are pre-zoning areas built before parking requirements existed.

**Big box vacancy cycles.** Small storefronts are adaptable; a closed barbershop becomes a coffee shop. A closed superstore sits vacant for years. The empty shell sits on road infrastructure it cannot pay for, anchored by a sea of impervious surface.

**The entanglement.** These are not independent failures -- they compound. Sprawl breeds car dependency breeds wider roads breeds more sprawl. Cities annex outlying areas to capture tax revenue, then inherit infrastructure they cannot afford to maintain.

On solutions: the author is appropriately skeptical of single-metric fixes (Goodhart again). Two legitimate tools exist: Pigouvian taxes that price negative externalities (congestion pricing is the clearest example) and carrot-side regulatory fast-tracking for preferred building types. No grand solution -- hundreds of small course corrections over time.

## Relevance

This is a powerful theoretical frame for housing production arguments in Frederick. The fiscal drag argument maps directly onto the case for infill density and missing middle housing -- downtown and walkable neighborhoods subsidize the sprawl at the edge. The Pigouvian tax framing is useful for talking about parking minimums, impact fees, and TIF districts without sounding like a housing ideologue. The data point on Utrecht (38 sq mi, 370k people) vs. Chattanooga (151 sq mi, 180k people) is a strong visual anchor for why density is a fiscal strategy, not just a lifestyle preference.

The Goodhart's Law frame also applies to housing production targets themselves -- worth flagging when the Housing Production Target Resolution is being discussed, to avoid the metric becoming a ceiling rather than a floor.
