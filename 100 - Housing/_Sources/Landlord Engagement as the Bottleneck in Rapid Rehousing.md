# Landlord Engagement as the Bottleneck in Rapid Rehousing

## Summary

Cleveland's rapid rehousing program revealed that unit acquisition -- not outreach, case management, or funding -- is the rate-limiting step in moving unsheltered people into housing quickly, and that sustained landlord relationship-building is the only way to solve it.

## Key Points

**The pilot's core lesson.** When Cleveland launched A Home for Every Neighbor in August 2024, the first pilot of 12 people took six weeks to house -- far too slow. The holdup was not finding interested participants, securing funding, or doing casework. It was finding enough rental units. The program was new, explaining it to landlords was time-consuming, and the prospective tenants had profiles -- little or no income, criminal histories, prior evictions -- that triggered automatic rejection from many landlords.

**The revamp.** After the pilot, the team completely reworked its landlord engagement strategy based on what it had learned. The next cohort: 40 people housed in three weeks. The improvement came almost entirely from having pre-built landlord relationships rather than starting cold on each case.

**How unit acquisition now works.** Connor Marrott, the program's unit acquisition specialist, maintains an active list of landlords who have agreed to participate and spends significant time searching online and making cold calls to recruit new ones. The pitch to landlords includes rent coverage, case management support, and a separate fund managed by NEOCH to handle unusual situations (utility debt payoff, ID replacement, record sealing) that would otherwise create landlord risk. Some landlords have left the program after bad experiences; a comparable number have been cut by the program for racist or discriminatory conduct.

**Why this matters structurally.** Most rapid rehousing programs hand participants a voucher and tell them to find a landlord themselves. NEOCH's executive director describes this as a tremendous burden on someone already under the stress of homelessness -- and it produces worse placement outcomes. The Cleveland model inverts it: staff do the landlord work, participants choose from a curated list. This is why the program can achieve an 11-day average when comparable programs measure in months.

**The market constraint that remains.** The landlord engagement model can optimize placement speed, but it cannot solve the underlying inventory problem. Cleveland rents are rising at more than twice the national average. The program pays an average of $1,225/month per unit. As rents rise and affordable units disappear, even excellent landlord relationships cannot produce units that do not exist.

## Relevance

This is the operational insight behind the Cleveland model, and it has direct implications for how any city designs a rapid rehousing program. The lesson is not "build better case management" -- it is "build a dedicated unit acquisition function with sustained landlord relationships maintained between placements, not rebuilt from scratch on each case."

For Frederick, this matters most for the housing stability end of the ALICE work and any future conversation about local rapid rehousing capacity. If Frederick ever designs a program in this space, the unit acquisition role needs to be funded and staffed as a standing function, not a reactive one.
