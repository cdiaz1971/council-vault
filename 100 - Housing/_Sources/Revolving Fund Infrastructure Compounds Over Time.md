# Revolving Fund Infrastructure Compounds Over Time

## Summary

The pattern across Austin, Columbus, Michigan, Massachusetts, and New York is consistent: start with a modest bond-backed fund, demonstrate results, build public confidence, return for more. The fund is infrastructure that compounds, not a one-time appropriation.

## Key Points

The national track record:

- **Austin:** $250M bond approved by voters in 2018. Returned four years later for $350M more.
- **Columbus:** $50M in 2019, $200M in 2022, $500M in November 2025 -- the largest local housing investment in the city's history -- after its commitment helped create or preserve 7,000 affordable homes.
- **Michigan, Massachusetts, New York:** State-level funds now operational, each modeled in part on the HOC mechanism.
- **Nashville:** Mayor recently proposed a bond-backed loan fund explicitly framed as "phase one," not a final answer.
- **New York:** $100M fund attracted $115M in additional private capital alongside it.

None of these jurisdictions solved their housing crisis in a single vote. The pattern is: establish the mechanism, demonstrate results, build public confidence, and return for more capital. The fund becomes a platform rather than a project.

The authors argue Maryland has structural advantages over all of these precedents: DHCD already administers LIHTC at scale, already has multifamily bond authority, already has developer relationships built over decades. Maryland would be launching a fund with an existing institutional home rather than building one from scratch.

## Relevance

The compounding logic applies directly to how Frederick should think about local housing finance tools. The city doesn't need to solve the housing crisis in one budget cycle -- it needs to establish a mechanism, demonstrate results, and build political durability for larger commitments over time. The $14M Affordable Housing Opportunity Fund in Montgomery County doing rapid acquisition, complementing the $100M production fund, is the right model for Frederick's scale. The argument for starting small and returning for more is also politically useful when presenting the Housing Production Target Resolution -- it reframes the ask from "solve everything now" to "build the infrastructure."

For the broader state advocacy context: if the Maryland statewide fund advances, Frederick could position itself as an early adopter and use the state fund to backstop deals that can't close on local financing alone.
