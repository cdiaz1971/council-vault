# Divided Dublin -- Knowledge Economy Concentration and Housing Inequality

## Summary

Irish case study showing how tech-sector concentration in Dublin has produced severe spatial and income inequality -- and how housing costs are both an expression of that inequality and a cause of further displacement, with implications for how Frederick frames its own housing demand problem.

## Key Points

This is Part IV of a series situating housing affordability within the political economy of the knowledge economy. The Ireland case is speculative but instructive.

Ireland is among the most globalized economies in the world, with an FDI-led growth model. Between 2004 and 2016, service exports grew from 35% to over 55% of all Irish exports, with ICT services driving the shift. By 2025, Ireland's ICT sector employed 6.7% of workers -- nearly double the EU average of 3.5%.

The concentration effect is extreme: over 75% of ICT jobs created between 2003 and 2018 were in Dublin. The four major cities (Dublin, Cork, Galway, Limerick) captured over 85% of FDI jobs while representing just 40% of the population.

The labor market has not "hollowed out" in the classic sense. Instead of growth at both high and low ends, there has been stagnation or decline in middle and low skilled jobs, with high skilled roles as the only growth sector. Workers in internationally-oriented sectors saw wage increases of 4-13% from 2008-2017; domestic sector workers saw wage declines for eight or nine of those ten years. One in five male workers and one in three female workers are low-skilled. The ICT sector's median weekly earnings are nearly double the economy-wide average.

The housing consequence: average Dublin house prices in March 2026 were just over €580,000. Dublin 6 (affluent) median prices were €750,000; Dublin 11 (Finglas/Ballymun) median was €350,000. A house in the most expensive local electoral area costs nearly seven times one in the cheapest. Traditional working-class Dublin neighborhoods are now effectively only accessible to high-skilled workers.

The theoretical frame: housing demand is a function of income, and incomes are highly unequal. Politicians reach for supply-and-demand as "economics 101" but avoid the fact that demand is shaped by wage levels and borrowing capacity -- not just unit counts. High house price differentials push lower-income households out to suburbs and commuter towns, and down into precarious rental housing.

The "left behind places" dimension: rural and peripheral regions of Ireland have not experienced industrial collapse (unlike the north of England or France), but they are not doing well *relative to Dublin*. That comparative gap breeds resentment and political disconnection.

The closing frame: housing drives economic and spatial inequality, but can also be a vehicle for social and economic integration if addressed at the structural level.

## Relevance

The Dublin case is a sharper version of dynamics visible in Frederick. The tech corridor (DC/NOVA) functions like Dublin relative to Frederick's role as a commuter exurb -- high-wage workers priced out of core metros have pushed demand and prices up in Frederick, displacing lower-income residents from established neighborhoods.

This gives intellectual grounding for Frederick's housing policy frame: supply arguments alone are insufficient if demand is structurally driven by income concentration. The [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] and Real Rent Ordinance both need to account for this -- adding units at market rate serves the demand side but not the displacement side.

The "demand is a function of income" framing is also politically useful: it lets you challenge the supply-only narrative without dismissing supply, by naming what supply arguments leave out.
