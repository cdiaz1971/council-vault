# Raleigh's Exempt Plat Ordinance (TC-4-26)

## Summary
Raleigh's draft ordinance TC-4-26 would legalize exempt plats to modernize minor subdivision review, a peer model for reducing barriers to small-lot and starter home production.

## Key Points
Raleigh City staff drafted ordinance TC-4-26 last month to update the city's subdivision regulations and legalize exempt plats, a streamlined minor subdivision process. The text change goes before the Planning Commission Text Change Committee on August 19, 2026. Comments can be submitted through Raleigh's engagement portal before that date.

The case for the ordinance rests on a stark comparison. Creating a small lot subdivision currently takes one to two months in Durham but one to two years in Raleigh under existing rules. Modernizing the minor subdivision process would bring Raleigh in line with most other North Carolina municipalities and reduce a major procedural barrier to small and starter homes.

The same brief also flagged national guidance from the Institute for Progress and Pew Research on implementing the ROAD to Housing Act at the state and local level. One early recommended step is certifying to HUD that state manufactured housing policy treats manufactured homes the same as site-built homes, which unlocks federal code changes that lower per-home costs by thousands of dollars. North Carolina's manufactured housing share sits at roughly 10 percent of stock, twice the national average, making this a live issue there, though Maryland's own ROAD to Housing Act implementation work is already tracked separately in the vault.

## Relevance
Exempt plats are a procedural reform, not a rent or subsidy tool, which makes them a politically easier lift than more contested housing interventions. That is the same dynamic already noted for Rockville's algorithmic pricing ban under the Real Rent Ordinance thread: reforms that target process or market manipulation clear more easily than reforms that touch price directly.

The core mechanism, streamlining minor subdivision review to unlock small-lot and starter home production, connects directly to two open Frederick threads. The Housing Production Target Resolution is built around a 4,000-unit minimum floor and a 13,774-unit documented need by 2035; procedural reforms that lower the cost and timeline of small-scale infill are one of the more achievable levers toward that floor. The Inclusionary Zoning thread is separately tracking whether Frederick's Chapter 19 MPDU framework and its 12.5 percent baseline can be recalibrated, and the Golden Mile corridor's more than 30 underutilized commercial parcels are a plausible target for exactly the kind of small-lot redevelopment exempt plats are designed to unlock.

Raleigh's draft ordinance and comment process, open through August 19, is now a second live peer implementation to cite alongside Rockville's Chapter 18 rewrite when Frederick's own housing production or LMC reform work resumes.
