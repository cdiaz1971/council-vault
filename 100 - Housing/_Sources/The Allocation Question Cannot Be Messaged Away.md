# The Allocation Question Cannot Be Messaged Away

## Summary
Three Michael Byrne pieces converge on one claim. The progressive housing case keeps trying to win without stating who housing is for and why, and allocation is where that avoidance breaks. Frederick needs a published, consistent allocation principle before new affordable units come online.

## The Pattern
[[Housing and Immigration Framing]] (June 2026) offers the communications playbook. Lead with collective benefit, connect housing to shared infrastructure, show that policy works, and steer away from eligibility and blame. Its last move is an admission that framing is not enough, and that the substance of allocation fairness also has to be explainable.

[[Populist Planning and Cultural Housing Aspirations]] (June 2026) supplies the mechanism for what happens when that substance goes missing. When planners and progressives answer people's stated aspirations with technical criteria instead of engaging them as legitimate, the backlash gets labeled populism, and it pushes voters toward authoritarian alternatives. It also names a consistency test. A framework that honors cultural claims for some communities and dismisses them for others loses credibility with everyone.

[[Social Housing Allocation as a Nativist Flashpoint]] (September 2026) shows the fight arriving. Across Ireland, the UK, France, Germany, and Spain, the far right is turning allocation into a question of national belonging, and Byrne rejects the three standard progressive replies (denial, messaging, moral dismissal). His answer is a clear normative case for need-based allocation that ordinary people find intuitive.

Read in order, the three pieces describe one gap. Collective-benefit framing works for building support in the abstract, but allocation is eligibility by definition. Every subsidized unit forces a choice about who gets it, and a movement that has only practiced avoiding that question has no answer ready when an opponent asks it loudly.

## The Synthesis Claim
Messaging and expertise can carry the case for building housing. They cannot carry the case for distributing it. That second case requires a stated values position on who comes first and why, and it has to hold up under the consistency test, meaning the same principle applies whether the claimant is a longtime resident, a newcomer, or an immigrant family. If the Council does not state that principle, the vacuum gets filled by the residency-and-nationality framing now spreading in Europe, which is the exclusion mechanism described in [[Belonging as Infrastructure -- Disproportionate Exclusion Across Political and Economic Domains]].

There is a real tension to resolve rather than hide. The Framing note's universality move (housing benefits all of us) and the need-based allocation principle (housing goes first to those who need it most) point in different directions. The durable version probably holds both. Building more benefits everyone, and distributing scarce subsidized units by need is fair because anyone in the community can fall into need.

## Frederick Implications
The [[Housing Production Target Resolution]], any affordable set-asides, and future subsidy programs will eventually produce units that someone has to be selected for. The allocation rules for those units should be written and argued in public before the first unit is available, not after a contested selection makes news.

Candidate criteria worth weighing include housing need and cost burden, displacement risk, local employment or ties to Frederick, and household composition. Local ties is the one to handle carefully. A modest local preference can read as fairness to longtime residents, but a length-of-residency rule is the exact tool Vox proposes and would fall hardest on immigrant households the [[City Values Act]] is meant to protect.

The consistency test from the Populist Planning note gives a usable standard for any rule the Council adopts. Would it be defended the same way no matter which community it helped or excluded?

## Open Questions
What allocation language, if any, already exists in Frederick's MPDU program or Housing Authority policies, and does it include any residency preference? Who in Frederick is already making the nativist version of this argument, even quietly? Is there a peer city that has adopted and publicly defended a need-based allocation principle and survived a challenge to it?
