# ROAD to Housing Act Savings Carry No Requirement to Reach Tenants

## Summary
Housing analysts argue the ROAD to Housing Act mainly cuts developers' soft costs and time, with no requirement that savings show up as lower rents or prices. Critics say direct subsidy tied to affordability would do more.

## Key Points
Tram Hoang of PolicyLink argues the law does nothing meaningful to directly produce housing, since it mandates no new units anywhere. Most provisions lower soft costs such as review time, while the main barrier to supply is the hard cost of financing.

Because the law includes no pass-through requirement, any developer savings stay with the developer unless something else forces them to the end user. Hoang points to deed restrictions for ownership units and rent regulation for rentals as the tools that would.

Shamus Roller of the National Housing Law Project calls the reforms positive but says they leave the basic structure of the housing market unchanged. LISC's Mark Kudlowitz counters that time is money and the NEPA changes will let marginal projects pencil.

## Relevance
A clean statement of the supply-versus-affordability tension: streamlining helps production but does not by itself make units affordable. Useful when local streamlining is pitched as an affordability measure, since the same question applies to any Frederick permitting reform: what ensures the savings reach residents?
