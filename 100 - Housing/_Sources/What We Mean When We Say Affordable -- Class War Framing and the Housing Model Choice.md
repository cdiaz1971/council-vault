# What We Mean When We Say Affordable -- Class War Framing and the Housing Model Choice

## The Shared Diagnosis

[[Class War Framing -- Why Affordability Talk Undersells the Structural Crisis]] and [[Abundance Model vs Vienna Model -- Housing Politics Typology]] are making a closely related argument from different positions.

The class war framing note argues that "affordability" describes a consumer inconvenience when the actual experience for low-wage households is economic catastrophe. Prices rising 55-60% in the span of months is not a policy challenge to be managed. It is a class-driven extraction -- coordinated, intentional, and devastating for people without assets to cushion the blow. Calling it an "affordability problem" understates the cause, understates the harm, and implies that tinkering at the margin can solve it.

The housing typology note argues that both dominant housing policy models -- the abundance (supply-side) model and the Vienna (social housing) model -- are losing traction with a growing cohort of voters who are pro-homeownership, anti-institutional investor, and skeptical of both market solutions and large state housing programs. This cohort's politics are not captured by either model because neither model names what the cohort is actually experiencing: wealth being extracted from their communities by institutions -- corporate landlords, private equity, institutional investors -- that neither ideology was built to confront.

The through-line: both notes are pointing at a political economy reality that standard housing policy language has been built to avoid naming. The abundance model avoids it because naming class conflict undermines the technocratic neutrality the model depends on. The Vienna model names class conflict but routes it through state provision rather than community ownership, which is also not what the cohort wants. "Affordability" is the term that lets both models avoid the confrontation.

## What the Affordability Frame Leaves Out

When a city council says it wants more affordable housing, the frame implies several things that are not necessarily true:

The problem is a supply shortage that the market, properly enabled, can begin to address. This is the abundance model's premise. It locates the cause in regulatory barriers and the solution in their removal. It does not name institutional investors, speculation, or concentrated land ownership as causal factors -- because the supply model treats all supply as equivalent regardless of ownership structure.

The problem is a public goods shortfall that government provision can address. This is the Vienna model's premise. It locates the cause in market failure and the solution in non-market provision. It names ownership structure as relevant but routes the solution through government rather than community institutions.

Both frames treat housing primarily as a units question: the problem is too few of them, the solution is more of them. What the class war framing is insisting is that housing is also a power question: who owns the units, who benefits from their appreciation, who captures the surplus when land values rise, and who bears the cost when they rise beyond reach.

## The Political Economy the Cohort Is Naming

The typology note's emerging cohort -- pro-homeownership, anti-institutional investor, skeptical of both models -- is articulating something the standard frames have no language for. They are not asking for market efficiency or state provision. They are asking who took their ability to build intergenerational wealth, and why the people who took it are not held accountable.

That is a class politics question. It is also a racial wealth gap question. It is also a local government sovereignty question -- because the concentration of housing ownership in institutional hands is partly a product of policy choices made at every level of government, and local governments are among the few actors positioned to push back.

The Frederick housing politics context sits exactly here. The LMC reform work engages abundance-model tools because those are the tools the state has given municipalities. The equity commitments push toward Vienna-model goals because the market is not reaching the households the council cares about. But neither frame names what is actually happening to housing in Frederick's affordable stock: LIHTC financing structures that create temporary affordability and then convert to market rate; institutional purchasers of single-family homes in formerly working-class neighborhoods; the absence of a community land trust infrastructure to permanently steward affordability.

## What a Sharper Frame Looks Like

The class war framing is not a substitute for the wonkier policy work. It is the political frame that makes the wonkier work legible to a wider coalition. The production target resolution needs both.

The sharper version of "we need more affordable housing" is closer to: the housing crisis is not a market imperfection or a permitting delay. It is the product of decades of deliberate policy choices that transferred wealth from working households to investors -- through exclusionary zoning that drove up land values, through tax policy that rewards speculation, through the dismantling of non-market housing institutions that used to provide permanent affordability. What we are trying to build back is not just more units. It is the community capacity to own and steward housing over time.

That frame does several things the affordability frame does not. It names a cause, not just a symptom. It identifies accountable actors -- the policy choices that were made and can be unmade. It speaks to the cohort that is pro-homeownership and anti-institutional investor by offering them a common enemy that is not their neighbors who want to build a duplex. And it connects the housing work to the democratic infrastructure argument: community ownership of housing is community power, and community power is what authoritarianism dismantles first.

## Implications for Frederick's Policy Work

The production target resolution should include framing language that names the structural causes of housing shortfall, not just the numerical gap. The Abundance vs. Vienna distinction is useful internally for mapping policy tools, but the public frame should speak past both models to the community ownership question.

The CLT work -- still underweighted in Frederick's policy portfolio -- is where the class war framing and the housing model typology converge most directly. Community land trusts are the policy instrument that answers the question the emerging cohort is actually asking: how do we build housing that working people can own, that stays affordable permanently, and that does not get captured by institutional investors? That is not primarily a supply-side question or a state provision question. It is a community power question.

The framing implication for council communications: stop leading with unit counts and start leading with what housing makes possible -- for the family, for the neighborhood, for the city's fiscal health, for community sovereignty over what the city becomes.
