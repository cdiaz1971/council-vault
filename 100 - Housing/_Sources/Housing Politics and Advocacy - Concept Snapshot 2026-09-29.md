# Housing Politics and Advocacy

## Frederick

- **The ADU ordinance as a live example.** Public comment on PC25-614ZTA, which removes the ADU owner-occupancy requirement, was split:
  - an existing ADU owner spoke in support
  - Citizens for Responsible Growth wrote in opposition, citing cost and neighborhood character
  - a resident proposed a 15-year rent-and-inspection covenant

  The Planning Commission recommended keeping owner-occupancy, and its chair is on record against the draft. The production case rests on zero detached ADUs permitted since the 2021 code change ([[ADU Ordinance Brief 2026-08-13]]; Notion roadmap, checked 2026-09-24: Workshop).
- **Related pages.** Framing and allocation arguments are on [[Housing Affordability and Inequality]], the reforms themselves on [[Zoning Reform]], and demonstration projects on [[Frederick Urbanism and Placemaking]].

## The political map

- **Two models.** Michael Byrne (*The Week in Housing*) describes two dominant frameworks:
  - **The "abundance" model** is supply-side, market-oriented, and neutral on renting versus owning. It removes barriers and lets housing filter down. Its weakness is that it treats all supply as equivalent, so it can't explain why institutional investors owning single-family homes feels wrong to most voters.
  - **The "Vienna" model** calls for a large non-market sector (20%+ of housing nationally, 40%+ in big cities). It comes in a social-democratic urbanist version and a socialist version. Its weakness is that neither offers working people a path to ownership.

  ([[Abundance Model vs Vienna Model -- Housing Politics Typology]])
- **The cohort neither model reaches.** A growing group of voters doesn't fit the usual left-right lines:
  - pro-homeownership
  - hostile to institutional investors
  - skeptical of large public housing spending
  - unmoved by efficiency arguments

  They may back rules that constrain corporate buyers while opposing density. Byrne argues the durable politics will embed affordability in the language of ownership, stability, and protection from extraction. The source cites a 2025 presidential attack on institutional investors as a politician reading this cohort from the right ([[Housing Politics Realignment -- Beyond Left-Right Divide]]).
- **Populist planning.** Byrne's example is Ireland's debate over one-off rural houses. When planners answer culturally rooted aspirations with efficiency metrics alone, the backlash gets labeled populism. He also names a consistency problem: experts support culturally specific housing for marginalized groups but dismiss the same claim from rural majorities. A framework that takes cultural claims seriously for some communities and not others loses credibility with all of them ([[Populist Planning and Cultural Housing Aspirations]]).
- **Allocation as a nativist flashpoint.** Across Europe, far-right parties are recasting social housing allocation as a question of national belonging:
  - Reform UK's "two-tier system" claim
  - the Conservatives' pledge to bar foreign nationals in England
  - Rassemblement National's citizenship condition
  - Vox's years-of-residency criterion

  Byrne rejects three progressive replies: denial, treating it as a messaging problem, and dismissing voters as bad actors. He argues for a plain normative case for need-based allocation ([[Social Housing Allocation as a Nativist Flashpoint]]). The Frederick application is on [[Housing Affordability and Inequality]].

## How opposition works

- **Homeowner incentives.** A CITYBUILDER retrospective on Kim-Mai Cutler's 2014 San Francisco essay argues that homeowners have a direct financial stake in scarcity and that North Carolina is earlier in the same cycle. Early YIMBY wins came partly from publishing the inaccurate and exclusionary arguments made in public comment ([[Housing -- NIMBYism Cycle and the YIMBY Counter-Strategy]]).
- **"Vibes-based planning."** Aaron Lubeck uses the term for officials who say the right things about affordability while structuring policy against it. His example is Marblehead, Massachusetts, which designated a private golf course as its state-required multifamily zone. He gives three reasons the pattern persists:
  - few people understand zoning well enough to spot obstruction
  - local media is collapsing
  - participatory processes can't tell civic concern from organized obstruction, and meetings are dominated by people with time and fluency

  He treats YIMBY (whether to allow housing) and New Urbanism (what kind to build) as complements ([[Vibes-Based Planning -- When Cities Say They Support Housing but Vote Against It]]).
- **The abundance argument restated.** Lubeck, responding to Matthew Yglesias, frames abundance as restoring the right to build on land already zoned for it, subject only to genuine safety and environmental review. He notes that opponents often call reforms both too radical and too modest at once ([[The Radical Simplicity of Housing Abundance]]).
- **Preservation draws the same fire.** In Cary, North Carolina, the Raleigh Housing Authority sought approval to buy and preserve Amberwood at Lochmere: 340 existing units, 96% occupied, renting to households near 52% of area median income. Testimony stereotyped the current residents as a crime threat, even though no one new would be moving in ([[Cary NC NIMBY Opposition Blocks Affordable Housing Preservation at Amberwood]]).
- **Election-year votes.** Three similar rezonings on the same Raleigh corridor, before the same council in the same election year, passed or failed with different coalitions each time. That points to incumbent protection rather than project merits ([[Raleigh Housing Vote Splits -- Election-Year Rezoning Dynamics]]).
- **State laws meet local resistance.** After Texas passed a law allowing more apartments, Dallas–Fort Worth suburbs adopted design and size requirements to limit its effect. The Texas Tribune notes those suburbs have turned from cheap bedroom communities into corporate centers with some of the state's highest housing costs. Research it cites finds denser development cheaper to serve and more productive in property tax per acre ([[Housing Digest 02-14-26]]).

## Expertise, legitimacy, and imagination

- **Politics must lead.** An Irish analyst recalls that he and other progressive commentators said three measures would end Ireland's housing crisis: 10,000 social units a year, rent regulation, and cost rental. All three were adopted and the crisis continues. The advocates underestimated delivery costs and overestimated how well European models transfer. His conclusion is that "listen to the experts" rhetoric implies democracies can't solve the problem, and that trade-offs have to be settled politically ([[Limits of Expert-Led Housing Reform Politics Must Lead]]).
- **Code change is the middle step.** César's synthesis draws on Aaron Lubeck's hierarchy, The Happy Urbanist, and Better Block. Lubeck's sequence runs: cultural discontent, then political will, then legal reform, then design capacity, then good development. Skipping steps stalls reform. The synthesis draws three implications:
  - Residents can't be argued past what they've never experienced, so demonstration comes before debate. Better Block reports that $5,000–$50,000 pop-ups routinely unlock millions in follow-on public investment.
  - Hearings dominated by opponents are a poor signal, because the people who would benefit haven't had the experience that would bring them to the room.
  - Even after code changes, a bench of designers and builders fluent in human-scale housing will take about a decade to develop.

  ([[The Imagination Problem in Housing Reform -- Why Code Change Is Step 3 Not Step 1]]; sources archived under [[Frederick Urbanism and Placemaking]])

## Advocacy tools

- **Showing up.** CITYBUILDER's hearing script for non-experts:
  - arrive early
  - wear a visible pro-housing signal
  - keep comments to one plain sentence of support

  The same issue argues that a small lot split in Apex, NC shouldn't need a legislative rezoning at all ([[Apex Infill Rezonings and Housing Advocacy Playbook]]).
- **Citizen data analysis.** Before an Arlington County Board vote on the RiverHouse upzoning, a resident economist used public data to argue three points: nearby transit could absorb new riders, the upzoning would add $8–11 million a year in revenue, and housing costs had risen sharply. He was explicit about the analysis's limits ([[Citizen Data Advocacy for Housing Density]]).
- **Public maps.** Fairfax County layers housing need, demographics, transit, and site data into public dashboards. Opposition is often to "density" in the abstract, and a map of specific need at a specific place changes the conversation ([[GIS for Affordable Housing Community Buy-In -- Fairfax County Case]]).
- **Negotiate, don't just oppose.** Strong Towns' MK Lindsey argues that the "evil developer" frame gives a community veto power, which only produces "no." Deal-making logic produces benefits where development is likely anyway ([[Working With Developers as Partners Not Opponents]]).
- **Coalition partners.**
  - **Unions.** Membership reached 16.5 million in 2025, the highest since 2009, and unions are active on housing ([[Housing is a Human Right -- Ten Reasons for Realistic Hope Part Two]]).
  - **Wage and tax campaigns.** Housing costs are framed as a pay problem as much as a housing problem. 34 states and DC are above the $7.25 federal minimum wage, and California has a billionaire tax on the November ballot ([[Housing Advocacy -- Ten Reasons for Realistic Hope Part Three]]).
- **Public support is broader than hearings suggest.** NeighborWorks America's October 2025 survey of 4,201 adults found:
  - 95% call affordability important.
  - About two-thirds (67% in suburbs) would support zoning changes for affordability.
  - 64% would consider neighborhoods with mixed housing types.
  - Support for zoning changes is similar across income groups: 65% under $50,000, 62% at $50,000–100,000, and 65% over $100,000.

  ([[Housing Digest 02-14-26]])
