# Treating Child Care as Infrastructure Funding

## Summary
When Seward, Alaska received a $1 million cruise line donation for community needs, residents chose to prioritize child care rather than treat it as a discretionary program, expanding capacity and stabilizing its seasonal, tourism-dependent workforce.

## Key Points
Seward is a rural cruise port with a workforce shortage problem tied directly to lack of childcare. Year-round employers, including a state prison and a medical center, have struggled to staff shifts. A licensed childcare provider closed in 2019, and a Title I-funded preschool closed in 2021, leaving only 20 slots for more than 80 children needing care.

Norwegian Cruise Line's $1 million donation, part of a $10 million COVID-recovery package to Alaska port towns, was redirected into childcare capacity building rather than more conventional infrastructure like roads or docks. City planner Courtney Bringhurst frames the logic directly. Families cannot stay year-round without reliable childcare, and without families staying, schools and other local businesses cannot be sustained either.

Experts caution the model does not scale without public dollars. Private windfalls like this one are the exception, not a substitute for sustained public funding. The town is now pursuing a cruise ship tourism tax as a permanent funding source for childcare, explicitly framing childcare as tourism-supporting infrastructure rather than a separate social program.

## Relevance

Seward's approach, treating windfall or dedicated-tax revenue as legitimately available for childcare rather than only physical infrastructure, gives a concrete peer precedent for framing childcare spending decisions when that thread resumes.
