# 440,000 Federally Assisted Homes Face Affordability Restriction Expirations by 2030

## Summary

More than 440,000 federally assisted homes nationwide, including nearly 329,000 Low-Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) units (12.3% of tracked stock), reach subsidy or affordability-restriction expiration dates between 2026 and 2030, creating a wave of decision points where continued affordability requires new financing rather than happening automatically.

## Key Points

- LIHTC (created by Congress in 1986) originally required 15 years of affordability, extended to a 30-year minimum in 1989; some states such as Florida require or encourage 50-year terms.
- Expiration doesn't mean automatic loss of affordability -- owners can secure new subsidies, refinance under new affordability agreements, remain under overlapping restrictions, or renew rental-assistance contracts -- but each expiration is a financing decision point that can go either way.
- Project-Based Rental Assistance (PBRA) contracts behave differently: 145,408 homes (13.3% of tracked stock) have PBRA end dates 2026-2030, but owners usually renew since the ongoing payments fund operations, making opt-outs relatively rare.
- Rising interest rates, insurance premiums, labor, and materials costs compound the preservation challenge even for properties with extended restrictions. Florida alone has 66,227 assisted units 30+ years old with no recent preservation funding.
- Expiration risk is not evenly distributed geographically: Greene County, MO has 12.6% of its federally assisted stock approaching end dates versus 8.6% nationally; Los Angeles County faces 13.8% (15,135 units); New York City only 4.5% (16,900 units).
- Vincent Reina, University of Pennsylvania housing professor: federal policy shifted "from prioritizing public development to public-private partnerships -- subsidizing private development in exchange for affordability for a fixed period." Buildings seeking to keep offering lower-cost rentals "will probably need some form of subsidy to help support recapitalization" as costs rise.

## Relevance

Direct evidentiary backing for the Housing Production Target Resolution and Real Rent Ordinance work -- the structural argument for why building new units alone isn't enough. Existing subsidized stock needs active preservation financing or Frederick risks losing affordable units it already has on the books as agreements age out.
