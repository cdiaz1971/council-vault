# Single-Stair Buildings and Small Lot Zoning for Housing Production

## Summary

Comprehensive policy and design case for single-stair residential buildings paired with small lot zoning, arguing that code reform is the missing piece that could unlock local developer participation and missing middle housing production.

## Key Points

**What single-stair buildings are.** Multifamily residential buildings organized around one primary staircase serving all units, typically 3-6 stories and 4-12 units. Common throughout Europe and older North American cities; prohibited in most U.S. jurisdictions above a certain size due to fire code requirements for a second stair. The second stair requirement is justified as life safety but in practice imposes severe cost and design penalties -- requiring additional floor area, structural complexity, and double-loaded corridors that make small lot development infeasible.

**The construction efficiency argument.** Single-stair buildings maximize shared walls, floors, ceilings, and mechanical systems, dramatically reducing exterior material per unit. They also heat and cool more efficiently. The missing cost savings are substantial: eliminating one stair can free hundreds of square feet and reduce construction cost enough to make infill lots that would otherwise be unbuildable financially viable.

**The financing argument.** Large apartment projects require institutional investors and sophisticated capital structures. Single-stair buildings on small lots can be financed at human scale. For owner-occupied buildings of 4 units or fewer, Freddie Mac-backed loans underwrite the borrower (not just the project), counting ~75% of non-owner rental income toward qualifying income. This homeowner-style financing is one of the few ladders into missing middle development for local builders.

**The regulatory feedback loop.** Developers respond to what is legal and financeable, not to what would be socially optimal. When single-stair buildings are prohibited, there are no rent comps. Without comps, appraisals fall back to single-family benchmarks. Without appraisable value, financing disappears. The market then defaults to the only two legal options: detached single-family or large apartment blocks that can absorb regulatory costs through scale. The missing middle doesn't exist because the rules prohibit it, not because the market doesn't want it.

**The small lot connection.** Small lot zoning creates the regulatory environment for single-stair buildings to fill infill parcels -- vacant lots between existing houses, underbuilt corner parcels, obsolete commercial sites in residential zones. These lots are cheaper than land for large-scale development, making the investment feasible for local builders who couldn't compete for larger sites.

**Internal cross-subsidization.** Single-stair buildings can mix unit types within the same building -- studios next to family-sized units -- enabling income diversity without external subsidy. Smaller affordable units are offset by larger premium units; the building remains financially viable without pricing every unit at a premium.

## Relevance

Frederick has active housing production targets and a missing middle problem. Single-stair reform is currently moving at the state level in several jurisdictions (NC is highlighted here but Maryland has its own legislative track). This piece is the strongest student-accessible argument for why building code reform is a housing affordability intervention, not just a regulatory technicality.

The financing section is especially useful for council conversations -- it shows how small local developers can build missing middle housing using homeowner-style financing, which reframes the political argument from "big developer subsidies" to "unlocking local builder participation."

This connects directly to the Housing Production Target Resolution and the active missing middle thread. Worth having on hand for briefings and any future single-stair legislation in Maryland.
