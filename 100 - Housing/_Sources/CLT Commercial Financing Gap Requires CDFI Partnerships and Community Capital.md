# CLT Commercial Financing Gap Requires CDFI Partnerships and Community Capital

## Summary

Unlike residential CLT acquisitions, commercial CLT deals lack dedicated public subsidy programs and must pencil out the same way as for-profit development -- making CDFI relationships, community capital campaigns, and patient closing timelines essential tools. The South Tower CLT case in Fresno illustrates both what is possible and why it is harder than the housing side.

## Key Points

OakCLT executive director Steve King identifies the core structural problem: there are many established subsidy programs for nonprofits acquiring or developing residential properties, but almost none for commercial acquisitions. CLTs seeking commercial properties have to make deals work based on rental income and whatever capital they can raise through donations or private investors -- the same as a for-profit developer.

South Tower CLT's $477,000 acquisition (including renovation) was financed through three channels: a $350,000 CDFI loan from Community Vision (where the executive director had previously worked, giving him a relationship and credibility), private foundation grants, a bank motivated by CRA compliance, and individual donations as small as $5. No public subsidy program covered any of it.

CDFI lenders require three or more months to evaluate a deal -- longer than conventional lenders -- because they are underwriting mission and capacity alongside financials. This means CLTs need a patient seller or a strong negotiating position to make the timeline work. South Tower got both: a soft commercial real estate market in the Tower District meant no competing buyers and an owner willing to grant a three-month closing window.

The trust also benefited from the executive director's prior CDFI experience. For a new CLT without those relationships, the commercial financing path is harder to access. This suggests that any Frederick CLT strategy should build CDFI relationships early -- before a specific deal is on the table.

Individual community capital (donations as small as $5) contributed meaningfully to South Tower's acquisition. This is not just a financing mechanism -- it is also a community ownership signal. People who donated $5 have a stake in the building. That matters politically when the CLT needs public support or zoning relief.

## Relevance

A Frederick CLT pursuing commercial acquisition on the Golden Mile would face this same financing gap. The Maryland Housing Certainty Act and any future state revolving fund (see [[Montgomery County Housing Production Fund as State Model]]) are focused on residential. Commercial CLT financing in Frederick would likely need to be assembled the same way South Tower did it: CDFI anchor loan, foundation grants, CRA-motivated bank participation, and a community capital campaign. Worth flagging to any future Frederick CLT working group as a structural challenge to plan for from the start.
