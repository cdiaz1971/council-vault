# Vernacular Housing Typologies as a Framework for Missing Middle Reform

## Summary

Max Podemski's "A Paradise of Small Houses" argues that American cities each developed distinct vernacular housing types through local conditions, materials, and labor -- and that understanding this history gives reformers a vocabulary for arguing that dense, small-scale housing is not radical but traditional.

## Key Points

Each American city developed its signature housing type out of specific circumstances: Boston's triple-deckers served as wealth-building vehicles for immigrant families who could rent out floors while living in one unit. New Orleans shotgun houses emerged from lot width constraints and available lumber. Portland bungalows, Houston townhouses, Philadelphia rowhouses -- each was shaped by who was building, what materials were at hand, and what codes permitted or required.

The throughline is that small builders and ordinary families were the agents of urban housing production, not large developers. Regulatory accumulation -- zoning, fire codes, building standards -- progressively eliminated these typologies even as the social conditions that produced them (multigenerational households, workforce immigration) persisted or intensified.

The political implication is sharp: reformers can argue that ADUs, triple-deckers, and infill townhouses aren't foreign impositions but recoveries of what cities looked like before zoning eliminated them. The aesthetic and historical legitimacy is already there.

Houston's minimum lot size reform in the 1990s is the clearest contemporary model: simple rule change, massive supply response, price points well below neighboring metros.

## Relevance

This frames the LMC reform and missing middle work in Frederick in a different register. Rather than defending density as a policy choice, it can be positioned as restoring a historical norm that zoning disrupted. The "what did Frederick look like before suburban zoning?" question is worth surfacing publicly. The triple-decker / ADU connection is also directly applicable -- Frederick has a growing multigenerational household population and ADU reform is already on the table.

The book club event is Triangle-specific (May 28 in Raleigh), but the framework is transferable. Worth tracking Payton Chung's work.
