# Housing Water Nexus and Tap Water Avoidance in Dublin

## Summary
Research in Dublin finds a third of households rely on bottled water because aging housing and pipe infrastructure erode trust in the tap, shifting the cost and labor of safe drinking water onto low-income tenants. Water insecurity can exist in fully connected homes and should be treated as a housing habitability issue.

## Key Points
The "housing-water nexus" is the idea that housing is not a self-contained infrastructure. It depends on water and other services, and failures in one compound failures in the other. The authors call this "double precarity." Because housing and water policy sit in separate departments and agencies, the overlap tends to go unseen, and nobody owns the problem of repair and maintenance at the point where building plumbing meets the public supply.

The Plumbing Poverty project surveyed 120 households across four Dublin neighborhoods in July 2025 using the HWISE-HIC instrument (Household Water Insecurity Experiences in high-income countries), then followed up with interviews in a social housing estate in The Liberties in June 2026. It is the first documentation of tap water avoidance in Dublin.

A third of households said bottled water was their primary drinking source, with the highest rates in areas with more social housing. Residents cited taste, smell, and color, and connected those problems to old and poorly maintained building and water infrastructure. A third rated their tap water 5 or lower on a 10 point scale.

Because water in Dublin is free at the tap for most residents, the issue is treated as solved. The research shows the opposite. Buying bottled water privatizes the problem and moves the cost from the public system onto individual households. A quarter of households said getting safe, sufficient water was financially unmanageable, and some paid for courier delivery or traveled long distances to buy in bulk.

The burden is also physical and emotional. Nearly a quarter reported considerable physical effort to get water, including injuries from hauling bottles for elderly neighbors, and a quarter to a third reported mental strain, including budgeting stress, rationing, and feelings of shame and stigma. Building location, design, and condition shaped how hard this was.

The broader argument is that the affordability crisis extends past rent and supply. For households already squeezed by housing costs, distrust of the tap adds a recurring expense and unpaid labor that does not show up in any housing cost metric.

## Relevance
This reframes water as a habitability and tenant protection issue, not only a utility billing issue. Frederick's existing work in [[Water Shutoff Prevention -- Frederick Existing Programs and Proposed Legislative Actions]] and [[RUBS Strike and Utility Billing as a Tenant Protection Gap]] focuses on access and billing. This piece adds a third angle. A tenant can be connected, billed fairly, and still be water insecure if building plumbing is degraded and the landlord has no obligation to address it.

Questions worth raising locally: whether Frederick's rental licensing or code enforcement inspections cover in-unit water quality or building plumbing condition, whether older multifamily stock in District 2 has any testing history, and whether tap water avoidance is measurable here through a short survey of tenants or partner organizations. The HWISE-HIC instrument is a ready-made tool if the city or a partner ever wanted to measure this.

It also fits the pattern in [[Frederick Water, Sewer, and Stormwater]] of aging infrastructure creating costs that land unevenly. [[Divided Dublin -- Knowledge Economy Concentration and Housing Inequality]] provides background on why Dublin's housing pressures are so severe.
