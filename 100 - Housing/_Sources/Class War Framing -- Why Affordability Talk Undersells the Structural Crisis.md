# Class War Framing -- Why Affordability Talk Undersells the Structural Crisis

The framing problem: when Democrats talk about "affordability," they describe a consumer inconvenience. When prices rise 55-60% in a few months -- as the author observed across a routine grocery and garden store run in May 2026 -- the experience for low-wage workers is not an inconvenience. It is a disaster. The gap between the political frame and the lived experience is the organizing failure.

The author's core claim: the billionaire assault on wages, social supports, and democratic participation is coordinated class warfare, not a policy disagreement. The rich are not unusually intelligent; they are unusually self-absorbed and concentrated enough to act in their own interest while everyone else absorbs the cost.

## The policy prescriptions named

Dramatically higher top-bracket taxation (90%+ for the highest bracket), wealth taxation, removal of the Social Security tax cap, elimination of Citizens United, and hard limits on campaign contributions. The argument is not that these are new ideas -- it is that the scale of the attack requires matching the scale of the response.

## The organizing prescription

Elect candidates who see the structural reality clearly. Keep activism loud and visible. Control the algorithms by engaging -- likes, restacks, shares -- to surface alternative framing. United action can end oligarchy; passive hope cannot.

## Why this matters for César

The affordability language problem is directly relevant to how Frederick frames housing, transit, and budget conversations. Saying "we need more affordable housing" is true but insufficient -- it does not communicate that the housing crisis is a product of concentrated wealth, speculation, and deliberate policy choices that benefit a small class of property owners and investors. The class war frame is sharper and more honest, even if it requires more courage to use in a municipal context.
