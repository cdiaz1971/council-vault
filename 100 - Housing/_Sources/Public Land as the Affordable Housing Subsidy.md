# Public Land as the Affordable Housing Subsidy

## Summary

New York City is redeveloping 100 Gold Street, an aging city office building in Lower Manhattan, into roughly 4,000 homes with about 1,000 permanently affordable, rent-stabilized units and no city funding. The land itself carries the subsidy, a model relevant to the city-owned land RFP in César's housing resolution.

## Key Points

100 Gold Street currently houses city agencies. Under the Mamdani administration's plan, the site becomes a mixed-income development of roughly 4,000 homes, with about a quarter permanently affordable and rent-stabilized. The program also includes an older adult center, public open space, a fitness center, and about 40,000 square feet of neighborhood amenities. The city says the project will nearly double the number of permanently affordable homes built in Manhattan Community District 1 over the past decade.

The financial structure is the core idea. The city contributes no capital funding. Instead it contributes the land, and the value of that land, combined with the market-rate units, pays for the permanent affordability. Public ownership also lets the city set the affordability terms as a condition of disposition rather than negotiating them against a private landowner.

The project grew substantially under the current administration. It is roughly four times the size of the version former Mayor Eric Adams introduced in 2025, a reminder that the density allowed on public land is itself a policy choice. Public review began in September 2026, with a hearing scheduled for October.

## Relevance

The Resolution on Affordable Housing Action names a city-owned land RFP among its nine priority recommendations. This is a working example of what that recommendation produces at scale, and of the design choices behind it: permanent rather than time-limited affordability, a defined affordable share, community amenities written into the deal, and density sized to the affordability goal.

Two cautions apply when carrying the model to Frederick. First, the land subsidy is only as strong as the land value. Lower Manhattan land is worth far more per square foot than anything Frederick owns, so in Frederick the land may close only part of the financing gap, and pairing it with the City Housing Fund, LIHTC, or a CDFI partner is likely necessary. Second, the deal depends on the City being willing to relocate its own operations. Whether any City facilities are aging, underused, or slated for consolidation is a question worth putting to CAO Barkdoll, since an office move could open a site the same way.
