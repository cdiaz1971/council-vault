# Rockville Chapter 18 Rewrite - Staff Reports and Legislative Record

## Summary
Six staff reports document Rockville's rewrite of City Code Chapter 18 from first work session to adoption. The adopted text is summarized separately in [[Rockville Chapter 18 Rewrite - Adopted Ordinance Text]]. This note captures what the ordinance alone does not show: why staff recommended each piece, the evidence and peer models used, what changed or was dropped between drafts, implementation capacity, and stakeholder objections. Copies are in Google Drive under City council / 02 - Legislation / Tenant Protection.

**Timeline**
- Fall 2024: rewrite identified as a key action in Rockville's Housing Strategies Work Plan. Housing is one of the Mayor and Council's five focus areas.
- Spring 2025: research and engagement began. Virtual landlord input meeting May 20, 2025, tenant meeting May 27, 2025, plus interviews and an Engage Rockville survey, comment form, and question portal. A second round of input meetings was held April 15 and 16, 2026.
- June 16, 2025: work session on transparency and data.
- November 10, 2025: work session on rental license inspections.
- December 8, 2025: first policy work session.
- February 23, 2026: second policy work session.
- May 18, 2026: draft ordinance introduced.
- June 29 to July 6, 2026: revised draft adopted. Effective January 1, 2027. Staff will then draft regulations and a Landlord-Tenant Handbook.

**Stated goals of the rewrite:** clarify existing code, align with neighboring jurisdictions, improve housing stability and tenant agency, require greater transparency, align with Maryland law, use local and national best practices, and connect data collection to city goals.

## Key Points

**Administration.** Chapter 18 enforcement is split between the Code Enforcement division of Community Planning and Development Services (licensing and inspections) and the Housing Programs Division of DHCD (landlord-tenant affairs and the Commission).

**Transparency and data (June 16, 2025)**
- The old Article V required monthly unit-level rent reports from complexes of eight or more units, quarterly reports to the City Manager, and filing of "any type of lease used by any landlord." Staff lacked capacity to collect most of it and were gathering only partial data through annual licensing.
- Staff recommended annual reporting at license renewal instead of monthly, standard leases only, removing the nonprofit exemption so affordable properties appear on a public dashboard, and unit-level reporting (unit, bedrooms, square footage, current rent) so staff can compute year-over-year increases themselves. Daily fines and eventual license revocation for failure to report were proposed.
- **Public dashboard concept:** an interactive map of rental properties showing structure type, owner, age, units, amenities, included utilities, fees, license status, code infractions, average rent by bedroom size and per square foot, and rent increase information.
- Staff weighed how to display rent increases and **recommended a color-coded tier based on five-year average increases rather than raw percentages**, partly because publishing recent raw increases could help landlords coordinate prices upward.
- Lease-signing disclosures considered: a simplified renter's bill of rights, the property's or unit's last three increases, recent code violations, average utility costs, fee schedule, and city and state landlord-tenant office contacts.

**Rental license inspections (November 10, 2025)**
- Staff: seven inspectors, one manager, one administrative assistant. About 2,500 single-family rentals (inspected at each two-year renewal) and about 9,135 multifamily units.
- Multifamily: 20% of units inspected before annual renewal. Most common violations: broken or inoperable appliances, inoperable smoke alarms, infestation, wall damage, broken locks.
- Changes from January 2026: stagger license dates (renewals were all March 31), raise the sample from 20% to 25% (each unit inspected about every four years instead of five, roughly 100 more inspections per inspector per year), and pilot an **Enhanced Inspection Program**.
- **Enhanced inspection triggers:** if 25% of sampled units have missing or broken smoke detectors, pest infestation, broken entrance doors or locks, or suspected mold, or 10% fail to hold 68°F in heating season, the sample rises to 40%. Other violations (leaks, minor wall or tile, carpet, refrigerator gaskets, routine HVAC) get a notice of violation and must be fixed before licensing.
- Seven enforcement tools: inspect at application or renewal, inspect on reasonable suspicion, resident complaints, deny, suspend, revoke, cite. Complaints are answered within 24 to 48 hours. **Staff were not aware of any recent suspension, revocation, or denial**, because managers usually fix violations within the deadline. Citations can be $1,000 per violation per day, but contested citations stall for months waiting for court dates.
- **Maryland Tenant Mold Protection Act (July 2025)** passed with no state implementation resources and unclear local enforcement responsibility, though the fiscal note assumed local enforcement. Rockville began enforcing anyway, requiring professional air quality testing when a black substance is seen and contracting an air quality consultant.
- Peer licensing comparison: Gaithersburg (annual, no pre-license inspection, monthly rolling inspections), Montgomery County (annual, complaint-driven), Prince George's County (biannual, inspection before renewal).

**First policy session (December 8, 2025)**
- Code changes proposed: offer two-year leases (Montgomery County alignment), more early termination grounds, lease copy two days before signing and executed copy within three business days, renewal letter rent must match the lease, tenant organizing protections including free meeting rooms and the right to invite outside organizers, a fee-free non-electronic payment option, rental payment history on request, hard-copy delivery of all legally required notices, and the data changes above.
- **Algorithmic pricing:** RealPage clients make up nearly 90% of the U.S. market for multifamily buildings of 50+ units, and the White House Council of Economic Advisers estimated one in four multifamily providers use a RealPage algorithm, adding about $70 a month to rent nationally and $112 in the D.C. region. **Eight Rockville properties, nearly 2,000 market-rate apartments, are managed by companies named in the U.S. DOJ suit.** As of October 2025, 27 management companies had proposed class settlements over $141 million. New York, California, and eleven cities had bans. Rockville testified for state SB 609 (2025), which died in committee. Berkeley's broader ban, covering any rent-setting algorithm, drew a RealPage First Amendment suit and was postponed. Staff therefore recommended a ban limited to devices using **non-public competitor data**, covering fees and other terms, exempting coordination among one owner's own properties, and drafted so the City's own rent data publication would not violate it. Definitions follow Minneapolis and Philadelphia.
- **Court costs and legal fees:** filing a failure-to-pay-rent case costs $50 to $60 and a warrant about $40. Court costs are set by the state and cannot be regulated locally. Staff recommended Montgomery County's lease rule: no tenant liability for attorney fees unless court-awarded, fees are not rent for redemption, and fee clauses must be reciprocal.
- **Fees:** staff chose Montgomery County's approach of listing allowed fees rather than setting general limits, but declined the county's inflation-indexed fee caps because those exist to stop circumvention of the county's rent stabilization, which Rockville does not have. **Staff recommended that tenants be able to recover up to three times any fee charged in violation, plus attorney fees, with claims allowed during the tenancy or within two years after.** That private remedy does not appear in the adopted ordinance.
- Staff did not recommend late fee changes because state law already caps late fees at 5% (with $3 a week and $12 a month limits for weekly leases) and limits them to the unpaid portion.
- **Guarantors:** guarantor company fees run 4% to 10% of annual rent, up to $2,400 on a $2,000 apartment. Staff found no locality restricting when a guarantor can be required and recommended allowing it only for insufficient income, limited credit, unstable employment, or no rental history, with written reasons and Commission complaint information. Adopted version kept only the written reason requirement.
- **Repair and deduct:** modeled on Montgomery County (24 to 48 hours for life-threatening repairs, 15 to 30 days otherwise, 48 hours for repeat violations), California (one month's rent, twice a year), and Virginia (licensed contractor). Staff recommended raising the cap to two months' rent given Rockville rents over $2,000. In the adopted text, repair and deduct runs through City Manager authorization after a failed inspection deadline, not directly at tenant election.
- **Engagement themes.** Tenants: rising rents and desire for rent stabilization, new fees (technology, amenity, application, hold, court, eviction, month-to-month), city-county discrepancies, lack of information on license status, violations, contacts, and past increases, wish for relocation assistance, slow maintenance, retaliation, and no AC requirement. Landlords: rising costs, slow evictions, a harder licensing process than the county's, staff responsiveness, keeping up with dual city-county rules, and confusion over voluntary rent guidelines.

**Second policy session (February 23, 2026)**
- **Fee caps:** no study anywhere measures the effect of fee caps. Staff expected some landlords to fold banned fee revenue into base rent and judged a fair, understandable fee regime worth that short-term adjustment.
- **Two-year leases:** existing "reasonable cause" language was judged enough to protect small landlords. In Rockville, 46.4% of renters have been in place one year, about 36% two to four years, and 15.5% five to 14 years. Brookings found about half of renters stay more than two years and about a third would prefer longer leases.
- **Eviction prevention:** five strategies were listed: emergency rental assistance (REAP, with demand growing sharply and a median recipient household income of $20,800), security deposit assistance, legal counsel (tenants with counsel are two to three times more likely to stay housed, and the City may supplement the state's program), affordable housing investment, and preventive programs such as Bank On. Staff flagged a concern that some landlords delay filing until arrears exceed $10,000, beyond what public and nonprofit funds can cover, because assistance requires an active court case. No recommendation was made.
- **Background checks:** staff recommended adopting Montgomery County's Housing Justice Act (Chapter 27, § 27-15A): no criminal history questions or checks before a conditional offer, no use of arrests without conviction, a list of excluded minor offenses, misdemeanors older than two years excluded, and a seven-day window before revoking an offer. **They later learned Rockville's code already incorporates all of Montgomery County Chapter 27**, so these restrictions already apply in Rockville. Staff separately recommended banning eviction-history questions on applications, which was adopted. The report also notes Maryland's 2024 eviction-record shielding for failure-to-pay-rent cases filed after October 1, 2024 that did not end in possession, and petition-based shielding after redemption plus 12 months. HB 1077 (2025), which would have limited criminal screening statewide, failed.
- **Relocation peer review:** Montgomery County Bill 18-19 (2020, condemned units), Takoma Park (two months' rent plus deposit for permanent displacement), D.C., Arlington, Fairfax, Minneapolis, Portland ($2,900 to $4,500 by unit size), Seattle ($5,354 split between landlord and city for low-income tenants), California, and Santa Monica as the main model. **Minneapolis paid $170,523 to tenants over three years and recovered only $43,390, about a quarter, with recovery taking about two years at best.** Rockville's adopted answer is a property lien collected like taxes.
- **Permanent relocation cost example:** a five-year tenant at $2,000 a month displaced mid-month would receive about $9,782 ($2,282 deposit with interest, $1,000 prorated rent, $6,000 for three months' rent, $500 moving). Staff proposed deducting owed rent from the payment. The adopted version requires payment regardless of arrears.
- **Penalties:** Rockville's maximum fine under Chapter 18 was only $100. **Maryland raised the maximum municipal infraction penalty from $1,000 to $5,000 as of October 2025.** Staff recommended a fine schedule up to $5,000 by violation type, set by resolution.
- **Future items outside this rewrite:** a landlord-tenant handbook, lease addendum, and fee schedule template; grants or nonprofit contracts to support tenant organizing; expanded eviction counsel; a Tenant Opportunity to Purchase law beyond state law's three-unit limit (Rockville already gives condo tenants a purchase opportunity); a cooling requirement like Montgomery County's 80°F standard; commercial tenant protections for immigrant-owned small businesses; and senior living consumer protections.

**Draft introduced (May 18, 2026)**
- Tenant feedback: strong, consistent demand for rent stabilization and disappointment it was left out, enthusiasm for the algorithm ban and fee limits, concern that six months of displacement before permanent relocation is too long, and interest in an online rental property map.
- Landlord feedback: a general sense of bias toward tenants, objections to the two-year lease offer (they wanted to keep mid-term increases), the eviction-history ban (some suggested a look-back limit instead), tenant repair without landlord approval, and relocation cost and fault determination; requests to allow returned check fees and higher repeat lockout fees.
- Equity analysis: renters are 45.1% of Rockville households. Black households are 11.4% of all households but 19.8% of renter households. Median renter income is $86,690 against $186,717 for owners. 53% of renters are cost burdened and over 20% severely. Hispanic, multiracial, "some other race," and Black households are disproportionately burdened. The City has no landlord demographic data.
- Economic analysis: neutral overall. Possible exit by small landlords and some upward rent pressure, against housing stability gains and slight downward pressure from transparency.
- Montgomery County Bill 8-26 (price coordination and rent-setting algorithms) is described as largely similar, with impact statements attached.

**Changes made before adoption (June 29 to July 6, 2026)**
- Added definitions of "written notice" and "tenant organization."
- Clarified that the Commission's triple-damages award for deposits over one month applies only absent a valid state-law exception.
- Tenants must allow properly noticed City inspections.
- **Landlords may raise rent during a two-year lease only if the increase is disclosed in the lease up front.**
- Added a "non-public competitor data" definition to the algorithm ban in response to RealPage's written testimony. Staff reported that all algorithm-ban lawsuits it reviewed were brought by RealPage and were settled or still pending.
- Payment history timing changed to "seven business days" in the staff summary. **Note: the adopted redline reads 14 days**, so check the final codified text.
- Lease termination for over-income tenants extended to all income-restricted units, so those tenants can be offered leases longer than one year.
- Added the ban on rent payment fees (except credit card and bounced check fees), an exemption for introducing RUBS or utility cost recovery, extended the stale-charge window from 45 to 120 days, allowed valet trash fees if regular trash remains an option, and narrowed the storage fee rule to unit-associated storage.
- Relocation: alternative agreements no longer need City-set criteria, temporary assistance ends at execution of a warrant of restitution, and tenant-caused unfitness is excluded from both temporary and permanent assistance.
- Advertising fee disclosure exempts third-party websites. Utility estimates are "to the best of their ability."
- Staff's comparison of early termination grounds with state law: state law already covers abuse (30-day move-out window, documentation required), active-duty military reassignment, and certain medical conditions with liability capped at two months' rent. State law does not cover job relocation, unfit units, license revocation, unrepaired violations, death of a wage earner, or "other reasonable cause."

## Relevance
- **Capacity reality check.** Even with seven inspectors, Rockville had not suspended or revoked a license recently and contested citations stall in court. Frederick should expect the same and design enforcement that does not depend on court dates: administrative orders, license conditions, and liens.
- **Municipal infraction ceiling.** Maryland's October 2025 increase in the municipal infraction maximum from $1,000 to $5,000 applies to Frederick. Confirm Frederick's current schedule and whether its tenant-related fines take advantage of it.
- **The private remedy dropped out.** Staff proposed triple damages for fee violations through tenant claims. The adopted ordinance relies on Commission orders and municipal infractions instead. That pattern matches Frederick's *Aleti* problem and is worth asking Rockville about directly.
- **Relocation costs are recoverable only with a lien.** Minneapolis's 25% recovery rate is the cautionary data point. Rockville's lien mechanism is the fix to copy if Frederick funds relocation up front.
- **Mold law.** Frederick faces the same unfunded state mold mandate. Whether Frederick code enforcement enforces it, and how, is a live question.
- **Criminal screening.** Rockville gets Montgomery County's Housing Justice Act by incorporation. Frederick has no county equivalent to borrow, so a local fair chance rule would have to be its own ordinance building on the state conditional-offer law (see [[Maryland Tenants' Bill of Rights - October 2026 Edition]]).
- **Data and display.** The tiered five-year rent increase display is a thoughtful answer to the worry that publishing rent data could help landlords coordinate.
- **Political map.** Tenant demand for rent stabilization and landlord objections to two-year leases, repair and deduct, and the eviction-history ban are the arguments Frederick should expect.
