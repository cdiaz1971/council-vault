# Housing Starts at Pandemic-Era Low -- Supply Shock Deepening

## Summary

May 2026 housing starts are down 8.5% year-over-year and at the lowest level since April 2020, driven by higher material and financing costs. Annual completion rates are down 14.2%. The national supply contraction is direct macroeconomic context for Frederick's Housing Production Target Resolution.

## Key Points

**The numbers:**
- May 2026 housing starts: lowest since April 2020 (pandemic onset)
- Year-over-year decline: 8.5% nationally
- Regional concentration: South (-15%) and West (-11%) hit hardest; Northeast and Midwest saw increases
- Annual completion rate: approximately 1.3 million units, down 14.2% from May 2025 -- lowest since January 2022
- Apartment permits: roughly flat nationally, but down 26% in the Midwest

**The cause:** Builder confidence has dropped because of higher material and financing costs. This is not a demand-side problem -- it is a supply-side constraint driven by input costs and credit conditions.

**Why it matters nationally:** The gap between housing demand and production was already severe before this contraction. A sustained dip in starts means the backlog deepens and any near-term affordability pressure cannot be addressed through market-rate production alone. Subsidy programs, community ownership models, and regulatory reform all become more important as the private market contracts.

## Relevance

Frederick's Housing Production Target Resolution (1,400 units/year through 2035) is swimming against a national tide. The data here does not argue against the resolution -- it argues for it. If national starts are declining, Frederick needs an explicit local production target more than ever to signal to builders, lenders, and state partners that this is a priority jurisdiction.

The [[Maryland Housing Supply Crisis -- Inventory Down 16.4 Percent, Prices Up 4.7 Percent in 2026]] note already captured the Maryland-specific picture. This adds the national frame: the Maryland crisis is not a local anomaly. It is a concentrated version of a national contraction.

The South and West data (-15%, -11%) is worth noting because those regions are where most housing innovation and deregulation has happened -- parking reform, ADU reform, upzoning. Even there, starts are falling. This complicates pure supply-side arguments and strengthens the case for coupling production targets with subsidy and community ownership strategies.
