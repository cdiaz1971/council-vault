# Retrofitting Affordable Housing for Geothermal Is the Hardest and Most Necessary Problem

## Summary

New construction geothermal is easier and flows to higher-income developments; retrofit geothermal in naturally occurring affordable housing is technically harder, more expensive, and equitably necessary -- a structural tension that Maryland's stalled pilot program makes concrete.

## Key Points

Del. Lorig Charkoudian named the problem directly in discussing the WARMTH Act delays: "It would be much easier to do this in new construction, but new construction is likely to be higher-income communities. Doing this as a retrofit in naturally occurring affordable housing is technically the hardest thing to do... and yet it is the equitable and appropriate thing to do."

This is a generalizable observation that extends well beyond geothermal. The pattern recurs across energy efficiency, building upgrades, and environmental remediation: the properties and communities that most need the intervention are structurally harder to serve because:

1. Retrofit is technically more complex and expensive than building it in from the start
2. Naturally occurring affordable housing is owned by landlords whose cost pressures already constrain capital improvement
3. Regulatory cost-benefit frameworks evaluate total program cost against ratepayer impact, which effectively weights the interests of the broader customer base against the specific community being served
4. Equity-focused programs must navigate the gap between program design intent and the economic logic of the entities executing them (utilities, developers)

The Maryland pilot is trying to crack this by making utilities the constructors and spreading the cost across the ratepayer base. The PSC rejected the proposals as too expensive -- which may be correct -- but the result is that the intended beneficiaries are still waiting while the cost structure gets renegotiated by parties who don't live in the buildings.

## Relevance

This framing is useful for any affordability or infrastructure policy argument where César needs to explain why the technically simple path and the equitable path diverge. It's the same logic that applies to MPDU reform (easier to build market-rate than affordable), APFO reform (easier to permit in established neighborhoods than underserved areas), and energy access. The structural claim: equity requires doing the harder thing deliberately, because the market will always choose the easier path.
