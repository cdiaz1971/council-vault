# Affordability as Power -- Demos on Structural Drivers of Economic Insecurity

## Summary

Demos president Taifa Smith Butler argues that the affordability crisis is fundamentally a story about power imbalance -- corporate concentration shaping markets and policy at the expense of Black and brown families -- not simply about prices or inflation.

## Key Points

- Conventional political framing centers affordability on price reduction (inflation, supply/demand), but Smith Butler proposes a structural reframe: affordability is about who holds economic and political power and how the rules of the economy are written
- Corporate concentration in housing, childcare, utilities, and grocery markets allows a small number of actors to shape both prices and policy -- disproportionately harming Black and brown families
- The question is not just "how do we lower costs" but "what would it take to build an economy that works for everyone" -- a structural question requiring more than short-term fixes
- Demos is releasing a fuller report on affordability shortly; this piece is a preview framing essay

## Relevance

This reframe is politically useful for council work. The affordability narrative in Frederick often gets trapped in supply/demand debates that obscure the underlying power dynamics -- who owns land, who sets rents, who funds campaigns. Connecting affordability to corporate power and racial equity gives the Housing Production Target and City Values Act a stronger structural argument. The Demos frame also aligns with the ALICE data: Frederick households are squeezed not because they're not working but because the rules are written against them.
