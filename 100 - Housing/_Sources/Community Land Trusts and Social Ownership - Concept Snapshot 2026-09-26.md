# Community Land Trusts and Social Ownership

## Frederick

- **No trust yet.** Frederick has no community land trust and no City–CLT relationship ([[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]]).
- **State law.** HB 85 / SB 511 (2026) created a state process for converting entities into cooperative limited-equity housing corporations. That removes one legal obstacle to CLT-plus-co-op structures ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]).
- **Maryland land trusts.** About fifteen affordable-housing land trusts operate in Maryland, concentrated in Baltimore and the DC suburbs.
  - **College Park Community Preservation Trust:** launched in 2023 with $14.9 million from the City of College Park, DHCD, federal Community Project Funding, and the Metropolitan Washington Council of Governments. By 2024 it had bought, renovated, and sold nine single-family homes to income-qualified buyers under permanent affordability covenants. It's the closest in-state model, and it's funded in a way Frederick could replicate without running a trust itself.
  - **South Baltimore Community Land Trust:** supported through DHCD's Reinvest Baltimore, it's the most active Baltimore-area trust.

  ([[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]])
- **Lucas Village.** On April 16, 2026, Council approved three items for the Lucas Village LIHTC redevelopment:
  - a parkland dedication waiver (7-0)
  - a right-of-way adjustment
  - the transfer of an unused City-owned parcel (6-1)

  Council Member Nash voted no on the transfer over concerns about how the City disposes of property ([[Hearing Notes 2026-04-16]]).
- **Right of first refusal.** At the September 14, 2026 Government Operations workshop, Nash proposed a Charter amendment:
  - extend the public notice for disposing of City property from 20 to 30 days
  - give nonprofits serving City residents a 30-day right of first refusal at a "market-competitive price"

  The brief flags it as a possible route for a housing nonprofit or land trust to acquire City parcels ([[Government Operations Committee Brief 2026-09-14]]). Following the workshop, Nash is drafting a policy (per César, 2026-09-25).
- **City land.** "RFPs for City Land" is in Committee Review at Government Operations (Notion roadmap, checked 2026-09-24).
- **Planning-stage work,** including a CLT initiative and a public land disposition policy, is tracked in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]]. Funding vehicles are on [[Housing Finance and Preservation]].

## How land trusts work

- **Separating land from buildings.** A CLT holds land permanently. Residents own or rent the buildings, and a resale formula caps appreciation so the next buyer also pays an affordable price. The land never returns to the market ([[Community Land Trusts as Civic and Political Infrastructure]]).
- **Why it differs from covenant programs.** MPDU and LIHTC affordability ends after a covenant period (40 years under Frederick's MPDU program), and then the units return to market. A trust's affordability is permanent and accumulates as land is added. The main cost is upfront land acquisition, after which ongoing subsidy needs fall ([[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]]).
- **Scale.** The Champlain Housing Trust in Burlington, Vermont is the oldest major US model. Nakagawa counts over 300 CLTs nationally ([[Community Land Trusts as Civic and Political Infrastructure]]). Next City counts roughly 225, most focused on housing ([[Commercial CLT Acquisition Lowers Barrier for Mission-Aligned Small Businesses]]). The movement began with Black farmers protecting land in the rural South (New Communities, Inc.).
- **Shared equity recycles subsidy.** CLTs and limited-equity co-ops keep a subsidy working across resales instead of letting the first owner capture it. Examples:
  - Grounded Solutions Network reports rising government interest in making shared-equity projects eligible for funding.
  - Nashville's Barnes Housing Trust Fund added scoring for community ownership, producing new limited-equity co-ops.
  - Oregon restructured its LIFT program in 2024. Rosa Village Co-op (52 permanently affordable homes) was the first co-op funded.

  ([[Shared Equity Housing -- Making Subsidies Permanent]])

## Land trusts and co-ops together

- **Canada vs. the US.** Co-ops are a substantial share of Canadian CLT housing but under 2% of US CLT housing. That reflects historical Canadian federal support for co-ops, not any incompatibility. In the combined model the trust holds the land and guarantees affordability, and a resident co-op governs the buildings democratically. US examples include Vermont's CLT network and Washington's Lopez Island CLT. The obstacles are financing co-op shares inside land leases, lender unfamiliarity, and staff capacity in small trusts ([[CLT and Co-op Housing Integration -- Lessons from Canada]]).

## Public land into trusts

- **Pensacola.** After deciding repairs to the vacant Malcolm Yonge Gym would exceed $3 million, Pensacola demolished it and routed the site to the Northwest Florida Community Land Trust, which Pensacola Habitat for Humanity created in 2022. The trust will build nine homes with annual costs capped at a third of a qualified buyer's income. It's the city's first project with permanent rather than time-limited affordability ([[Pensacola Uses Community Land Trust to Guarantee Permanent Affordability on City-Donated Land]]).
- **Sources of land for a trust.** César's brief identifies four sources:
  - City-owned parcels, with Lucas Village as the precedent for donating land
  - faith-community land
  - contributions from LIHTC and mission-driven developers
  - housing contributions negotiated in community benefit agreements

  ([[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]])

## Commercial trusts and neighborhood control

- **South Tower CLT, Fresno.** The trust bought a 2,300 sq ft building for $295,000 ($477,000 with renovation) and leased it to an indie bookstore that couldn't find affordable space. The owner then joined the board. The trust also shaped a new neighborhood plan adopted by Fresno in 2025 ([[Commercial CLT Acquisition Lowers Barrier for Mission-Aligned Small Businesses]]). Commercial deals have almost no public subsidy programs, so this one used:
  - a $350,000 CDFI loan
  - foundation grants
  - a bank motivated by the Community Reinvestment Act (CRA)
  - donations as small as $5

  CDFI underwriting takes three months or more, so trusts need patient sellers and CDFI relationships built in advance ([[CLT Commercial Financing Gap Requires CDFI Partnerships and Community Capital]]).
- **Kensington Corridor Trust, Philadelphia.** It bought a building for $225,000 in 2023. With about $800,000 invested, it now houses the neighborhood's first grocery store in years, which accepts EBT, prices about 30% below the owner's other location, and includes a shared commercial kitchen ([[Neighborhood Trusts as a Model for Community-Controlled Commercial Real Estate]]). Its director frames the goal as power, not services: "amass some land to amass some wealth to amass some power, so we can change policy and change systems" ([[Community-Owned Real Estate as a Path to Wealth and Political Power]]).

## Transfers after landlord failure

- **New York.** Mayor Zohran Mamdani announced in 2026 that the city will pursue ownership transfers from its worst landlords to responsible stewards, explicitly including tenants. The source suggests the mechanism is likely New York's existing Article 7-A receivership. With tenant co-ops or trusts as recipients, enforcement produces permanently affordable housing rather than a new landlord. Details weren't public at the time ([[Mamdani's Bad Landlord Policy -- Receivership and Tenant Ownership Transfer]]). Frederick's parallel gap, what happens after a rental license is revoked, is on [[Tenant Protections]].

## Municipal social housing

- **Cambridge.** Cambridge, Massachusetts formed a Social Housing Task Force in June 2026. It follows a September 2025 policy order and an April 2026 Finance Committee vote making social housing a budget priority, with $51 million for affordable housing in the FY2027 budget.
  - The model: publicly financed, publicly owned, mixed-income, and permanently affordable, with at least 40% of units at or below 80% AMI.
  - It's financed through low-interest revolving loans, cross-subsidy, and reinvested surplus.
  - Cambridge cites Montgomery County's revolving fund as the closest US model.
  - Social housing often doesn't qualify for LIHTC, which limits how many units can be produced.

  ([[Cambridge Social Housing Task Force -- Peer City Moving From Policy to Implementation]])

## Scaling and the civic case

- **State coalitions.** Community ownership has scaled where state coalitions organize across multiple legislative sessions:
  - Oregon amended its tax exemption and tax credit to include limited-equity co-ops.
  - Missoula's CLTs are building on Montana's 2023 zoning reforms.
  - Virginia's coalition won five housing bills in 2026.
  - Texas passed a missing-middle package.

  The common features are cross-sector membership (nonprofits, labor, faith, legal), flexibility on bill language, and continuity across sessions ([[State-Level CLT and Co-op Coalitions Are Building Durable Policy Infrastructure]]; [[How State Coalitions Are Advancing Community Ownership of Housing]]).
- **Civic participation.** CLT residents tend to show unusually high civic participation because they have a lasting material stake in how the community is governed ([[Community Land Trusts as Civic and Political Infrastructure]]).
- **The political frame.** César's synthesis argues that a trust answers the homeownership-aspiration cohort neither the abundance nor the Vienna model reaches: ownership with the land cost removed and affordability made permanent. It isn't rental, public housing, or corporate development. It calls CLTs three kinds of infrastructure at once:
  - **rules:** who can own
  - **coalition:** an organized constituency with a lasting stake
  - **relational:** governance that requires working through disagreement

  The natural coalition runs through mission-driven developers, CDFIs, faith communities holding land, and immigrant-serving organizations, not market-rate developers ([[CLT as the Political Answer for the Homeownership-Aspiration Cohort]]). César's brief adds that for District 2, the civic dimension is central, not secondary ([[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]]). The cohort itself is described on [[Housing Politics and Advocacy]].
