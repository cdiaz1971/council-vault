# Cohousing Affordability -- Why the US Model Struggles to Scale

## Summary

Cohousing -- intentional communities with private homes and shared common spaces -- has long promised lower costs through resource sharing and smaller unit sizes. In practice, US cohousing communities remain expensive to build and demographically homogeneous. The barriers are structural: high upfront development costs, zoning hostility, lack of public subsidy, and a developer fee model that punishes small-scale projects.

## Key Points

Cohousing communities reduce ongoing costs through shared meals, shared equipment, childcare networks, and pooled resources. But the initial acquisition and construction cost is often higher per unit than conventional development. Smaller unit counts mean fewer square feet over which to amortize land, site work, and permitting costs. Developers working at small scale cannot access the efficiencies available to large corporate builders.

US cohousing lacks the public subsidies available in European countries (particularly Scandinavia and Germany), where cohousing is a mainstream tenure type. Without subsidy, projects must be entirely market-financed, which restricts participation to higher-income households. Studies have found that roughly 95% of US cohousers are white and two-thirds hold graduate degrees -- a demographic profile shaped as much by financial access as by preference.

Key barriers identified by practitioners: zoning codes that do not recognize the cohousing model, requiring case-by-case approval and adding time and cost; difficulty obtaining conventional construction financing for projects without a track record; and the need for a "burning soul" developer who will absorb the professional work of project coordination without a fee, since eliminating the developer margin (typically 15%) is often the only way to make numbers work.

Workarounds that have succeeded: partnering with nonprofit affordable housing developers, using deed restrictions to cap resale prices, retrofitting existing housing stock rather than building new, and developing rental rather than ownership units to reduce barriers to entry.

## Why This Matters for César

Cohousing is worth understanding as a housing typology that the LMC review could either enable or obstruct. Frederick's land management code may not explicitly address cohousing -- if so, that's a gap. The model also illustrates the broader principle that supply-side reform alone doesn't produce affordable housing: without subsidy or public financing, market-rate development (including cohousing) serves upper-income households regardless of its community design intentions.
