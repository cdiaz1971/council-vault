# What Happens After License Revocation -- The Missing Piece in Frederick's Tenant Protection Stack

## Thesis

Frederick is building a tenant protection stack -- the Real Rent Ordinance, the rental licensing rewrite, the SRO amendment -- but every piece of that stack stops at punishment. None of them answers the question that matters most to tenants: when a bad landlord finally loses their license, who takes over the building, and does anything good come of it? That gap is not a technical oversight. It is the difference between enforcement as deterrence and enforcement as transformation.

## The Pattern Across the Stack

Three active legislative threads share the same structural hole.

The rental licensing ordinance rewrite (Brehm's draft, Public Works workshop May 26) establishes a 3-inspection revocation escalation pathway. If a landlord accumulates three formal violations, their license is revoked. That is a real consequence -- stronger than what exists now. But the draft is silent on what happens to the ~7,800 licensed units at the moment of revocation. Tenants in a revoked building have no housing rights that the ordinance itself protects. The building does not become affordable; it does not transfer to anyone. It sits in a legal limbo that the existing HHS referral provision handles by directing tenants toward social services -- which is a safety net response, not a housing policy response.

The SRO Ordinance's 10-section amendment package similarly builds out inspection, registration, maintenance, and anti-conversion standards with a revocation escalation pathway. SROs are the most vulnerable housing stock in Frederick: they house people with the least mobility, the highest proportion of immigrants and low-income workers, and the least capacity to navigate displacement. A revoked SRO license with no stewardship successor is a displacement event by another name.

The Real Rent Ordinance addresses prohibited fees, anti-circumvention, and rent increase limits. It is stronger tenant protection than the licensing ordinance, but it operates on the assumption that the tenancy continues. It has no provision for what happens when a landlord has so thoroughly violated the ordinance that removal is the only appropriate remedy.

## What NYC Is Doing and Why It Matters

Mayor Mamdani's bad landlord policy does not primarily function as a deterrent -- it functions as a conversion mechanism. The threat of receivership and ownership transfer is only as powerful as the credibility of the receiving entity. What Mamdani is building is the infrastructure to make tenant cooperatives a viable receiving entity: not just a threat that buildings can be taken, but an organizational and financing apparatus that gives tenants the capacity to steward what they receive.

The mechanism is New York's Article 7-A receivership process, which already exists but has historically been underused. The political innovation is the combination of aggressive enforcement with tenant cooperative preference as the transfer destination. The outcome of enforcement, in this model, is not a new private landlord -- it is permanent affordable housing governed by the people who live in it.

The financing piece is not resolved in the NYC announcement, but the logic points toward the same instruments that are already in the Frederick CLT Policy Brief: CDFI bridge lending, land trust structures, and mission-driven nonprofit intermediaries who can hold buildings through a transition period.

## The RUBS Connection

RUBS -- Ratio Utility Billing Systems -- belong in this analysis because they illustrate how the post-revocation gap compounds. A landlord operating RUBS is extracting revenue through an opaque billing mechanism that the Real Rent Ordinance may not currently prohibit. If that landlord also lets the building deteriorate toward license revocation, the tenants face a double injury: they have been paying inflated effective rents through RUBS, and now they face displacement. The enforcement pathway that the licensing ordinance offers does not unwind the RUBS extraction, does not compensate tenants for it, and does not produce any affordability outcome from the revocation.

The LA campaign's demand is a ban, not regulation. That framing is correct: regulation of RUBS tends to get captured by landlord lobbying; a ban removes the mechanism. For Frederick, the actionable ask is whether the Real Rent Ordinance's prohibited fees definition covers RUBS-style utility billing. If not, an amendment to explicitly include it is a clean, defensible addition.

## What a Post-Revocation Stewardship Framework Would Look Like

A complete tenant protection stack requires a fourth element that currently does not exist: a post-revocation stewardship mechanism. The basic shape:

When a rental license is revoked (whether for a single-family rental, a multi-unit building, or an SRO), the city invokes a structured transition process rather than leaving the building in limbo. The process has three stages.

First, a receiver is appointed -- either the city's housing department, a designated nonprofit intermediary, or a CDFI -- to manage the building during transition, collect rents, and maintain services. This protects tenants immediately and prevents building deterioration during the transition period.

Second, the receiver assesses the building's condition, the tenant population's preferences, and the feasibility of alternative ownership structures. Tenant cooperative formation, CLT transfer, or sale to a mission-driven affordable housing nonprofit are evaluated in that order of preference.

Third, the building is transferred to the preferred steward on terms that ensure long-term affordability. If the CLT is the receiving entity, the land enters the trust permanently. If a cooperative, tenants become member-owners. If a nonprofit, affordability covenants are attached to the deed.

The legal mechanism for this in Maryland requires research -- Maryland may or may not have receivership statutes comparable to New York's Article 7-A. That research is the first concrete next step.

## The CLT as the Receiving Institution

The Frederick CLT Policy Brief argues that a CLT requires significant upfront land acquisition capital but dramatically reduces ongoing subsidy requirements once land is in trust. The post-revocation framework inverts the capital logic usefully: revoked buildings are not acquired at market price. They are transferred from a landlord who has forfeited their operating right, often in buildings with deferred maintenance that has depressed market value. The CLT's land acquisition cost in this pathway may be substantially lower than open-market acquisition.

This creates a pipeline logic. Every license revocation, in a world where the post-revocation framework exists, is a potential CLT land acquisition. The enforcement system becomes a production mechanism. The worst landlords' failure becomes the permanently affordable stock's foundation.

That is a fundamentally different framing than anything currently in Frederick's housing legislation. It is worth naming explicitly and building toward.

## Open Questions

These are the specific research and drafting tasks that follow from this synthesis:

What receivership statutes exist in Maryland? Does Maryland Code give localities the authority to appoint receivers for habitability failures, and if so, under what conditions and through what court process?

Does the Real Rent Ordinance as currently drafted prohibit RUBS-style utility billing? If not, what specific language would close that gap?

Does the rental licensing ordinance rewrite include any provision for tenant protection during the post-revocation transition period? If not, can a 60-day transition receivership provision be added before introduction?

Who would serve as the receiver in Frederick today if the framework existed? The city does not currently have a designated housing receiver function. The Housing Fund program, HHS, or a contracted nonprofit intermediary are the logical candidates.

Can CLT transfer be made an explicit preference in both the rental licensing ordinance and the SRO ordinance as the preferred post-revocation outcome? Even a non-binding preference stated in findings or legislative purpose would signal the direction and create political cover for future implementation.
