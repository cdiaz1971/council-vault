# MPDU Reform -- Personal Strategy Brief

**For internal use only**

---

## The Context

The Moderately Priced Dwelling Unit program is Frederick's primary tool for ensuring that new residential development produces affordable units. It is established in City Code Chapter 19, substantially re-enacted by Ordinance G-23-20 in November 2023. The program has real legal authority and a 40-year affordability control period, which is stronger than most peer jurisdictions. The problem is not the program's structure. The problem is that two of its core parameters -- the payment-in-lieu rate and the activation threshold -- are set at levels that do not reflect current market conditions and do not capture the housing types the LMC amendments are designed to unlock.

Fixing those two parameters is what MPDU reform accomplishes. It moves in parallel with the LMC zoning amendments, not after them, because the two tracks tell a complete story together: we are removing barriers to production and we are ensuring that production serves the households that need it most.

---

## What the Program Currently Does

Any residential development of 25 or more units must set aside 12.5% of those units as MPDUs. Developers who increase that share to 15% receive a density bonus of up to 22%.

For rental MPDUs, affordability is structured in thirds: one third at 60% of Frederick County Area Median Income, one third at 70%, and one third at 80%. For-sale MPDUs are structured at 70%, 80%, and 90% AMI in thirds. The 40-year control period runs from original sale or rental, with covenants that run with the land and sit senior to permanent financing instruments.

Developers who cannot or choose not to produce on-site MPDUs may pay into the City Housing Fund in lieu of units. That rate is currently $2.00 per square foot of each dwelling unit in the development. The fund's authorized uses are broad -- it can finance LIHTC projects, provide emergency loans, fund homebuyer education, and support households below the MPDU income thresholds.

---

## Problem One: The Payment-in-Lieu Rate

The $2.00 per square foot rate was set in November 2023. The ordinance itself contemplates recalculation after three years of square footage and affordability gap data. That recalculation trigger exists in the law already. This is not jumping the gun -- it is using the mechanism the ordinance provides.

The rate is indefensible on the merits. Here is the math. A typical two-bedroom unit in a new Frederick development is approximately 1,000 square feet. At $2.00 per square foot, the payment-in-lieu contribution for that unit is $2,000 total. The actual affordability gap -- the difference between what a market-rate unit generates in rent and what an affordable unit at 80% AMI can generate -- is approximately $350 per month, or $4,200 per year. Capitalized over the 40-year control period, that gap represents a subsidy need of roughly $60,000 to $80,000 per unit depending on discount rate assumptions. The $2,000 payment-in-lieu covers approximately 3% of that need. It is not a serious affordability tool at that level.

Peer jurisdictions that have done this analysis are landing at payment-in-lieu rates in the $10-25 per square foot range. Montgomery County uses significantly higher rates. The right number for Frederick depends on a full affordability gap analysis using current market data, which the Housing and Human Services department can produce using the recalculation process the ordinance already mandates.

The introduced number is $12 per square foot. That is six times the current rate. It is defensible without the full affordability gap data in hand because it is grounded in the basic math above -- even at $12 per square foot a 1,000 square foot unit generates $12,000, which is still only about 15-20% of the actual subsidy need but is a meaningful contribution to a financing stack that includes LIHTC, HOME funds, and other sources. It is also a number that can be defended in public without a lengthy technical explanation.

The ordinance includes language requiring recalculation every three years tied to affordability gap data. That mechanism stays in place. If the data supports a higher rate at the first recalculation, it moves up.

**The developer pushback to expect:** They will argue $12 suppresses development by tipping marginal projects from viable to unviable. The honest response is that the current $2 rate means the development community is contributing almost nothing to the affordability problem their projects contribute to. A meaningful rate is not a penalty. It is a cost of doing business in a city with a documented housing crisis. The appropriate response to the feasibility argument is to request pro forma data from developers before introduction, not to set the rate based on their objections alone.

---

## Problem Two: The Activation Threshold

The current threshold is 25 units. Every development below 25 units is completely exempt from MPDU requirements. No on-site affordable units. No payment-in-lieu contribution. Nothing.

This creates a direct contradiction with the LMC zoning amendments. Track One is designed to unlock missing middle development -- duplexes, small multifamily, ADUs -- in the 2 to 20 unit range. If the MPDU threshold stays at 25, every unit produced by those amendments contributes nothing to the affordable housing pipeline. The supply grows but the affordable share of it does not.

The solution is a tiered threshold structure rather than a single cutoff number.

**The tiered structure:**

Projects of 15 units and above face the full MPDU requirement: 12.5% on-site affordable units with the option to pay in lieu at the new rate. This moves the threshold down from 25 to 15, capturing a meaningful additional category of development without hitting the smallest projects with the full compliance burden.

Projects of 7 to 14 units pay into the City Housing Fund at the new payment-in-lieu rate but are not required to produce on-site affordable units. A 10-unit building would not be required to designate one unit as an MPDU and manage it under the program's resale and rental restrictions. It pays the fee instead. The fee goes into the fund and finances affordable housing elsewhere. This captures something from missing middle development without imposing operational complexity on small developers who lack the infrastructure to manage MPDU compliance.

Projects below 7 units have no requirement. Duplexes, triplexes, and small ADU additions are exempt entirely. The transaction costs of administering MPDU requirements on a 3-unit building would consume most of the affordability benefit.

**Why 7 and 15 as the thresholds:** Seven units is the point at which a payment-in-lieu contribution becomes meaningful relative to administrative cost. A 7-unit building at $12 per square foot on 800 square foot average units generates approximately $67,000 in fund contributions -- enough to make a real difference in a financing stack. Fifteen units is the point at which on-site MPDU production is operationally manageable for a developer -- at 12.5%, a 15-unit building produces roughly two affordable units, which is a workable compliance number.

**The developer pushback to expect:** They will argue the tiered structure hits exactly the small local developers and homeowners that the LMC amendments are designed to activate. This is partially true. The tiered structure is designed to minimize that impact by keeping the smallest projects exempt and using payment-in-lieu rather than on-site units for the middle tier. But there is genuine tension here. The response is that the city cannot justify unlocking new production capacity without capturing some affordability benefit from that production. The tiered structure is the most developer-friendly way to do that while still producing meaningful results.

---

## What These Two Changes Accomplish Together

Raising the payment-in-lieu rate to $12 per square foot dramatically increases the capitalization of the City Housing Fund, which currently depends on a $2 rate and MPDU foreclosure proceeds -- both structurally modest. A better-capitalized fund can finance LIHTC projects, provide gap financing for affordable developments, and support households below the MPDU income thresholds in ways the current fund cannot.

Lowering the threshold to a tiered 7/15 structure ensures that the production unlocked by the LMC amendments generates some affordability benefit, even if that benefit flows through the fund rather than through on-site units at the smaller scale.

Together they do not solve the housing crisis. They are not designed to. They ensure that as Frederick grows its housing stock, a meaningful and growing share of that growth serves households that the market is currently failing.

---

## The Numbers to Remember

Current payment-in-lieu rate: $2.00 per square foot. Proposed: $12.00 per square foot.

Current MPDU threshold: 25 units. Proposed: full MPDU requirements at 15 units and above, payment-in-lieu only at 7 to 14 units, exempt below 7 units.

Current MPDU percentage: 12.5%. This stays unchanged in this ordinance. The density bonus structure for developers who voluntarily reach 15% also stays unchanged.

The recalculation mechanism already in the ordinance stays in place and is used to justify the rate increase as an exercise of existing law, not a new imposition.

---

## The Factual Foundation

Frederick's ALICE data shows 43% of city households below the threshold for basic household needs. Seventy-two percent of children in rental units live in ALICE or poverty households. The racial gap is documented: 35% of Black veterans and 30% of Hispanic veterans are ALICE households compared to 17% of white veterans. The City Housing Fund exists in law but is undercapitalized. The $2 per square foot rate generates too little to finance meaningful affordable housing production. The 25-unit threshold exempts every missing middle development from contributing anything. Both of those need to change.
