# Data Centers vs Affordable Housing -- Two Fronts on the Same Problem

## The Pattern

Three notes from this session converge on the same underlying dynamic, even though they appear to be separate issues:

1. The affordable housing finance model is under structural stress -- costs rising faster than rents and subsidies can follow ([[Affordable Housing Finance Under Structural Stress -- Insurance Rates and Operating Costs]]).
2. Data center development is directly displacing affordable housing investment in documented cases -- $240,000 in Maine, environmental resources in Utah ([[Data Center Displacing Affordable Housing Funds -- Maine Case Study]], [[Utah Stratos Data Center -- Community Resistance and Environmental Concerns]]).
3. CLT and co-op models offer an alternative finance and governance structure that partially sidesteps market dependence, but require deliberate policy support to scale ([[CLT and Co-op Housing Integration -- Lessons from Canada]]).

The connecting thread: public subsidy is scarce and actively contested. When data center tax incentives divert local revenue, that revenue is unavailable for housing. When data center infrastructure investment draws on capital budgets, those dollars cannot go to housing-related infrastructure. When data center development competes for water and power, those utilities face capacity and cost pressures that raise operating costs for everyone -- including affordable housing operators already squeezed by rising expenses.

## Why This Matters for Frederick

Frederick is actively developing a data center ordinance and community benefit agreement policy. The standard framing of that work is tax base and jobs. This synthesis suggests a second framing: the data center ordinance is also housing policy. Every dollar a data center developer captures through tax incentives is a dollar unavailable for the City Housing Fund. Every development approval that bypasses community benefit negotiation is a missed opportunity to attach housing contributions.

The Maine case makes the displacement mechanism concrete and citable. The Utah case shows what happens when approval processes move without community input -- and the organizing response communities are mounting. Both cases strengthen the argument for proactive CBA requirements in Frederick's ordinance rather than case-by-case negotiation after the fact.

## The CLT Connection

The CLT-co-op model from Canada is relevant here precisely because it operates partially outside the market finance stack. A CLT owns land permanently; co-op residents govern collectively; neither the land nor the units cycles back to market pricing. This structure is less exposed to the insurance, interest rate, and operating cost pressures that are squeezing conventional affordable housing operators. If Frederick were to establish a CLT and capitalize it with payment-in-lieu funds and data center CBA contributions, the result is a permanently affordable housing vehicle that is less dependent on the subsidy streams currently under pressure.

## The Synthesis Claim

Data centers and housing finance stress are not parallel problems -- they are competing claims on the same scarce public resources. Local policy that treats them as separate tracks (data center ordinance over here, MPDU reform over there) misses the connection. An integrated approach would link data center CBA requirements explicitly to housing fund capitalization, and frame the ordinance partly as a mechanism for ensuring that Frederick's infrastructure investments support housing as well as economic development.

## Open Questions

- What is the current revenue impact of existing data center or large-scale tech facility tax arrangements in Frederick or Frederick County?
- Does the proposed data center ordinance include explicit housing contributions as a CBA element?
- Is there appetite for a CLT proposal in the FY2028 or FY2029 budget cycle, capitalized by payment-in-lieu and CBA revenue?
