# Frederick City AMI - Analysis and Options

## The question

Frederick's affordable housing income limits come from HUD's Washington-Arlington-Alexandria metro area. That area includes the District, Arlington, Alexandria, and Montgomery County, where incomes are much higher than in Frederick. Programs that use federal or state money must use HUD's figure. Does Frederick gain anything by creating its own City-based measure for programs the City controls?

## The gap, in numbers

| Measure | Amount |
|---|---|
| HUD FY2026 median family income, Washington metro area (applies to Frederick) | **$166,100** |
| City of Frederick median family income (ACS 2020 to 2024, 2024 dollars) | **$112,741** (±$5,324) |
| City median household income | $97,069 |
| City median gross rent | $1,764 |

Frederick's median family income is about **68 percent** of the HUD figure. The ACS number is a five-year average in 2024 dollars, while HUD trends its figure forward to 2026. Adjusting for that would narrow the gap only a few points.

## What "affordable" means under each

Maximum rents in programs like the Low Income Housing Tax Credit (LIHTC) are set at 30 percent of the income limit. A two-bedroom unit is priced for a household of three. The table below compares the regional figure with a City figure built HUD's way, adjusting for household size with HUD's standard factor of 0.9 for three people.

| Two-bedroom, three-person household | Regional HUD AMI | City-based measure |
|---|---|---|
| 60% income limit | $89,700 | about $60,880 |
| Maximum "affordable" monthly rent at 60% | **about $2,243** | **about $1,522** |
| 50% income limit | $74,750 | about $50,730 |
| Maximum rent at 50% | about $1,869 | about $1,268 |

**This is the core finding.** Under the regional figure, a two-bedroom apartment labeled affordable at 60 percent of AMI can legally rent for about **$2,243 a month**. That is about **$480 more than the median rent** of every rental in the City ($1,764). Even the 50 percent level allows a rent above the City median.

Under a Frederick-based measure, the same 60 percent unit tops out around $1,522, below the City median rent. Many units that the City counts as "affordable" today are priced above what typical Frederick renters pay on the open market.

The 80 percent "moderate income" level shows the same problem. Uncapped, it is $132,900 for a family of four under the regional figure, well above Frederick's median family income. HUD caps its official 80 percent limit in high-income areas, so check which version a given program uses. The City-based 80 percent level for four would be about $90,190.

*These are calculations from the published figures, not official limits. Rent limits include utilities through a utility allowance.*

## What the City controls and what it doesn't

**Must use HUD's figure:**
- LIHTC projects and anything financed through Maryland DHCD
- HOME and CDBG funds
- Housing Choice Vouchers and other HACF programs
- Any federal or state grant with its own income rules
- Frederick County programs, which the County controls and which use HUD's Washington figure

**Could use a City measure, because the City sets the rules:**
- **City Housing Fund grants and loans** paid with local money, including payment-in-lieu revenue.
- **The City's MPDU program.** Rental MPDUs are set in thirds at 60, 70, and 80 percent of "Frederick County area median income." In practice that is HUD's Washington figure. Confirm how HHS calculates it.
- **Tenant protection package triggers.** Rockville uses "households under 50 percent of AMI" as a trigger for permanent relocation help when redevelopment pushes tenants out.
- **City-funded assistance programs** such as water shutoff prevention, homebuyer help, and emergency rental help paid with local money.
- **Local tax credits or fee waivers** tied to affordability, if the City creates them.
- **Reporting.** How the City measures and reports affordability, including the annual reporting under Resolution 26-15.

## What other places have done

**Philadelphia.** The regional HUD figure for 2024 was $114,400 for a family of four. Philadelphia's own was about $87,000. A Reinvestment Fund brief written with the Council President's office estimated a city-only figure using HUD's method. Under it, 60 percent rents would be $300 to $490 a month lower than under the regional figure.

The brief found the regional figure hurts Black and Hispanic households most. Their median family incomes ($52,603 and $44,161) are far below the white median ($108,698).

Councilmember Jamie Gauthier called for a hearing, which was expected in November 2024. Pennsylvania law (HB 581) gives local governments room to define AMI locally for certain tax exemptions. Opposition came from two directions:

- The Building Industry Association said workforce housing deals wouldn't work without more subsidy.
- The Philadelphia Housing Authority said it must follow federal rules regardless.

*No adopted ordinance is confirmed.*

**New York City.** A 2022 report by the Association for Neighborhood and Housing Development (ANHD) found HUD's figure for a three-person household was 28 percent above New York's actual median household income. In 28 of 59 community districts, a typical household couldn't afford a 60 percent unit. ANHD's recommendation was not a new local figure. It was to use the City's own subsidy dollars to reach lower incomes than federal programs require.

**Detroit.** HUD's regional figure for 2024 was $95,900 for a family of four, while Detroit's median household income was about $36,000. "Affordable" rents at 80 and 50 percent remain well above what most Detroiters can pay. No local measure has been adopted.

**The pattern.** Every city that studied this found the same gap Frederick has. None is confirmed to have formally replaced HUD's figure. What cities can actually do is set **deeper targets for their own money**, measured against local incomes.

## Is there an advantage?

**Yes, in three ways.**

1. **It tells the truth about who is served.** The City can report affordability against what Frederick households actually earn. That keeps "affordable" from meaning rents above the citywide median.
2. **It aims City dollars at Frederick residents.** When the City spends its own money, a City-based target ensures that money reaches households the federal programs miss, especially in District 2, where poverty runs 1.5 times the county rate.
3. **It strengthens every housing argument you make.** "Affordable for whom?" becomes a measurable question in each project review, rezoning, and budget debate.

**The costs.**

1. **A deeper target needs more subsidy per unit.** Lowering a two-bedroom's maximum rent by about $720 a month means about $8,600 a year more subsidy per unit, or fewer units for the same money. A lower measure doesn't create money. It changes who the money reaches.
2. **Developers and the building industry will object** if a City measure is applied to MPDUs or inclusionary requirements, as Philadelphia's builders did. Applied to MPDUs, it could push more developers toward payment-in-lieu. That makes MPDU payment-in-lieu reform a prerequisite.
3. **Two income standards are more complicated to administer.** Projects that mix City money with LIHTC would have to meet both, and compliance monitoring gets harder.
4. **Some households at the top of the current range lose eligibility.** These are households between the City and regional 80 percent levels. Opponents will call them the "workforce."
5. **Data limits.** ACS figures carry margins of error and lag by a few years. The City would need a fixed yearly update method.

## Options

**Option 1. A reporting benchmark only.** Lowest risk. The City publishes a yearly Frederick City median income and the matching income and rent levels next to HUD's figures. City affordability reports, including those under Resolution 26-15, show both. No program rules change. The benchmark gives every later decision a local reference point and costs almost nothing.

**Option 2. Deeper targets for City money.** City Housing Fund money and other locally funded programs target households by the City measure. For example, a share of City subsidy goes to units affordable at or below 60 percent of Frederick City median income. Projects using federal or state money still certify under HUD. This is what New York's advocates recommend, and it is the clearest benefit.

**Option 3. A "lower of the two" rule for City programs.** City programs use whichever is lower, HUD's figure or the City's. Same effect as Option 2, simpler to explain.

**Option 4. Apply the City measure to MPDUs.** The most impactful option and the most contested. Lowers MPDU rents but raises the cost to developers. Do this only after payment-in-lieu reform, and only with a feasibility analysis.

**A reasonable sequence:** Option 1 now, Option 2 or 3 for the City Housing Fund as it grows, and Option 4 only after MPDU payment-in-lieu reform and a feasibility study.

**Connection to the tenant package.** Any income-based trigger in the renter protection package, such as relocation for lower-income households pushed out by redevelopment, can be defined against the City measure. That would be one of its first concrete uses.

## How a City measure would be calculated

1. **Base.** Start with the latest ACS five-year median family income for the City of Frederick (table B19113).
2. **Update.** Trend it forward to the current year the way HUD does, or use the CPI.
3. **Household size.** Set the four-person figure equal to the trended median. Adjust for other sizes with HUD's standard factors: 70 percent for one person, 80 for two, 90 for three, 108 for five.
4. **Publish.** Release 30, 50, 60, 80, and 100 percent levels each year on a fixed date, alongside HUD's.
5. **Responsibility.** Name the department responsible, likely HHS, and state the method in the ordinance or resolution so it can't drift.

## Open questions

- How does HHS currently calculate "Frederick County area median income" for MPDUs?
- Which City-funded programs currently use HUD limits by habit rather than by legal requirement?
- What does Frederick's income gap look like by race and ethnicity? ACS table B19113 has breakouts by race, and Philadelphia's analysis found the biggest effect there.
- Would the County join a shared county-level local measure, or does it stay with HUD?
