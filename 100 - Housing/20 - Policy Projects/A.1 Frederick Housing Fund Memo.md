SUBJECT: Establishing a Bond-Funded Housing Production Revolving Loan Fund — A Proposal to
Capitalize the Frederick Housing Accelerator Fund (HAF)
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
This memo explores the authorization of a $15 million General Obligation bond, explored for FY2028, to
capitalize the Frederick Housing Accelerator Fund (HAF) — a revolving public lender for mixed-income rental
housing. The Fund is modeled on two proven programs: Montgomery County's Housing Opportunities
Commission and Chattanooga's Invest Chattanooga, both of which make subordinate loans covering 15–30%
of project cost, repaid and recycled into new developments.
The HAF is a capital tool, not a subsidy. Unlike a grant or fee waiver — spent once and gone — every dollar
that leaves the Fund as a construction loan returns as repayment and is re-lent, indefinitely. Modeled
conservatively, a single bond can finance several times its face value in development over its 20-year term.
The fiscal commitment is modest and well-secured: an estimated $1.0–1.2 million in annual debt service
over 20 years, under 0.7% of the City's FY2027 general fund, backed by a AAA/Aa1-caliber borrower. In
exchange, the Fund closes financing gaps that stall mixed-income development and lowers the cost of doing
so enough that affordability can be set well above the bare minimum needed to make a project work — and,
for some projects, is the difference between the deal happening at all.
Recommendation: a three-step path forward — (1) authorize a feasibility study and enabling ordinance in
FY2027; (2) issue the bond and open the Fund for lending by late 2027; and (3) evaluate performance and
plan a larger second tranche within 5–7 years, ideally co-capitalized with Frederick County and philanthropic
partners.
BACKGROUND
Frederick County must add 31,853 new housing units by 2035 to keep pace with growth, and the City of
Frederick — the County's primary jobs and population center — alone accounts for roughly 14,000 of that
need. More than 10,000 of the County's required units must be reserved for households earning below 60%
of Area Median Income (AMI). Current production is running at less than half the required pace, and County
planners warn the development pipeline could dry up by 2032 absent new tools.
Frederick anchors the region's economy — Fort Detrick, Frederick Health, and Frederick County Public
Schools are among its largest employers — and downtown revitalization alone is now drawing more than
$150 million in private investment. That growth needs a workforce housed nearby to support it. Yet the City
currently has no dedicated capital program to help close financing gaps on new mixed-income rental
housing.

Existing tools do not fill this gap. Federal Low-Income Housing Tax Credits (LIHTC) are increasingly
competitive and are designed for rents based on the DC metro area, often nearly market rate in Frederick,
and not at the affordability levels and within mixed-income housing Frederick most needs to add at scale. In
fact, Frederick's shortfall mirrors a pattern documented nationally. An Associated Press analysis published
just last week, found that only about 4 million affordable rental units exist nationwide for 11 million
extremely-low-income renter households (those earning below 30% of AMI) and that roughly three-quarters
of those households pay more than half their income toward rent and utilities. The same reporting found
that just 12% of new LIHTC units nationally were affordable at 30% AMI in 2024, with most tax-credit
production instead targeting 50% AMI and above. This reinforces the case that Frederick's own tools should
not rely on LIHTC alone, and that a locally controlled fund has room to target the deepest tier of need.
Separately, as of August 2026, advocates are seeking that the State of Maryland create a $250 million
statewide revolving housing fund, explicitly modeled on Montgomery County's program. A City-level
program would position Frederick to draw down state matching capital later, rather than starting from
scratch after the state program is already established.
ANALYSIS OF THE ISSUES
The Core Problem Is the Cost of Capital, Not Demand
A market-rate unit supports more debt because its rent is unrestricted. An affordable unit, by definition,
cannot — its below-market rent generates less revenue to service debt even though it costs the same to
build.Already in fully market rate projects, developers close financing gaps with private mezzanine debt or
preferred equity often priced at 12–20% annually. This capital is so expensive that it caps how many
affordable units, if any, a project can absorb. Public capital, placed in the same subordinate position at
roughly 5–6%, closes the same gap far more cheaply, leaving more project income available to support
additional affordability.
How a Revolving Loan Fund (RLF) Works
A revolving loan fund lends public capital to developers. Bond proceeds capitalize the Fund; the Fund makes
a subordinate loan (typically 15–30% of project cost, with senior construction debt and private equity
financing the rest); the project stabilizes and refinances within 3–6 years; and principal plus interest is repaid
and re-lent to the next project. At a 5-year construction-to-refinance cycle, the same capital can turn over
roughly 3–4 times across a single bond's 20-year term.
The Fund also outlives the bond. While the bond is active (years 1–20), debt service is paid from the general
fund and capital revolves every 4–6 years. Once the bond is retired, there is no more debt service to pay: full
loan repayments plus interest income stay in the Fund, compounding with no offsetting cost. The Fund's
lending capacity continues to grow rather than winding down.
Illustrative Example: A $60 Million, 100-Unit Project
To make the mechanism concrete, consider a mid-size Frederick development: $60 million in total cost, 100
new rental units, financed with $42 million (70%) in senior construction debt, $12 million (20%) in a
subordinate loan, and $6 million (10%) in developer equity.

The Fund's value shows up in how that $12 million subordinate slice is priced. Financed with private
mezzanine equity at a 20% required return, it costs the project about $2.4 million per year. Financed instead
by the HAF at 6%, it costs about $720,000 per year — a saving of roughly $1.68 million every year, about 70%
less than the conventional cost. Real private mezzanine equity is typically priced as a blend of cash-pay and
deferred (PIK) interest rather than a flat annual rate. $1.68M/year is used throughout this analysis as a
conservative, blended-average estimate of the savings and not a promise of a fixed annual cash payment.

That $1.68 million annual savings pool comfortably covers a meaningful affordability trade. Setting aside 40%
of the units, 20 at ≤30% AMI and 20 at ≤50% AMI, gives up roughly $276,000 per year in rent relative to
market, based on current Frederick-area rents and Maryland DHCD LIHTC rent-limit tables. The savings pool
covers that shortfall about 6.1 times over, leaving an estimated $1.4 million per year in residual savings
available to the developer. In practice, this converts 40 of the project's 100 units, which would otherwise all
rent at market rate, into permanently affordable homes, without relying on federal tax credits. It is important
to note that this test asks only whether the capital-cost savings cover the rent given up. It is not a full
development pro forma meaning that it does not model senior debt sizing, operating expenses, vacancy, or
the developer's return on equity.
Beyond deepening affordability at the margin, some of this savings can matter even more directly: for a
project that does not currently pencil at market rate, the Fund's cheaper capital can be the difference
between the deal moving forward at all. Framed this way, public capital does three jobs at once: it closes the
financing gap that would otherwise stall a project entirely; it lowers the cost of closing that gap enough that
affordability can be set above the bare minimum needed to make the deal work; and, for some projects, it
can make a stalled deal possible at all, adding to Frederick's overall housing supply, not only its
affordable-housing slice.
REVIEW OF OPTIONS
Option 1 — Status Quo: Rely on LIHTC and Existing State/Federal Tools

• Pros: Same City financial commitments or debt; uses existing city gap financing and state and federal
administrative infrastructure.
• Cons: LIHTC allocations are increasingly competitive and structurally designed for DC metro-area rents,
not the more deeply affordable and mixed-income product Frederick needs at scale. Production would
likely continue at its current sub-50%-of-need pace, and the pipeline risk identified by County planners
(drying up by 2032) would go unaddressed.
• Feasibility: Relatively high cost per unit, but does not close the identified gap, effectively one-time
expenditures due to long payback periods.
Option 2 — One-Time Capital Grant or Fee-Waiver Program
• Pros: Simple to administer; immediate, visible impact on the projects it funds.
• Cons: Every dollar is spent once and is gone — there is no repayment or recycling, so the program's
impact is capped permanently at the size of its initial appropriation. Future affordable housing
production would require new appropriations each cycle, competing annually with other City priorities.
• Feasibility: Politically simple to launch, but fiscally the least durable and lowest-leverage option over a
20-year horizon.
Option 3 — Bond-Funded Revolving Loan Fund: The Housing Accelerator Fund (Recommended)
• Pros: Capital is repaid and re-lent indefinitely, producing several times the bond's face value in
development activity over its term; a modest, well-secured annual debt-service cost (well under 1% of
the general fund) is spread over 20 years; the Fund continues growing after the bond retires; and the
structure directly matches the two peer models with demonstrated results (below).
• Cons: Requires a new bond authorization and the upfront work of establishing an independent lending
entity and underwriting guidelines; benefits accrue over years, not immediately; carries the general (but
well-precedented and well-secured) risks of any bond-funded program.
• Feasibility: Demonstrated at comparable or larger scale in three peer jurisdictions; a phased rollout
(feasibility study followed by an initial tranche before evaluation and expansion) limits early exposure.

Governance and accountability:
• Structure the Fund as an independent nonprofit affiliate of HACF, governed by its own board, with
lending guidelines covering eligible projects, terms, affordability requirements, and risk standards.
• Require audited financials and open board meetings, plus regular reports to the City Council.
• Track annual performance publicly: units produced, affordability levels achieved, private dollars
leveraged, repayment performance, and defaults.
• Limit early exposure through the phased rollout itself — a feasibility study precedes capitalization, and
formal evaluation precedes any second tranche.
COUNTERARGUMENTS
Objection: This is just a subsidy to private developers.
Response: It is a loan, not a grant. Unlike a fee waiver or grant — spent once and gone — every HAF dollar is
repaid with interest and re-lent to the next project, in exchange for a binding affordability commitment the
City would not otherwise get.
Objection: Why not simply expand our use of LIHTC instead?

Response: LIHTC allocations are increasingly competitive and are structurally designed to use DC MSA
income limits which are very close to market rate, not the moderate-affordability, mixed-income housing
Frederick most needs. A locally controlled revolving fund fills precisely that gap and can move on the City's
own timeline rather than a competitive state cycle.
Objection: Issuing $15M in bonds puts taxpayers at risk.
Response: The commitment is modest and well-secured: an estimated $1.0–1.2M in annual debt service,
under 0.7% of the FY2027 general fund, backed by a AAA/Aa1-caliber borrower. The phased rollout —
feasibility study, then a first tranche, then a formal evaluation before any second tranche — further limits
exposure before the City scales its commitment. Additionally, the repaid 6% interest will produce
approximately $900,000 per year, on the full $15M, reducing the impact on the general fund to between
$100,000 and $300,000 per year.
Objection: The savings figures in the illustrative example seem too favorable.
Response: The analysis is deliberately conservative. The $1.68M/year savings figure uses a conservative,
blended-average estimate of mezzanine pricing rather than the higher figure a full
cash-plus-deferred-interest structure could imply, and the affordability coverage test explicitly is not a full
development pro forma — it does not model senior debt sizing, operating expenses, vacancy, or developer
return on equity. The claim that some deals may not otherwise pencil is presented qualitatively.
Objection: Why should Frederick act now rather than wait for the state program?
Response: Maryland may be considering a $250 million statewide revolving fund modeled explicitly on
Montgomery County's program. A City-level program positions Frederick to draw down state matching
capital when that program launches, rather than starting the design and legal-structuring process from
scratch afterward.
Objection: If deep affordability is the real need, why does the illustrative example split the set-aside between
30% and 50% AMI instead of targeting 30% AMI exclusively?
Response: It doesn't have to. The 20/20 split is a balanced illustration, not a ceiling, the same coverage math
shows that shifting the full 40-unit set-aside to 30% AMI stays within the Fund's savings pool (about 3.9x
coverage, versus 6.1x for the balanced split). National reporting suggests the 60–80% AMI tier LIHTC
production concentrates on is already softening in several metros, while the ≤30% AMI tier is the most
underserved, so this is presented as a genuine design choice, weighed against the smaller residual it leaves
for the developer, rather than a fixed feature of the proposal. However, it is important to note that this is just
an illustrative example and that every deal is different. Providing flexibility to the lending and underwriting
organization would provide the best ability to maximize the benefits for Frederick residents.
CONCLUSION
Frederick faces a housing shortfall that current tools are not built to close, at a moment when the City's own
economic growth depends on housing the workforce behind it. The Housing Accelerator Fund applies a
single, modest, well-secured bond authorization to a proven mechanism that is repaid, recycled, and grows
for decades, rather than spent once. The Committee is asked to support:
1. Authorizing the feasibility study and enabling ordinance in FY2027;

2. Issuing the bond and opening the Fund for lending by late 2027; and
3. Planning a second, larger tranche within 5–7 years, contingent on performance.
A single bond authorization, deployed once and reused indefinitely, is recurring leverage for decades and is a
meaningful, durable step toward closing Frederick's housing gap.
