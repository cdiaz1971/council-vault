# LMC Zoning Amendments -- Personal Strategy Brief

**For internal use only**

---

## The Context

Frederick has an 8,133-unit housing shortage. The state has assigned us a target of 5,194 units by 2030. Median rent has risen roughly 75% since 2019 -- from approximately $1,425 to over $2,400 across all unit types. Forty-one percent of Frederick households are renters. Forty-five percent of city households fall below the ALICE threshold, meaning they cannot meet basic household needs. The market is not producing enough housing, and what it is producing is not affordable to the people who need it most.

The reason is not primarily developer appetite or financing. It is the rulebook. Frederick's Land Management Code makes it illegal or economically unviable to build the smaller, denser, cheaper housing types that would naturally increase supply and moderate price pressure. These four amendments fix the most consequential of those restrictions.

---

## Amendment One: By-Right Missing Middle in R4 and R6

**What the code currently says:** R4 and R6 are Frederick's lowest-density residential zones. In both zones, only single-family detached dwellings are permitted by right. Duplexes are not permitted. Townhouses are not permitted. Multifamily is not permitted. The minimum lot size in R4 is 8,000 square feet. In R6 it is 7,000 square feet.

Every other residential zone in Frederick -- R8 through R20 and the downtown zones -- already permits duplexes and townhouses by right. R4 and R6 are the holdouts, and they represent a significant share of Frederick's residential land area.

**Why this matters:** A duplex on a lot that currently holds one single-family home doubles the number of households that land can serve without requiring new infrastructure, new roads, or new utilities. A townhouse development on an R4 lot can produce three to five units where one exists today. These are not large apartment buildings. They are the kind of gentle density that fits into existing neighborhoods without dramatically changing their character.

The amendment permits duplexes and townhouses by right in R4 and R6 using lot size standards consistent with what R8 already requires -- 4,000 square feet for duplexes, 1,400 square feet per townhouse unit. It does not mandate anything. A homeowner who wants to keep a single-family home keeps it. The amendment simply allows more options on the same land.

**The political framing that matters:** This is not about changing neighborhoods. It is about giving property owners more choices for what they can do with land they already own. No state mandate is coming to do this for us. The Maryland Starter and Silver Homes Act, which would have required townhouse permissions in these zones, failed in the 2026 session. Opening R4 and R6 is Frederick's decision to make under its own zoning authority.

---

## Amendment Two: Parking Minimum Reform

**What the code currently says:** LMC Section 607, Table 607-1 sets the following residential parking minimums outside the downtown overlay. Single-family requires two spaces per unit with no maximum. Duplexes require 1.5 spaces per unit with a maximum of 2.5. Townhouses require two spaces per unit with a maximum of 3.0. Multifamily requires 1.5 spaces per unit with a maximum of 2.5. These minimums apply citywide except in the Carroll Creek Overlay and the downtown DB district.

**Why this matters:** Every required parking space adds cost and consumes land. A surface parking space costs roughly $5,000-10,000 to construct. A structured parking space costs $25,000-50,000. Those costs are passed directly to residents through higher rents and purchase prices. Beyond cost, parking minimums consume land that could be housing. A lot sized for a duplex with four required parking spaces may not have enough buildable area left for the housing itself once setbacks and parking are accounted for.

This is especially consequential for missing middle housing. A small multifamily building on an infill lot may be financially viable as housing but not viable once parking requirements are factored in. The parking requirement is often the deciding factor between a project penciling out and not.

The amendment reduces or eliminates parking minimums for missing middle and multifamily residential development outside downtown, consistent with what the downtown overlay already allows. Single-family parking requirements are left unchanged. The amendment does not prohibit parking -- it removes the mandate. Developers and homeowners who want parking can build it. Those for whom it is not financially viable are not forced to build it anyway.

## Amendment Three: ADU Owner-Occupancy Elimination

**What the code currently says:** ADU regulations are in LMC Section 802, last substantially revised in 2021. ADUs are permitted by right in R4, R8, R12, R16, R20, DBO, and DR districts on lots with a single-family detached dwelling, duplex, or townhouse. However, the current code requires that the property owner live in either the primary residence or the ADU. Only one ADU per lot is permitted. Detached ADUs are capped at 800 square feet and must be in the rear yard on a foundation. In planned developments -- TNDs, PNDs, and MU zones -- no more than 10% of single-family lots may include ADUs.

**Why this matters:** The owner-occupancy requirement is the primary suppressor of ADU production in Frederick. It means that a homeowner who wants to build an ADU as a rental investment but does not live on site cannot do so legally. It also means that properties held by small landlords -- someone who owns two or three houses and rents all of them -- cannot add ADUs to any of their properties.

ADUs are one of the fastest and cheapest ways to add rental units to an existing neighborhood. They use existing infrastructure, they fit within existing building envelopes, and they produce small affordable units that are often cheaper to rent than larger apartments. The 2021 ADU code was an improvement over what preceded it, but the owner-occupancy requirement significantly limits the production potential.

Cities that have eliminated owner-occupancy requirements have seen meaningful increases in ADU permit applications. California eliminated its statewide owner-occupancy requirement in 2020 and ADU production increased dramatically in the following years. Oregon, Washington, and several other states have followed.

The amendment eliminates the owner-occupancy requirement. Every other ADU restriction -- the 800 square foot cap, the rear yard requirement, the foundation requirement, the 90-day minimum lease term -- remains in place. This is a targeted change to the single provision that most suppresses production.

---

## Amendment Four: ADU Cap Removal in Planned Developments

**What the code currently says:** In TNDs, PNDs, and MU zones, no more than 10% of single-family lots in a development may include ADUs. This is a hard cap written into the code with no sunset or review mechanism.

**Why this matters:** Frederick has significant residential land area in planned developments -- Traditional Neighborhood Developments and Planned Neighborhood Developments built in the 1990s and 2000s as large-scale master-planned communities. The 10% cap means that in a 200-lot TND, only 20 lots can have ADUs regardless of how many homeowners want to build them or how much rental demand exists.

There is no documented policy justification for the 10% figure. It appears to have been set as a precautionary limit when ADUs were a relatively new concept. The housing landscape has changed substantially since then and the cap now functions as an arbitrary brake on gentle densification in neighborhoods that already have infrastructure in place to support additional units.

The amendment removes the 10% cap entirely, allowing ADUs in planned developments on the same terms as any other residential zone. Homeowners in TNDs and PNDs who want to build ADUs can do so subject to all other ADU restrictions, which remain unchanged.

---

## The Package Argument

These four amendments are not four separate ideas. They are one coherent response to the same problem: Frederick's code prevents the production of smaller, cheaper, more diverse housing types on land that already exists within the city. The zoning amendments open R4 and R6 to missing middle. The parking reform makes missing middle development financially viable. The ADU amendments unlock the largest untapped source of gentle densification -- the backyard, the garage, the basement -- across the entire city.

None of these changes mandate anything. None of them require city spending. None of them tell a property owner what to do with their land. They expand options. The market does the rest.

The factual foundation is the 8,133-unit shortage, the 75% rent increase since 2019, the 43% of City households below the ALICE threshold, and the state's 5,194-unit production target by 2030. Frederick cannot meet any of those benchmarks without changing the rules that govern what can be built.
