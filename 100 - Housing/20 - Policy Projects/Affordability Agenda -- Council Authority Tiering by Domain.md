# Affordability Agenda -- Council Authority Tiering by Domain

## Purpose

This note maps the six ALICE Household Survival Budget categories against the City of Frederick's actual legislative and administrative authority. The goal is to distinguish where the council can act directly and produce measurable results, where the city can facilitate or partner to reduce burden, and where the city's role is limited to advocacy or mitigation. This framing prevents the common failure mode of building a broad affordability narrative without a clear theory of change at each domain.

The six ALICE domains are: housing, childcare, food, transportation, healthcare, and technology. A seventh category -- financial stability and asset building -- is not a standalone ALICE budget line but cuts across all six and has a distinct set of city-level tools worth naming separately.

---

## Tier Definitions

**Tier 1 -- Direct City Action:** The council has clear legislative or administrative authority to act. Ordinance, resolution, budget allocation, zoning amendment, or administrative policy can produce a direct reduction in cost burden or improvement in access without requiring state or federal action. Preemption risk must be assessed for each specific instrument but the general domain is within city authority.

**Tier 2 -- City as Facilitator or Partner:** The city cannot set rates, fund programs at scale, or mandate outcomes, but can materially shape conditions through land use decisions, city-owned assets, procurement, city-funded nonprofit contracts, or coordination with county, state, or nonprofit partners. Impact is real but indirect and dependent on partner capacity.

**Tier 3 -- Advocacy and Mitigation Only:** The primary levers are at the state or federal level. The city can pass resolutions, send letters, testify, and build coalitions, but cannot independently move the needle. Local mitigation (reducing the local cost of a state or federal failure) is sometimes possible at Tier 2 but the structural fix requires action above the city level.

## Domain Tiering

---

### Housing

**Primary tier: Tier 1**

Housing is the largest single ALICE budget line and the domain where the city has the most direct authority. Frederick has significant gaps relative to peer cities -- no tenant protections, no rental registry, no local CLT relationship, MPDU baseline at 12.5%, payment-in-lieu rate at $2.00/sq ft -- all of which are within council authority to address subject to preemption review.

Tier 1 instruments available or in progress:
- Rental junk fee prohibition (Real Rent Ordinance, pending city attorney preemption review)
- MPDU rate increase (currently 12.5%; 15% target established in housing production resolution)
- Payment-in-lieu rate modernization (currently $2.00/sq ft; well below peer benchmarks)
- Housing production targets (resolution passed; follow-on zoning and funding work pending)
- Zoning reform to permit missing middle and multi-family in R4/R6 zones
- Parkland fee-in-lieu rate modernization (flagged as structurally low)
- Land donation to HACF and other affordable housing vehicles (Lucas Village precedent set)

Tier 2 instruments:
- Partnership with HACF on LIHTC redevelopment
- CLT relationship (no current city CLT relationship; candidate for development)
- City-owned land inventory for affordable housing siting

Tier 3:
- Housing Choice Voucher funding levels (federal)
- State rental assistance programs

**Watch:** Maryland preemption is the threshold question for every new tenant protection instrument. Rachel Gray's review gates introduction.

---

### Childcare

**Primary tier: Tier 2, with Tier 1 edge cases**

Childcare is the largest ALICE budget line for families with young children ($2,500/month in Frederick County, ahead of housing at $2,250, per the 2026 report) and the domain with the largest gap between the scale of the problem and the city's direct authority. The city does not set childcare rates, license providers, or administer subsidy programs. Those levers are at the state level (Maryland State Department of Education Division of Early Childhood) and county level (Frederick County).

Tier 1 instruments available:
- Zoning: childcare facility siting, home-based provider permit rules, accessory use allowances in commercial and mixed-use zones. The city's zoning code directly controls where providers can locate. Barriers in the zoning code that limit provider supply are within the council's authority to remove.
- City employee benefits: the city as employer can examine its own childcare benefit structure. Limited impact but visible as a signal.
- Fee waivers: city permit and licensing fees for new childcare facility openings could be waived or reduced as an economic development tool.

Tier 2 instruments:
- County partnership: Frederick County administers the state childcare subsidy program and child care resource and referral network. The city can coordinate, co-fund, and advocate within that relationship.
- City-owned or city-adjacent facility space: if the city has underutilized community facilities, partnership with a nonprofit provider for subsidized childcare siting is within reach.
- Employer roundtable: convening major Frederick employers to examine childcare benefit gaps is a facilitation role the council president can play without legislation.

Tier 3:
- State childcare subsidy rates and provider reimbursement (Maryland EXCELS, Child Care Scholarship Program)
- Federal Child Care and Development Block Grant
- State pre-K expansion funding

**Watch:** This is an area where peer city models are worth researching. Several cities have used zoning reform, city facility partnerships, and employer coalitions to meaningfully increase childcare supply without controlling rates. That research note does not yet exist in the vault.

### Food

**Primary tier: Tier 2, with Tier 3 structural drivers**

Food is the third largest ALICE budget line ($514/month single adult; $1,394/month family of four at state average). The structural drivers of food insecurity -- SNAP eligibility thresholds, federal nutrition program funding -- are federal. But access barriers and local food system gaps are addressable through city land use and partnership tools.

Tier 1 instruments available:
- Commercial zoning: the city's zoning code controls where grocery stores, farmers markets, food cooperatives, and community gardens can locate. Food desert dynamics in parts of the city are partially a zoning and land use problem.
- Permit and licensing: food truck permitting, community garden allowances, urban agriculture zoning all affect local food access.
- City-owned land: the city can donate or lease land for community gardens, food pantry siting, or food cooperative development.

Tier 2 instruments:
- Partnership with Frederick Community Action Agency, Heartly House, and other food-serving nonprofits through city budget allocations
- Coordination with Frederick County on food bank access and SNAP outreach
- Farmers market fee waivers or city-subsidized market infrastructure in underserved areas
- SNAP/WIC acceptance expansion at farmers markets (state-level program but city can facilitate through market operators)

Tier 3:
- SNAP eligibility thresholds and benefit levels (federal)
- WIC program funding (federal)
- USDA community food programs

**Watch:** Frederick-specific food access gap data is not well documented. Before building a food access legislative agenda, a local landscape note is needed. The question of whether the city has food desert census tracts with documented access gaps is researchable through USDA Food Access Research Atlas data.

### Transportation

**Primary tier: Tier 2, with Tier 1 land use tools**

Transportation is the fourth largest ALICE budget line ($414/month single adult; $910/month family). The primary cost driver for most households is car ownership. For car-free households the burden is concentrated in ride-share dependency and limited transit coverage. The city does not operate a transit system (TransIT is a county program) but has significant land use authority that directly affects transportation cost burden over time.

Tier 1 instruments available:
- Parking policy: minimum parking requirements increase housing and commercial development costs and entrench car dependency. Reducing or eliminating minimums in transit-adjacent areas is within city zoning authority.
- Land use and density: zoning decisions that permit higher density near transit stops reduce the transportation burden on residents who can walk or use transit.
- Street design: the city controls right-of-way design, which affects pedestrian and bicycle access as alternatives to car trips.
- City employee transportation benefits: transit pass subsidies or bike commuter benefits for city employees are within administrative authority.

Tier 2 instruments:
- TransIT coordination: the city can partner with Frederick County on transit route planning, stop placement, and service frequency advocacy for city residents.
- United Way Ride United Network coordination: the city can facilitate connections between residents and RUN for healthcare and employment transportation.
- Employer coordination: advocating for employer transit benefits or vanpool programs is a facilitation role.

Tier 3:
- TransIT funding levels (county budget)
- Maryland Transit Administration programs
- Federal transit funding

**Watch:** The connection between land use decisions and long-term transportation cost burden is underrepresented in the current legislative agenda. Every zoning decision that permits car-dependent development increases the transportation burden on ALICE households for decades. This should be a named criterion in LMC reform deliberations.

### Healthcare

**Primary tier: Tier 3, with Tier 2 access mitigation**

Healthcare is the fifth ALICE budget line ($218/month single adult; $850/month family; $580/month adults 65+). The cost drivers -- insurance premiums, out-of-pocket costs, Medicare structure -- are almost entirely federal and state. The city has no authority over insurance rates, provider reimbursement, or program eligibility.

What the city can do is reduce the access barriers that cause healthcare costs to compound. The dominant local signal is that nearly half of United Way Ride United Network rides are for healthcare appointments, meaning transportation cost and healthcare access cost are functionally the same problem for many ALICE households.

Tier 2 instruments:
- Healthcare transportation: coordinating city-level transportation resources and advocacy for Ride United Network funding directly reduces the healthcare access burden.
- Community health worker programs: the city can fund or partner on community health worker outreach, particularly for immigrant communities with language barriers to healthcare navigation.
- City facility use: making city facilities available for mobile health clinics or vaccination events is within administrative authority.
- Language access: ensuring healthcare navigation materials and referral systems are available in Spanish and other languages spoken in District 2 is a city administrative function.

Tier 3:
- Insurance premium regulation (Maryland Insurance Administration, state)
- Medicaid eligibility and reimbursement rates (state)
- Medicare structure (federal)
- ACA marketplace subsidies (federal)

**Watch:** Federal policy changes (H.R. 1, Medicaid work requirements, ACA subsidy reductions) are active threats to healthcare coverage for ALICE households in Frederick. The city cannot offset these structurally but should be on record opposing them and should track the local impact.

---

### Technology

**Primary tier: Tier 1 and Tier 2**

Technology is the smallest ALICE budget line ($86/month per household) but is structurally important because technology access is a prerequisite for job search, telemedicine, remote education, and civic participation. The digital divide in Frederick is documented -- 80% of families receiving Chromebooks from United Way had no computer at home.

Tier 1 instruments available:
- Franchise agreements: the city negotiates cable and internet franchise agreements that include public access and institutional network provisions. These agreements are a direct lever on broadband availability and cost.
- Right-of-way policy: the city controls access to public rights-of-way for fiber and wireless infrastructure deployment. This affects both cost and coverage for internet providers.
- City facility WiFi: making city facilities (libraries, community centers, city hall) available as free public WiFi access points is within administrative authority.

Tier 2 instruments:
- Digital equity partnerships: the city can partner with Frederick County Public Libraries, Frederick Community College, and nonprofits on device access programs and digital literacy training.
- Affordable Connectivity Program advocacy: the federal ACP expired in 2024. The city can advocate for restoration and connect residents to any successor programs.

Tier 3:
- Federal broadband funding (BEAD program, E-Rate)
- FCC broadband access regulations

**Watch:** The franchise agreement renewal cycle is the highest-leverage moment for city action on broadband affordability. The timing of Frederick's current franchise agreements should be confirmed -- if renewal is approaching, this is a policy window.

### Financial Stability and Asset Building (Cross-Cutting)

**Primary tier: Tier 1 and Tier 2**

This is not a standalone ALICE budget category but cuts across all six domains. ALICE households have almost no financial cushion -- 32% have three months of emergency savings, 38% have any retirement assets. A single crisis event cascades across all six budget categories simultaneously.

The city has a set of tools that directly address financial stability independent of any single domain:

Tier 1 instruments:
- Municipal ID: a city-issued ID reduces the cost of financial exclusion for unbanked residents. Without ID, residents pay check-cashing fees that function as a tax on income. The Municipal ID is an affordability tool as much as an immigration protection tool. This connection is underleveraged in current framing.
- Fee waivers: city fee structures (permits, licenses, parking, utility deposits) can be made income-sensitive. Low-income households currently pay the same flat fees as high-income households for city services, which is a regressive cost structure.
- Living wage in city contracts: the city as a procurement actor can require living wage compliance from vendors and contractors, affecting wages for workers the city indirectly employs.

Tier 2 instruments:
- VITA (Volunteer Income Tax Assistance): the city can promote, co-locate, and help fund free tax preparation services that return earned income tax credits to ALICE households. EITC is one of the most effective anti-poverty tools available; low participation rates mean unclaimed money leaving the local economy.
- Financial empowerment programming: partnership with United Way's Prosperity Center (budget coaching, credit counseling, pathways to homeownership) through city budget allocations or facility support.
- Benefits navigation: connecting residents to the full range of programs they are eligible for -- SNAP, LIHEAP, CHIP, WIC, rental assistance -- is a city administrative function that does not require new programs, just intentional outreach infrastructure.

Tier 3:
- EITC eligibility and benefit levels (federal and state)
- SNAP, LIHEAP, CHIP program funding (federal)

## Legislative Sequencing Implications

The tiering has direct implications for what the council should prioritize legislatively versus what belongs in the budget, in advocacy letters, or in administrative directives.

Housing is where the council has the most authority and the most unfinished work. The Real Rent Ordinance, MPDU rate increase, payment-in-lieu rate modernization, and LMC reform are all Tier 1 actions that can produce measurable results without state permission.

Childcare and food are primarily Tier 2 -- the most productive near-term move is zoning reform that removes barriers to provider supply and food access, combined with partnership investments in the budget.

Transportation is a long-game land use problem. Every zoning decision that permits walkable, transit-adjacent density reduces the transportation cost burden for residents who live there. This should be a named criterion in every LMC reform decision.

Technology franchise agreements and the Municipal ID are the two Tier 1 tools outside housing that are most underutilized relative to their potential impact.

Financial stability tools -- VITA promotion, benefits navigation, fee waivers -- are low-cost, high-impact Tier 1 and Tier 2 actions that require political will and administrative attention more than new legislative authority.
