# Housing Production Strategies

A housing production strategy connects an assessment of need to the land, rules, infrastructure, financing, staff, and construction capacity required to deliver homes. A numerical target is one part of that strategy. Its usefulness depends on specifying which homes are needed, who they should serve, when they can be occupied, and who is responsible for implementation.

Production is also an anti-poverty tool, not only a supply-and-demand exercise. Pew's 2026 review of Austin and Minneapolis, both of which saw roughly 20% inflation-adjusted rent declines after zoning and permitting reforms, cites modeling finding that a rent decline of that size would cut poverty 18 to 26% and child poverty 21 to 38% across the ten highest-cost states, including Maryland, a magnitude comparable to the temporary 2021 child tax credit expansion and at no direct public cost. The mechanism is moving chains: new supply frees up older, cheaper units, with the largest rent relief in lower-income neighborhoods. Pew is explicit that this does not replace vouchers or LIHTC-financed units for the lowest-income households, who need subsidy regardless of how much the market loosens.[^pewpoverty] This is modeled, two-city evidence, not a Frederick projection, but it is the direct rebuttal to the claim that market-rate production only benefits higher-income renters. See also [[Housing Affordability and Inequality]].

## 1. Define the outcome before choosing the tool

Four different questions should be answered separately:

- **Need:** What housing problems exist, and what demand is projected?
- **Capacity:** What could legally and physically be built?
- **Delivery:** What can be financed, permitted, constructed, and occupied within the planning period?
- **Affordability:** Which households can actually afford the resulting homes, under what restrictions and for how long?

For example, allowing 1,000 additional units through zoning is a capacity change. It does not establish that 1,000 homes will be built, that they will be additional to what would otherwise occur, or that they will carry enforceable affordability restrictions.

### Follow projects through distinct stages

| Stage | Evidence to record | What it does not yet establish |
|---|---|---|
| Site or concept | Parcel, ownership, constraints, preliminary unit estimate | A feasible or approved project |
| Land-use approval | Decision, permitted density, conditions, expiration | Financing or construction readiness |
| Building permit | Issued permit, unit count, relevant conditions | A construction start |
| Construction | Verified start and progress | Completion by the forecast date |
| Completion/occupancy authorization | Completion record and certificate where applicable | Actual occupancy or household affordability |
| Occupancy and stewardship | Occupied homes, rents, household eligibility where applicable, covenant compliance | Permanent affordability without continuing legal and operational support |

Use a stable project identifier across these stages. A project moving from approval to construction should not be counted as two separate additions to supply. Track demolition and replacement as well as gross construction.

## 2. Frederick: verified baseline and policy status

### Projected demand and current hardship

The City's Housing Needs Assessment models **2025–2035** demand at **13,774 new homes**, divided into **7,284 for-sale** and **6,490 for-rent** units. It annualizes this as approximately **1,377 homes** over ten years. The model combines demand from new and existing households and holds several demographic and economic assumptions constant.[^frederick]

Separately, it identifies **8,133 existing cost-burdened households earning under $75,000**: 2,997 owners and 5,136 renters. It projects approximately **4,845 units of demand at or below 60% AMI**, including 3,666 rental units.[^frederick]

These figures answer different questions. Do not simply add current cost-burdened households to projected demand and call the sum a construction requirement. Existing households may need rental assistance, rehabilitation, refinancing, or a suitable lower-cost home. Overlap and the demand model's treatment of existing households must be understood first.

The assessment is a planning baseline, not a guarantee of future demand. Update assumptions and actual delivery periodically rather than resetting a ten-year annual average from whatever year a proposal happens to be debated.

### Proposed production and affordability goals

César's [[Housing Production Target Memo]] proposes **1,400 homes annually**, with **15% at or below 80% AMI** and **33% of that affordable share at or below 60% AMI**. The memo is evidence of a proposal, not by itself proof that these requirements have been enacted.

An illustrative calculation shows the distribution: 1,400 × 15% = **210** affordable homes; 210 × 33% = **69.3**, approximately 69–70 homes at the lower income threshold before applying any rounding rules. That is **4.95% of all units**, not 33% of all units. Exact one-third and 33% are slightly different formulations.

The remaining need requires an explicit strategy. Test the inclusionary share alongside subsidized production, preservation, rental support, and community ownership. A 60% AMI designation should not be described as reaching the deepest need without checking the rent against lower-income households' resources. See [[Housing Affordability and Inequality]].

The memo's 1,400-unit figure and affordability shares were not carried into the adopted resolution described below.

### Task force recommendations

The Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force final report describes estimated review periods of **18–24 months or more** and recommends moving qualifying affordable/workforce projects toward **8–10 months where feasible**. It recommends concurrent review, interdepartmental coordination, staffing, and performance tracking. The report also estimates that City-controlled changes could help place **4,000 additional homes in service over ten years**.[^taskforce]

Those are attributed baseline estimates, recommendations, and projections—not a measured reduction already achieved. Establish consistent start and stop dates before evaluating improvement, and do not add overlapping unit estimates from several strategies as if each produces a separate set of homes.

### Adopted goal: Resolution 26-15

Council adopted Resolution 26-15, "Declaring the City of Frederick's Commitment to Advancing Affordable Housing Production," 6-0 on **October 1, 2026**, as written. It adopts the task force report and the TPMA Housing Data Report (February 2026) as the City's evidence base, declares housing essential infrastructure and a human right, and adopts the task force's **4,000 additional homes over ten years** as the City's production goal. It lists nine priority recommendations: an annual housing priorities process, an Affordable Housing Navigator and implementation team, tiered AMI-based APFO exemptions, an MPDU threshold of 10 units (from 25) with deeper affordability and in-lieu fee revisions, 8 to 10 month expedited review, financing tools, City land disposition, missing-middle and ADU zoning, and a production dashboard. It commits resources to a Frederick County AMI standard, adds an anti-displacement section, requires an HHS annual Housing Production Report starting in FY2028, and calls on Frederick County to set comparable production goals ([[Hearing Brief 2026-10-01]]).

A resolution states policy and requests action. It cannot compel departments or produce units, so the goal and reporting requirement carry legal force only if codified. César's codification plan is [[Housing Production Accountability Ordinance]].

## 3. Build a target that can be tested

A defensible target should disclose its components and assumptions: household growth, existing unmet need, vacancy, replacement losses, tenure, income distribution, and planning horizon. Different models handle these components differently; compare definitions before comparing totals.

**Lancaster, Massachusetts**, provides a transparent example. Its 2024–2029 plan recommends **295 homes**: 210 for household growth, 77 for a vacancy adjustment, and eight for replacement. The plan calls this a recommendation, not a requirement or a cap. Its locally selected vacancy assumptions should not automatically become Frederick's standard.[^lancaster]

For Frederick, a practical target specification would include:

| Dimension | Decision to make |
|---|---|
| Counting basis | Gross completions, net additions, or another explicitly defined measure |
| Time | Baseline date, annual milestones, cumulative endpoint, and update schedule |
| Income | Mutually exclusive income bands; identify deeply affordable homes separately |
| Tenure and suitability | Rental/ownership, bedroom mix, accessibility, and household needs |
| Location | Access to jobs and services, infrastructure capacity, and displacement exposure |
| Duration | Market-rate, time-limited restriction, or long-term stewardship arrangement |
| Delivery | Responsible parties, budget, pipeline, and major dependencies |

Track preservation in a separate series so its benefit remains visible without inflating new-construction totals. A rehabilitated home can be an important success while contributing zero net new units.

### Compare the target to a realistic pipeline

Separate projects that are conceptual, approved, financed, under construction, and complete. Identify the specific obstacles to each project's next milestone. Forecast ranges should disclose assumptions about delay and cancellation.

Do not subtract every proposed unit from the target as though all are certain to finish. Conversely, a large approved pipeline does not prove zoning and review systems are adequate for future needs: inspect project age, type, location, and reasons for delay.

## 4. Match strategies to the obstacle

The following is an analytical comparison framework. It does not assert that every tool is authorized, funded, or suitable in Frederick.

| Strategy | Mechanism | What to test before relying on it |
|---|---|---|
| Zoning and building-type reform | Enables homes or configurations currently restricted | Site feasibility, infrastructure, financing, and likely uptake |
| Concurrent review and clear standards | Reduces avoidable sequencing, rework, and uncertainty | Staff capacity, dependencies, complete applications, and comparable elapsed time |
| Pre-approved plans and small-project pathways | Reduces repeated design/review work | Site-specific review, code updates, financing, and completed projects |
| Public land and infrastructure investment | Makes feasible sites available and serviceable | Title, utilities, remediation, disposition terms, subsidy value, and schedule |
| Construction or acquisition finance | Addresses a documented financing gap | Full project cash flow, repayment, risk, and affordability obligations |
| Fee relief or tax incentives | Changes project costs or operating income | Additional production attributable to the incentive and foregone public revenue |
| Affordable-housing requirements or subsidy | Directs a share of production toward specified households | Feasibility, income depth, actual rent, duration, enforcement, and selection |
| Preservation and rehabilitation | Retains useful homes and prevents losses | Physical needs, tenant protections, long-term affordability, and net-unit accounting |

Small-scale local builders are a production channel the table does not capture on its own. In Memphis, a builder trained through the Incremental Development Alliance built Malone Park Commons, a 35-unit for-rent pocket neighborhood of cottages and fourplexes, in phases over five years, revising the pro forma more than 30 times and stabilizing each phase before starting the next; his earlier work pushed Memphis to reduce minimum lot sizes ([[Incremental Developer Builds a 35-Unit Affordable For-Rent Pocket Neighborhood in Memphis]]). The account comes from an Alliance faculty member, so treat it as a case study. For Frederick, the lesson is that phased small projects depend on predictable small-lot rules and small-loan financing more than on large subsidies.

Financing details belong in [[Housing Finance and Preservation]], zoning in [[Zoning Reform]], and design/code options in [[Building Types and Codes]]. The production strategy should connect those instruments to one implementation schedule.

## 5. What peer examples actually demonstrate

### Portland: an adopted implementation framework

Portland adopted Resolution 37673 on August 28, 2024, establishing its Housing Production Strategy with **35 strategies across eight categories** and coordination among bureaus responsible for land use, transportation, infrastructure, economic development, and housing. Adoption did not itself appropriate all implementation funding; the resolution discusses later budget processes.[^portland]

The transferable element is organizational: assign a lead, partners, milestones, resources, and monitoring to each action. The resolution demonstrates adoption of a framework, not that all actions have been implemented or its housing goals achieved.

### Washington, DC: total production and affordability can diverge

DC reported **36,216 total homes delivered by July 29, 2024**, exceeding its 36,000-home goal. At the same date it reported **9,845 affordable homes**, or **82% of its separate 12,000-home affordable goal**. This is a dated official progress report, not a statement of today's final totals.[^dc]

That distinction is central to Frederick's dashboard design. Report overall delivery and affordability goals independently. The announcement also describes funding and multiple policy interventions; it cannot establish that a dashboard—or AI—caused the production outcome.

### Philadelphia: a goal broken into tracked steps

Philadelphia's H.O.M.E. Initiative, launched February 19, 2025, sets a goal to **create and preserve 30,000 housing units** with a four-year, $2 billion investment that includes $800 million in housing bonds.[^phl-home] The City broke its approval-process work into **39 recommendations** and publishes their status on Philly Stat 360, updated quarterly. Its March 2026 update listed 12 complete and 23 in progress. One completed item gets Zoning Board of Adjustment hearing results to applicants sooner, which the City says cut more than a month from the process.[^phl-tracker] A September 2026 Route Fifty column reports ZBA appeal times falling from 78 days to 12. That figure does not appear on the City pages reviewed and should be cited as reported, not verified.[^routefifty]

The transferable element is the Process and Accountability rows in section 8: a published list of discrete, City-controlled steps with owners and status, reported on a fixed schedule. It does not show that tracking caused any production outcome.

### North Carolina: coordination with an accountable lead

Executive Order 36, signed May 19, 2026, creates a Senior Advisor for Housing Policy and directs coordinated work across agencies. It calls for an initial tracking proposal within 90 days and subsequent semiannual reporting. Its provisions link housing with data, infrastructure, transportation, and other agency responsibilities.[^nc]

The transferable question is who owns the gaps between departments. An assigned coordinator needs staff capacity, escalation procedures, clear authority, and reporting. An executive order establishes duties; evidence of completed homes requires subsequent outcome records.

### Massachusetts: planning is not automatic legal safe harbor

Massachusetts distinguishes an approved Housing Production Plan from **certification based on qualifying production**. State guidance ties one- or two-year certification to production equivalent to 0.5% or 1% of the relevant year-round housing stock, subject to program requirements.[^mass]

Frederick can borrow the transparent target-and-progress method. Adopting a local target does not create Massachusetts Chapter 40B protections or a new legal basis to deny otherwise lawful development. The earlier source note's “internal safe harbor equivalent” is a political analogy, not Maryland law.

### Public land as an inventory and a subsidy

Two New York City actions illustrate how publicly owned land functions as a production tool in its own right, separate from zoning or process reform. In July 2026 the city launched the LIFT Tracker, a public inventory of more than 100 city-owned sites it assesses as having capacity for over 50,000 homes. The tool forces an internal accounting of what the city owns and each site's housing potential before any disposition decision, and makes that pipeline visible externally so a stalled site becomes a visible policy choice rather than a fact buried in agency files.[^nyclift] One project moving through that pipeline, 100 Gold Street, redevelops an aging city office building into roughly 4,000 homes with about a quarter permanently affordable and rent-stabilized, financed with no direct city capital: the city's contribution is the land itself, plus market-rate units that cross-subsidize the affordable share.[^nycpublicland]

The transferable elements are the inventory-first sequence and the financing logic, not the dollar figures. Frederick's own ArcGIS parcel data (see [[Frederick City GIS Server -- Query Reference]]) could support a first-pass inventory of City-owned parcels without new software; the harder step is a housing-capacity assessment for each site, and Lower Manhattan land values are not transferable to Frederick, so a land contribution here would likely close only part of a financing gap and would need to pair with the City Housing Fund, LIHTC, or a CDFI partner. See [[Housing Finance and Preservation]].

A state-level version of the same logic is Maryland's own: in April 2026, Governor Moore and MDOT unveiled a plan to develop 134 acres of state-owned land near Baltimore Metro stations into roughly 5,000 homes, using existing parking lots rather than new land clearing, with a first phase at Rogers Avenue Metro Station projected at about 400 homes.[^transitplan] This is a Baltimore Metro initiative and is not transferable to Frederick's MARC-served geography by name, but it is evidence that a state transportation agency will develop its own surplus land for housing when asked, which is relevant if MDOT or MTA holds land near Frederick's MARC station or elsewhere in the city.

### Adaptive reuse of vacant public and historic buildings

A related channel reuses a vacant public or institutional building rather than vacant land. In Caswell County, North Carolina, DreamKey Partners converted a vacant historic Black school, closed since 2002 and listed on the National Register of Historic Places, into 53 income-restricted senior apartments, layering HUD Section 202 senior-housing funding with state and federal historic tax credits; leasing drew more applicants than units in a county where about 42% of renters are cost-burdened.[^dillard] The transferable elements are the inventory-first sequence and the financing stack, not the unit count: a city identifies a vacant historic or institutional building it already owns or can acquire, then matches it to this kind of layered funding. Frederick's inventory of underused public and historic buildings, not only vacant land, is the natural starting point if this channel is considered.

## 6. Permitting, standard plans, and automation

### Measure the process before promising a reduction

A review-time statistic is useful only when its clock is defined. Separate initial submission, completeness determination, first review, applicant revisions, final approval, permit issuance, and construction. Report both total elapsed time and time in government review; neither alone tells the whole story.

Compare like projects and show the median and a longer-delay measure, such as the 90th percentile. Record the number of review cycles and why applications return. Concurrent reviews help only where dependencies permit them and staff can resolve conflicting comments.

Faster approvals may improve feasibility, but they cannot independently correct every financing, labor, infrastructure, or market constraint. Evaluate whether the reform produces additional starts and completed homes, not only a shorter queue.

### Pre-approved designs still require site work

Eugene's ADU program provides plans already reviewed for building-code compliance. Its FAQ explicitly preserves site design, site-related review, and associated fees. It therefore demonstrates a way to reduce repetitive work without treating a standard building plan as approval for every parcel.[^eugene]

For a Frederick program, evaluate current code compliance, accessibility options, lot fit, utility connections, plan licensing, and the cost to maintain the library. Count permits and completions alongside downloads.

### Raleigh: update the status, retain the jurisdictional boundary

Raleigh's official case tracker now lists **TC-4-26, Minor and Exempt Subdivision Regulations**, as **adopted September 15, 2026, effective October 15, 2026**. It was therefore adopted but not yet effective at this article's review date. The engagement materials describe exempt subdivisions and a simplified minor-subdivision pathway under North Carolina law.[^raleigh-status][^raleigh-design]

This corrects the original page's draft-only description. The source note's Durham/Raleigh timeline comparison is not used here as a verified estimate of savings from the adopted ordinance. Any Frederick counterpart requires analysis of Maryland law and the City's own subdivision requirements.

### Automation: a testable administrative tool

HUD's 2026 Automated Permitting Systems Demonstration funding notice seeks jurisdictions to deploy systems and evaluate their performance under real operating conditions. A funding opportunity is evidence of an evaluation initiative, not proof that grants have been awarded or production has increased.[^hud]

For Frederick, candidate uses include completeness checks, document routing, and retrieval of relevant code provisions. A pilot should have a defined scope, human responsibility for decisions, an accessible correction process, and checks for erroneous or inconsistent outputs. Compare processing time, revision cycles, error rates, staff workload, and user experience before and after deployment.

The original notes' Denver, San Jose, and Pittsburgh examples remain in the research trail; Philadelphia's process tracking is covered in section 5. Their reported statistics should not be combined into a general AI effect: organizational performance management, permit review, and housing-voucher administration are different interventions.

Those three examples, by reported mechanism: Denver's cross-departmental permitting team used shared data to cut review times about 30% over seven months; San Jose uses AI to prescreen permit applications for missing required documentation, shaving days off the front end of review; and Pittsburgh uses AI to scan housing-voucher applications for errors so staff spend more time helping applicants rather than re-checking paperwork. Bloomberg Philanthropies' synthesis of these cases states the operative guardrail plainly: AI amplifies whatever the underlying system is designed to do, so trustworthy data, clear policy, active human oversight, community feedback, and regular auditing have to be in place before a tool is deployed, not after.[^aiguardrails] None of these three examples has an independent, Frederick-reviewed evaluation; treat the reported figures as agency-reported, not independently verified.

## 7. Maryland legal timing and implementation

Maryland's **Housing Certainty Act, Chapter 591 of 2026**, is enacted with an **October 1, 2026 effective date**. Its final text addresses applicable rules at substantially complete application submission and, after all required approvals, vested rights for the longer of five years or a locally determined period, with conditions and exceptions. It should not be summarized as an unconditional five-year entitlement beginning with any initial submission.[^maryland]

Frederick's **G-26-11**, concerning the Act, records introduction on September 3 and approval on **September 17, 2026** in the official ordinance. The summary tracker's September 3 final-action date differs from that document.[^city-law] Approval of a local measure, the effective date of state law, and the treatment of a particular application are separate questions. Use the final ordinance and statute for project advice; the first-reader bill is not the controlling enacted text.

The archived Mercatus reform menu and state-permitting examples remain useful leads. Statewide density mandates, vested rights, fee changes, and “permit czar” arrangements have different scopes. Their applicability must be established individually rather than imported as a single package.

## 8. A production dashboard that supports decisions

A useful public dashboard should show both implementation and household outcomes. This proposed specification can guide a Frederick decision note:

| View | Core fields |
|---|---|
| Delivery | Net and gross completions, starts, issued permits, cancellations, and aging projects |
| Affordability | Completed restricted homes by income band, all-in rents, tenure, and restriction end date |
| Preservation | Homes retained, rehabilitation completed, restrictions extended, and losses avoided |
| Process | Defined review intervals, revision cycles, backlog age, and performance by project type |
| Public investment | Committed versus disbursed funds, public land contribution, foregone fees, and ongoing obligations |
| Resident outcomes | Occupancy, displacement/relocation, access for intended households, and compliance |
| Accountability | Lead department, milestone, dependency, last update, and explanation of deviations |

Use documented definitions and avoid presenting announcements as expenditures or approvals as completions. Outcomes should be disaggregated enough to reveal whether success at one income level or location conceals a shortfall elsewhere.

## 9. Using this page for a Council proposal

A production proposal is ready for comparison when it answers five questions:

1. **Which problem and households?** Cite the demand or hardship baseline, its data period, and the income/tenure needs being addressed.
2. **Which obstacle?** Identify the actual bottleneck and why the chosen tool will change it.
3. **What delivery is plausible?** Show the project pipeline, timetable, responsible parties, infrastructure, and financing assumptions.
4. **What does the public receive?** Specify additional homes, affordability depth and duration, resident protections, and total public cost.
5. **What would trigger adjustment?** Set review dates and explain what happens if permits rise but starts stall, or total production rises while affordable delivery falls short.

An adopted target is a public commitment. Its practical value comes from the implementation decisions, resources, and evidence that follow.
