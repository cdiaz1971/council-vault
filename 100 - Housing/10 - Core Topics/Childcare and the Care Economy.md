# Childcare and the Care Economy

## Purpose

Use this page to evaluate childcare as household infrastructure, a labor-market constraint, a small-business sector, and an early-childhood service. The City does not control licensing or the principal subsidy, but its land, permitting, facilities, grants, employer relationships, and convening power can change local supply and access.

### A separate county study: City hotspots and a County pilot

A 2025 Frederick County feasibility study, reported by WYPR in June 2026, uses county rather than statewide data and reaches a geographically specific conclusion directly relevant to the City: it identifies the **Route 40/Golden Mile corridor** as one of the county's sharpest childcare hotspots, alongside Thurmont, Walkersville, and Middletown, with some regions seeing as many as 3.3 children per licensed slot. It reports licensed capacity fell from 10,352 slots on January 1, 2020 to 9,485 on January 1, 2024, an 8.4% decline, while the county's population of young children is projected to keep growing; a separate county dataset reports an 18.1% drop in the number of childcare centers and a 6.1% drop in capacity from 2017 to 2024. The study estimates ALICE households face a combined $25 million annual affordability gap if all of them sought care, with childcare costs averaging close to 19% of household income against a federal benchmark of 7%.[^frederickstudy] These figures come from a different survey than the Maryland Family Network FY2026 report in the table above; cite each with its own source and date rather than combining them into one number.

In response to the state's 2025 Child Care Scholarship enrollment freeze (see below), Frederick County launched its own pilot scholarship for families with four-year-olds. This is a County, not City, program. It is a model for the City's navigation and employer-coordination role in the Authority map below, not a program the City funds or administers.[^frederickstudy]

## Interpret the capacity carefully

Licensed or regulated capacity is not the same as:

- an open vacancy;
- a full-day slot matching a parent’s work hours;
- affordable care;
- a slot in the child’s age group;
- geographically accessible care;
- care that accepts scholarship payments;
- inclusive care for a child with disabilities; or
- a stable slot in a program with sufficient staff.

Nor should total capacity be divided mechanically by all children age 0–5: some children are cared for by parents, relatives, schools, or unregulated arrangements, while some school-age children use the same regulated system. Local decisions need vacancy and utilization data by age, schedule, price, and geography.

## The childcare market’s central tension

Parents can face unaffordable prices while providers still cannot pay competitive wages. A policy that lowers parent prices without supporting provider revenue may shrink supply; a policy that expands physical capacity without workforce or operating support may create licensed but unusable rooms.

Evaluate every proposal across four linked constraints:

| Constraint | Diagnostic question |
|---|---|
| Household affordability | What share of income do families pay after scholarships and employer support? |
| Provider economics | Do rates cover wages, benefits, rent, insurance, food, administration, and required ratios? |
| Workforce | Can programs recruit and retain qualified staff at offered compensation? |
| Physical/regulated supply | Are suitable sites permitted, financed, licensed, and located where care is needed? |

## Child Care Scholarship status

Maryland’s Child Care Scholarship program is administered by the State. The Office of Early Childhood reports a waitlist created by high demand and says that, beginning July 1, 2026, a small number of families have been moved from the waitlist as funds allow. Current income rules, priority categories, provider requirements, and application instructions should be checked on the [State Child Care Scholarship page](https://earlychildhood.marylandpublicschools.org/child-care-providers/child-care-scholarship-program) before public guidance is issued.

Three groups are exempt from the waitlist regardless of the enrollment freeze: families receiving Temporary Cash Assistance or SSI, siblings of a child already enrolled, and, as of July 1, 2026, families experiencing homelessness under HB 849 (Chapter 594 of 2026). MSDE reports that close to 3,000 children have been offered scholarships through these exemptions since the waitlist began; eligible families get 30 days to submit documentation after notification, and families not covered by an exemption stay on the waitlist.[^ccsupdate]

The City can assist with outreach, trusted navigation, employer coordination, and provider readiness. It cannot promise eligibility, award timing, or reimbursement levels.

## Local option matrix

| Option | Problem addressed | Design questions | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Zoning/code audit and permit navigator | Site and approval barriers | Where is care allowed? Are parking, drop-off, occupancy, and home-occupation rules proportionate? | Time/cost to opening; applications completed |
| Small capital grants or loans | Build-out, safety, equipment | Who owns improvements? Is operating viability demonstrated? | Net new usable slots by age/geography |
| City-facility partnership | Scarce/expensive space | Procurement, licensing, rent, maintenance, liability, public access | Stable slots and operating continuity |
| Workforce partnership | Hiring and retention | Wages, benefits, training time, transportation, career path | Vacancy/turnover and compensation |
| Scholarship/application navigation | Administrative access | Coordination with State and providers | Approved cases, reduced incomplete applications |
| Employer consortium | Irregular demand or employer recruitment | Shared contribution, hours, employee portability | Employee use, retention, absenteeism |
| Targeted operating support | Affordability and fragile provider economics | Duration, rate method, priority population, maintenance of effort | Parent price and provider stability |
| Data partnership | Poor visibility into vacancies and gaps | Privacy, reporting burden, update frequency | Reliable dashboard and faster placement |

Count **net** slots: new or preserved usable capacity minus closures or reductions elsewhere. Report infant/toddler capacity separately.

## Land-use and permitting review

Before proposing a code change, map the actual process from site search through State license. Review:

- districts where centers and family childcare are permitted by right or conditionally;
- occupancy, fire, health, accessibility, outdoor-space, parking, and drop-off requirements;
- whether similar uses face comparable standards;
- typical review time, rework, fees, and points of failure;
- rules affecting home-based providers and renters; and
- proximity to jobs, housing, transit, schools, and priority populations.

Do not assume zoning is the binding constraint until the City can show where applications stall or sites become infeasible.

## Data plan

Build a quarterly or semiannual dashboard using State/provider data where available:

- regulated programs and licensed capacity by age, geography, and setting;
- estimated vacancies and wait times by age/schedule;
- openings, closures, and net slot change;
- prices and scholarship acceptance;
- provider staffing vacancies, turnover, wages, and benefit availability;
- permit volume, processing time, fees, and abandoned applications;
- family travel time and nonstandard-hours need;
- scholarship navigation outcomes; and
- public dollars per net new or preserved slot.

Protect family and provider privacy; small-area results may need suppression.

## Decision framework

For each proposal, ask:

1. Which constraint—price, provider economics, workforce, site, regulation, schedule, or information—is binding?
2. Which age group, schedule, geography, and population are targeted?
3. Is the intervention within City authority, or is a State/County commitment documented?
4. Does it create or preserve usable slots, improve affordability, improve quality/stability, or all three?
5. What is the provider’s operating plan after one-time funds end?
6. Are wages and workforce conditions consistent with the promised capacity?
7. How will displacement or closure of existing providers be avoided?
8. What data will establish a counterfactual or at least a credible before/after result?

## Council checklist

- [ ] Use the FY2026 Frederick baseline and date every figure.
- [ ] Distinguish licensed capacity, vacancies, enrollment, and demand.
- [ ] Separate infant/toddler, preschool, school-age, and nonstandard-hour needs.
- [ ] Identify the binding constraint before selecting the intervention.
- [ ] State which entity controls licensing, scholarships, facilities, and implementation.
- [ ] Show the operating plan, not only capital cost.
- [ ] Report net new/preserved usable slots and workforce outcomes.
- [ ] Set a review date and prevent one-time funding from becoming an unfunded promise.

## State model: New Mexico

New Mexico began offering no-cost child care to all families regardless of income on **November 1, 2025**, the first state to do so ([Governor's announcement](https://www.governor.state.nm.us/2025/09/08/new-mexico-is-first-state-in-nation-to-offer-universal-child-care/)). Programs that pay at least $18 an hour to entry-level staff receive higher incentive rates. **SB 241**, signed March 10, 2026, put the program in statute and funds it from the state's Early Childhood Trust Fund ([ECECD 2026 session summary](https://www.nmececd.org/2026-legislative-session/)). The funding model is a State-level choice built on a large trust fund. Frederick cannot replicate it, though it is relevant to State advocacy.

A companion law is more directly useful to the City. **SB 96** (effective July 1, 2026) requires localities to treat licensed child care homes like other residences under zoning. It bars special fees, permits, or added parking requirements for them, bars restricting centers in commercial, mixed-use, and multifamily zones, and bars homeowners' associations from prohibiting child care homes ([ECECD 2026 session summary](https://www.nmececd.org/2026-legislative-session/)). It gives the City's land-use review above a concrete checklist. Before drafting, compare it with Maryland's existing protections for family child care homes and with [[Child Care Supply LMC Amendment]]. Source note: [[New Mexico Universal Child Care]].
