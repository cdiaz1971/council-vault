# Housing Affordability and Inequality

Housing affordability describes the relationship between the cost of a suitable home and a household's resources. Housing inequality concerns how access, costs, security, location, and ownership wealth differ across households. A community can add homes and still have substantial hardship among people with low incomes; it can also lower an average burden rate while vulnerable residents leave. A useful assessment therefore asks **affordable to whom, at what total cost, in which location, for how long, and with what security?**

> [!info] Scope and currency
> Reviewed October 3, 2026. Footnotes identify external evidence; vault links preserve research and César's policy reasoning. Report publication years and underlying data years are distinguished. This is a reference article for developing decision notes, rather than an adopted Council position or an eligibility determination for an individual household.

## 1. Measuring different aspects of affordability

| Measure | What it answers | Main limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Housing cost burden | How much household income goes to housing? | A percentage does not show whether the remaining money covers other necessities. |
| Residual income | What remains after housing, and is it enough for other needs? | Results depend on the household budget, taxes, and necessities included. |
| ALICE financial hardship | Is income sufficient for a locally estimated basic household budget? | It measures a broader budget shortfall, not housing need alone or eligibility for assistance. |
| Program income limits, often expressed as a percentage of area median income | Which households can qualify under a particular program? | Eligibility does not demonstrate that the offered rent is affordable to every eligible household. |
| Home price relative to income | How have purchase prices changed relative to incomes? | It omits mortgage rates, down payments, insurance, taxes, and maintenance. |

### Cost burden and residual income

The conventional housing cost-burden threshold is **more than 30% of income**; severe burden is **more than 50%**. Use the source's precise definition, including its treatment of utilities and households for which a ratio cannot be calculated. Census measures include housing expenses beyond the advertised rent or mortgage payment.[^burden][^severe]

The 30% benchmark is useful for consistent comparisons. Its weakness is that two households at the same percentage can have very different amounts left to live on. As a simple illustration, a household with $2,000 in monthly gross income and $600 in housing costs has $1,400 remaining before taxes and other expenses; one with $8,000 and $2,400 has $5,600. Both spend 30%. Household size, disability-related costs, child care, transportation, and debt can change the practical result further.

For a proposal, calculate both the percentage and a household budget. Include required fees and tenant-paid utilities in the housing comparison, then test transportation and care costs separately. Do not silently mix gross-income and take-home-income calculations.

### ALICE and poverty

**ALICE** means Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed. In the research classification, ALICE households have incomes above the Federal Poverty Level but below the estimated basic cost of living. **Below the ALICE Threshold** combines ALICE households and households in poverty. It is therefore incorrect to describe every household below the threshold as above-poverty ALICE.[^method]

The Household Survival Budget includes housing, child care, food, transportation, health care, technology, taxes, and a contingency allowance. Budgets vary by county and household composition. The Survival Budget does not provide the longer-term savings allowance included in the separate Stability Budget. A single countywide dollar figure should not be used as the necessary income for every household.[^method]

ALICE status does not establish eligibility or ineligibility for public benefits. Programs have their own income definitions, household rules, and other conditions. Housing assistance commonly uses HUD income limits based on median family income and geographic definitions; tax-credit projects have their own applicable income-limit tables. The previous claim that housing vouchers and most other assistance can all be evaluated from one poverty-line cutoff was too broad.[^hud-income]

## 2. Frederick baseline

### Financial hardship

The **2026 ALICE release uses 2024 data**. United Way of Frederick County reports **34,465 of 108,330 County households** below the threshold: approximately **31.8%**, published as **32%**. These figures describe an earlier data period, not a real-time count of households affected by 2026 policy changes.[^county]

The local presentation reports **43% for Frederick city** and **33,584 City households** on its census-place slide. This article uses that directly checked, rounded City rate. The prior vault synthesis gave 42.7% and 14,343 households; those more precise figures remain in its archived source rather than being presented here as independently reproduced.[^slides] At the October 1, 2026 Council meeting, Council President Díaz cited the 2026 report to note that the number of struggling households rose in the City while falling in the County, and said those families should be Council's focus; this is a council-record characterization of the trend, not an independently recalculated year-over-year figure in this review. [[Hearing Minutes 2026-10-01]].

The vault's underlying research identifies the County figures as ACS one-year estimates and the City figures as five-year estimates. **Do not use their arithmetic difference as a like-for-like annual City–County gap.** Census guidance advises comparing matching ACS products and non-overlapping five-year periods. The City was at 43% in both the 2019 to 2023 and 2020 to 2024 periods; the 45% figure previously used in the vault is Frederick County Election District 2, a county subdivision larger than the City and unrelated to City Council District 2, and should not be compared with the City rate.[^acs]

The old explanation that the City rate is higher *because of* its mix of renters, immigrants, single parents, and lower-wage workers was a local interpretation. These headline rates do not establish that causal explanation. Testing it would require comparable data by income, tenure, household composition, and geography.

### Household budgets

United Way's Frederick County presentation gives the following **2024 County Survival Budgets**, not Maryland averages:[^slides]

| Household | Annual basic budget | Monthly housing and utilities |
|---|---:|---:|
| Single adult | $50,868 | $1,949 |
| Two adults with two children in child care | $121,080 | $2,250 |
| Single senior | $54,780 | $1,949 |

These are modeled budgets, not observed spending for every household. The original page mislabeled the $50,868 amount as a Maryland average and offered $88,330/$73,428 as general age-based thresholds. Household-specific budgets are more useful for evaluating who a proposal serves; those two general thresholds are not used here as individual eligibility tests.

### Geography and trends

ZIP areas, census tracts, Council districts, and municipal boundaries answer different questions. Do not treat a ZIP-wide hardship percentage as a District 2 rate or assume every household in a high-hardship area has the same need. A district analysis should identify the boundary and data period and explain how smaller geographies were assigned to it.

For housing-specific need, HUD's **Comprehensive Housing Affordability Strategy (CHAS)** data provides household counts by income and housing problems. At review, HUD's page identifies its latest release as based on **2018–2022 ACS five-year estimates**. It can help target housing policy, but its older period must be visible alongside newer ALICE figures.[^chas]

## 3. Why housing becomes unaffordable

Affordability can deteriorate because housing costs rise, household resources fall, or both. The following mechanisms interact; a proposal should state which one it addresses and how its effect will be measured.

### Housing supply, demand, and location

Additional homes can absorb demand that would otherwise compete for existing homes. Asquith, Mast, and Reed's study of large market-rate apartment buildings in low-income areas found lower nearby rents relative to comparison locations and increased moves from low-income areas. This is evidence that new construction can relieve local rent pressure. It does not establish that every project benefits every nearby household or that market-rate production alone reaches the lowest-income renters.[^supply]

Pew's 2026 synthesis of Austin and Minneapolis, where zoning and permitting reforms were followed by roughly 20% inflation-adjusted rent declines, cites economist Zachary Parolin's modeling that a rent decline of that size would cut poverty 18 to 26% and child poverty 21 to 38% across the ten highest-cost states, a magnitude comparable to the temporary 2021 child tax credit expansion. The mechanism is supply-driven moving chains: new units free up older, cheaper units, with the largest rent relief concentrated in lower-income neighborhoods. Pew is explicit that supply lowers the floor for renters generally but does not substitute for vouchers or LIHTC-financed units for the lowest-income households.[^pewpoverty] This is modeled national and two-city evidence, not a Frederick-specific forecast, but it directly answers the claim that market-rate production only benefits affluent households.

Location, price, timing, and tenant circumstances matter. Distinguish permission to build from financed projects, completed homes, occupied homes, and homes affordable to a specified group. For additional policy detail, see [[Housing Production Strategies]], [[Zoning Reform]], and [[Parking Reform]].

### Incomes and the regional economy

Buchholz, Kemeny, Randolph, and Storper's January 2026 **working paper** argues that unequal income growth and geographically concentrated demand explain much of the affordability crisis. Its simulations suggest that even large deregulation-induced supply increases would take decades to achieve broad affordability in expensive markets. This is a research argument with model-dependent conclusions, rather than proof that supply has no effect.[^inequality]

The paper and the local construction study examine different scales and outcomes: broad affordability across income groups versus effects near particular new buildings. The decision-relevant question is how much relief a tool can deliver, to which households, and on what timetable.

César's [[Inequality Is the Housing Crisis -- Synthesis]] and [[Knowledge Economy and Housing -- Synthesis]] apply this argument to Frederick's position in the DC–Baltimore regional economy. The proposed mechanism is that higher-income commuters and remote workers can outbid lower-paid local workers. That remains a local hypothesis to test with migration, commuting, wage, rent, and construction evidence; the cited national research does not estimate Frederick's causal shares.

### Wealth and access to ownership

Income pays recurring expenses; wealth can supply a down payment, absorb a shock, or support a relative's purchase. A 2022 Federal Reserve Bank of San Francisco research summary links parental home-equity extraction to children's home purchases and subsequent housing wealth. Its estimates support a role for family wealth in unequal access to ownership. They do not show that inheritance is the only route to ownership or quantify that mechanism in Frederick.[^wealth]

This corrects the prior attribution: the parental-equity finding is separate research, not a newly established result of Harvard's 2026 housing report. A homeownership program should therefore test both entry costs and sustainable ongoing costs, and identify how resale rules divide appreciation between the current owner and future affordability. See [[Community Land Trusts and Social Ownership]].

### Credit, financing, and ownership power

The archived [[Erdmann's Contagion Cities and the 2008 Credit Crackdown]] records Kevin Erdmann's credit-access explanation and Michael Lewyn's critique. Retain it as an attributed debate. The original credit-score lending percentages are not used here as independently verified evidence that one post-2008 mechanism explains today's rents.

Dēmos and People's Action's 2026 report emphasizes corporate power, unequal wealth, wages, and public investment. It presents an advocacy framework for changing the distribution of economic resources and control.[^demos] César's [[Affordability Is a Power Problem Not a Price Problem -- Synthesis]] develops that framework into proposals for production, community ownership, and conditions on public deals.

To evaluate these ideas locally, measure ownership concentration, acquisition activity, fees, maintenance, and tenant outcomes. A general account of corporate power does not demonstrate that Frederick's new supply is all captured by concentrated owners. See [[Corporate Landlords and Private Equity]] and [[Housing Finance and Preservation]] for the relevant instruments and financing questions.

## 4. What national evidence establishes—and what it does not

Harvard JCHS's June 2026 summary reports existing home prices near five times median income and a loss of approximately seven million rental units below $1,000 over the preceding decade, through loss of units or movement into higher rent categories. Its rent-series note specifies inflation adjustment and contract rents excluding utilities. These figures establish national context, not a Frederick inventory.[^jchs]

Harvard also reports that, in 2024, 83% of renters earning under $30,000 spent over 30% of income on housing and 66% spent over half. Broad supply gains and severe low-income hardship can coexist. The policy implication is to measure market conditions and outcomes for the households a program intends to help separately.[^jchs]

**Cost burden does not mean being excluded from the housing market.** Census reported that 49.7% of renter households for whom burden could be calculated, and 56.2% of Black-alone renter households, were cost-burdened in 2023. These are largely housed renters paying a high share of income. The original phrase “priced out of the market” mischaracterized that statistic.[^race]

The 2026 Maryland ALICE report provides a different measure: in 2024, 49% of Black households, 45% of Hispanic households, and 32% of White non-Hispanic households were below the ALICE Threshold. Race and ethnicity categories can overlap, and these are statewide household figures, not City rates.[^state]

Parolin's companion analysis, using the Census Bureau's Supplemental Poverty Measure, identifies Maryland as one of ten high-cost states where 16 to 34% of measured poverty is attributable to above-average housing costs, and finds that in those states rising rents absorbed more than half of the growth in SNAP benefits between 1989 and 2023. This is a state-level finding, not a Frederick-specific statistic, but it names Maryland directly and gives the ALICE baseline a mechanism: income and benefit gains are being captured by housing costs before they reach a household's remaining budget.[^pewmaryland]

For local equity analysis, report both **rates within groups** and **numbers of households affected**. Examine whether a policy reaches low-income renters, households with children, older residents, people with disabilities, and residents facing language or documentation barriers. State and national disparities justify investigation; they cannot supply missing City estimates.

## 5. Matching policy tools to household outcomes

The following is an analytical framework for comparing proposals, not a finding that each instrument is already authorized or funded.

| Policy approach | Intended household benefit | What a decision note should test |
|---|---|---|
| More housing and a wider range of building types | More options and less competition for existing homes | Completed units, bedroom mix, rents, location, timing, and displacement during redevelopment |
| Rental assistance or targeted operating support | Lower immediate out-of-pocket housing costs | Who qualifies, benefit size, take-up, landlord participation, and funding continuity |
| Income-restricted production and preservation | Homes affordable at specified incomes over a defined period | Actual rents with utilities, income levels served, covenant duration, enforcement, and tenant outcomes |
| Tenant protections and legal assistance | Greater stability and fewer avoidable housing losses | Legal authority, access to help, enforcement, evictions, and unintended barriers to access |
| Community ownership and shared-equity homeownership | Durable affordability and an ownership pathway | Entry costs, stewardship capacity, resale terms, and distribution of appreciation |
| Transportation, child care, utility, and benefits-access measures | More resources remaining after essential costs | Net household savings, service availability, accessibility, and partner delivery capacity |

### Frederick implementation context

César's [[Affordability Agenda -- Council Authority Tiering by Domain]] is a useful planning map: City action, partnership, and state/federal advocacy. Its examples should be treated as proposals with instrument-specific authority and status, rather than as a blanket legal finding.

For each initiative, specify the responsible agency, legal instrument, funding source, implementation date, and expected household benefit. The City's affordable-housing task force provides a current source of recommendations on production and preservation; recommendations require subsequent implementation decisions.[^taskforce]

Two distinctions are particularly important:

- **Income geography:** HUD program limits, Frederick County household income statistics, and a City program's income standard are not interchangeable. Frederick's Chapter 19 includes the City-specific FCAMI concept; a proposal should cite the applicable section and current administrative table rather than calculate eligibility from the County median household income.[^citycode][^hud-income]
- **Child care administration:** Maryland's Child Care Scholarship Program is managed under MSDE through centralized CCS administration. The prior statement that Frederick County administers the state subsidy should not be relied on. County/provider partnerships and City siting proposals are separate implementation questions.[^childcare]

The earlier note's proposed rental-fee rules, MPDU changes, municipal ID uses, procurement standards, and broadband measures remain in the research trail. Their mention there is not evidence of enactment, preemption clearance, bank acceptance, or funded delivery.

## 6. Allocation, fairness, and public explanation

Producing an affordable home and selecting its occupant are separate decisions. César's [[The Allocation Question Cannot Be Messaged Away]] proposes a published, consistently applied need-based principle, with attention to housing burden, displacement risk, household composition, and local ties. That is a normative proposal worth assessing publicly.

Allocation rules must also fit the particular funding program. For example, **Housing Choice Voucher** regulations prohibit residency requirements, constrain residency preferences, and prohibit preferences based on how long someone has lived or worked in the area. The regulations also require covered workers to be treated as residents for a residency preference. Those rules should not be generalized automatically to every MPDU or locally funded home, but they demonstrate why “local ties” needs a specific program analysis.[^allocation]

JRF and FrameWorks UK's 2026 toolkit draws on UK research to recommend explanations centered on shared benefits, effective policy, and collective responsibility. It identifies scarcity and fatalism as barriers to constructive discussion. It is communications evidence from another setting, not proof that a particular message will work in Frederick or a substitute for defensible eligibility rules.[^framing]

[[What We Mean When We Say Affordable -- Class War Framing and the Housing Model Choice]] preserves César's argument for emphasizing ownership and who benefits from appreciation. In using it, distinguish empirical findings from preferred values and political judgments. Broad labels such as “abundance” or “Vienna” encompass varied policies; compare actual ownership, financing, tenant rights, and allocation arrangements.

## 7. Using this page for a Council decision

For each proposal, complete these five statements:

1. **Who needs help?** Define household incomes, sizes, tenure, geography, and relevant access barriers. Use a dated baseline with the correct denominator.
2. **What changes for them?** Estimate the all-in monthly housing cost, remaining household resources, and stability or ownership benefit.
3. **How does the tool cause that change?** Identify the mechanism, implementation timetable, responsible party, and evidence supporting the expected effect.
4. **Who receives the benefit, and for how long?** State selection rules, affordability duration, enforcement, and who receives appreciation or other financial gains.
5. **How will we know it worked?** Choose household outcomes as well as outputs: burden, residual resources, retention, evictions, access, and completed homes. Track public cost and effects on residents who do not receive assistance.

An illustrative test: calling a unit “affordable at 80% AMI” does not answer whether a household earning $35,000 can afford it. If its all-in monthly housing cost is $1,500, that household spends approximately **51.4% of gross income** on housing ($18,000 ÷ $35,000). This is hypothetical arithmetic, not a current Frederick rent or eligibility limit. It shows why the income target and the household outcome must both be stated.
