# Community Land Trusts and Social Ownership

> [!info] Reference scope — checked 2026-09-26
> This page synthesizes the vault's research with linked external sources. Dated program figures describe the cited reporting period. Frederick proposals and César's interpretations appear in their own section. Unresolved legal or factual questions are identified at the point where they matter.

## Overview

A **community land trust (CLT)** is an organization that acquires and stewards land or property for a continuing community purpose. In the familiar homeownership arrangement, the CLT retains the land and the household purchases the home under a long-term ground lease. A resale rule limits the price paid by the next purchaser. The household gains an affordable entry price and some equity; the restriction preserves access for future buyers. Some trusts use deed restrictions or other arrangements instead. CLTs can also steward rental housing, cooperatives, commercial property, and other land uses. Maryland's statutory *affordable housing land trust agreement* is expressly not a ground lease; see the Maryland section.[^hud-home][^census][^md-landtrust]

**Social ownership** is broader: it can include CLTs, limited-equity cooperatives, publicly owned housing, and community-controlled commercial property. These models differ in who holds title, who governs, who receives income or equity, and how affordability is enforced. A CLT is therefore one institutional tool within a wider field, not a synonym for all nonmarket housing.

### Models at a glance

| Model | Land/building control | Household interest | Main affordability mechanism |
|---|---|---|---|
| CLT homeownership | In classic model, nonprofit holds land; in Maryland statutory model, trust may hold a proprietary or reversionary interest | Home or other property interest, subject to conditions | Ground lease **or** Maryland affordable housing land trust agreement with transfer restrictions |
| Limited-equity cooperative | Cooperative controls building; land may be owned or leased | Membership/share and occupancy rights | Limited share resale and cooperative rules |
| CLT plus cooperative | CLT stewards land; cooperative controls housing | Cooperative membership | Ground lease plus cooperative restrictions |
| Public or municipal social housing | Public entity generally owns or controls housing | Typically tenancy | Public ownership, financing, and occupancy policy |
| Commercial CLT | Community entity controls site | Business lease or other occupancy right | Long-term land control and lease terms |

These are typical arrangements, not interchangeable legal definitions. Actual documents and funding agreements control each project.[^hud-home][^lincoln-partnership][^md-landtrust]

## How CLT homeownership works

1. **Acquire and capitalize.** A trust buys or receives land or homes, often using grants, municipal land, philanthropic funds, borrowing, or developer contributions. Acquisition and construction still need financing.[^lincoln-partnership]
2. **Sell or lease the improvements.** For a classic CLT home, a qualifying household buys the dwelling while the trust retains the underlying land. HUD recognizes ownership of a dwelling subject to a CLT ground lease of at least 50 years as homeownership for the HOME program. Maryland's distinct statutory land-trust agreement can instead secure resale restrictions through a recorded agreement.[^hud-home][^md-landtrust]
3. **Set continuing obligations.** A lease or other recorded instrument sets occupancy, maintenance, transfer, and resale terms. CLTs may also provide purchase counseling, monitor loans, and intervene when an owner has trouble paying.[^foreclosure]
4. **Resell affordably.** The owner receives the resale value determined by the governing formula, and the buyer receives a price below what an unrestricted sale might command. The precise split of appreciation varies by program; restricted resale limits the owner's upside and may limit the downside as well.[^census][^foreclosure]

The model preserves an affordability restriction across transactions **if the organization, legal instruments, and stewardship remain effective**. It does not make a home free of maintenance, taxes, financing costs, or administrative costs. HUD explicitly treats monthly ground-lease fees as an ongoing housing expense in HOME underwriting and notes that a higher fee may require a greater initial subsidy.[^hud-home]

### Contrast with time-limited restrictions

Some inclusionary-housing and tax-credit projects have defined affordability periods. Their exact duration and what happens at expiration depend on the recorded agreement, funding layers, and later preservation decisions. A CLT can impose a continuing restriction intended to outlast those periods. It would be inaccurate to say that **every** MPDU or LIHTC unit automatically converts to market price on a single specified date. In particular, do not import *Frederick County's* MPDU control periods into *Frederick City's* separate Chapter 19; examine the City code and the recorded project agreement for a local comparison.[^lincoln-partnership][^city-ch19]

## Governance and accountability

The classic CLT design brings residents, other community members, and public-interest representatives into governance; actual boards vary. A municipality may donate land or funds without running the CLT, or may take a more active role. The useful questions are who appoints the board, whether residents can change policy, how conflicts are resolved, what happens if the organization fails, and who enforces resale restrictions.[^hud-classic][^lincoln-partnership]

Shared ownership does not itself guarantee representative governance. A cooperative gives resident members formal control over building decisions, while a CLT can hold the land and preserve affordability. That combination requires workable ground leases, share financing, maintenance reserves, and organizational capacity ([[CLT and Co-op Housing Integration -- Lessons from Canada]]). Maryland's 2026 cooperative legislation addresses a route for converting certain nonstock corporations into cooperative limited-equity housing corporations; it is not, by itself, a general authorization or funding program for every proposed CLT/co-op structure.[^md-hb85]

## Finance, stewardship, and tradeoffs

Putting land or homes into long-term stewardship can protect a public investment across successive households. But capital must cover acquisition or development, and reliable revenue must cover counseling, compliance, accounting, legal work, maintenance obligations where applicable, and future transactions. Whether later **public** subsidy is needed depends on the particular project, the market, and the organization's balance sheet. The previous vault brief's statement that a one-time investment eliminates ongoing subsidy is a policy hypothesis, not an established universal result ([[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]]).[^hud-home][^lincoln-partnership]

The central tradeoff in resale-restricted ownership is **access for future households versus unrestricted appreciation for the current owner**. Other constraints include finding suitable sites, borrowing against unusual ownership interests, keeping fees affordable, sustaining professional stewardship, and scaling a portfolio. Rental, cooperative, and commercial models introduce different operating and capital-repair risks. A comparison with conventional rental subsidies or unrestricted ownership should specify the outcome sought: affordable entry, long-run affordability, household wealth, resident control, or units produced per public dollar.[^census][^lincoln-partnership]

## What the evidence establishes

- **National scale:** Grounded Solutions Network and researchers at the University of Toronto identified **314 CLT and shared-equity entities** in their 2022 census; the survey response rate was **62%**. The census includes self-identified CLTs and nonprofit shared-equity homeownership programs, but excludes organizations exclusively providing cooperatives from its defined population. **314 is not a count of CLTs alone.** The earlier vault page's “roughly 225” and “over 300” come from different sources and should not be treated as comparable measurements.[^census]
- **Foreclosure performance:** Research on CLT homeowners during the 2007–2009 downturn found lower delinquency and foreclosure than among unrestricted homeowners. Its period, samples, and program selection limit any claim that the same difference must hold in every future market.[^foreclosure]
- **Wealth and affordability:** A 2019 analysis assembled data from more than 4,000 homes in 58 shared-equity programs across 20 states. It examines outcomes across several shared-equity forms, so its results should not automatically be assigned solely to CLTs.[^shared-equity-study]
- **Civic participation:** The vault's claim that CLT residents *consistently* participate more in civic life comes from an interpretive newsletter and César's synthesis ([[Community Land Trusts as Civic and Political Infrastructure]]; [[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]]). Keep it as a potential mechanism or research question until a suitable comparative study supports the generalization.

## Illustrative applications

**College Park, Maryland.** The College Park Community Preservation Trust is a useful in-state example of a city-supported shared-equity program. Its own account reports nine homes purchased and sold to qualifying households through 2024, supported by city, state, federal, and regional grants. Its 2025 report describes **11 properties** in the portfolio, including ten single-family houses and a mixed-use property with four apartments and commercial space. The different figures describe different reporting dates and measures; they are not a contradiction. The trust's legal instruments, subsidy per home, resale terms, and municipal obligations need examination before calling it a directly replicable Frederick model.[^college-2024][^college-2025]

**Pensacola, Florida.** The City reports that its transaction with Pensacola Habitat for Humanity for the former Malcolm Yonge Center site closed in August 2026. Its plan is for nine homes in the Northwest Florida CLT, with affordability intended for 99 years. Earlier reporting described a planned project; the later City update supports the completed land transaction, **not** completion or occupancy of the homes. This is an example of public land conveyed for CLT development, with project results still to be measured.[^pensacola]

**Cambridge, Massachusetts.** The City's Social Housing Task Force began meeting in June 2026 to evaluate possible models and make recommendations. It is an example of an exploratory municipal process, **not** a completed social-housing program. The vault's proposed financing and income targets should be described as proposals unless adopted in a subsequent official record.[^cambridge]

**Commercial and cooperative cases in the vault.** [[Commercial CLT Acquisition Lowers Barrier for Mission-Aligned Small Businesses]] and [[CLT Commercial Financing Gap Requires CDFI Partnerships and Community Capital]] summarize the **same Fresno reporting**; [[Neighborhood Trusts as a Model for Community-Controlled Commercial Real Estate]] and [[Community-Owned Real Estate as a Path to Wealth and Political Power]] summarize the **same Philadelphia reporting**. Treat each pair as one source, not two independent confirmations. [[CLT and Co-op Housing Integration -- Lessons from Canada]] provides a comparative account of co-op integration. These examples show possible forms of stewardship; their transaction-level figures are retained in the underlying notes until independently checked.

**Community investment trusts (Portland, Oregon).** A community investment trust lets neighborhood residents who are not accredited investors buy small shares in local commercial property. The East Portland CIT bought Plaza 122, a 29,000-square-foot strip mall with about 30 tenants, for $1.2 million in 2014 and opened it to resident investors in 2017. Investors must live in four East Portland zip codes, complete a free six-hour financial course, and may invest up to $5,000. The operator reports 330 investing households, about $648,000 invested, a share price that rose from $10 to $19.65, and dividends averaging roughly 7% a year; its page does not date those totals.[^cit] Shelterforce reports higher figures for 2024 (345 households, $767,000); treat the operator's page as controlling and the difference as a reporting-date question.[^shelterforce-cit] The same article's Lansing, Rhode Island, and crowdfunding examples are unverified leads. This is a tool for commercial displacement and resident wealth-building, not housing production, and it depends on a securities structure and a guaranteed cash-out mechanism. Any City role would need City Attorney review. César's note [[Community Investment Trusts Let Low-Income Residents Own Local Commercial Real Estate]] connects the model to the Golden Mile's commercial parcels; that application is his analysis, not a finding of the sources.

**Buyer first-claim and buyer agreements (New York City).** A Community Opportunity to Purchase Act (COPA) gives approved nonprofit buyers, such as land trusts and mission-driven developers, a first chance to buy multifamily buildings when they sell, so distressed buildings do not cycle between speculative owners. New York's Council passed a version in December 2025 that the outgoing mayor vetoed; the override was never brought up.[^copa-fail] Council Member Nurse reintroduced it with 30 sponsors and the current mayor's support, and it was laid over in committee in September 2026 (current status: verify).[^copa-nextcity] San Francisco runs a COPA program that advocates credit with preventing displacement. Without a COPA law, Brooklyn's Dukler tenant union negotiated legally binding agreements with the buyer of its buildings at auction: the new owner honors the rent strike, makes repairs, and after six years brings in help to convert the buildings to a co-op. Tenants call it a partial win because the co-op step depends on the owner following through ([[Buyer MOUs Can Bind New Owners to a Tenant Co-op Pathway]]; [[Community Opportunity to Purchase Gives Mission-Driven Buyers First Claim on Distressed Buildings]]). Maryland's right-of-first-refusal options and Frederick's authority to create one are a City Attorney question; until then, a buyer agreement is the tool tenants and nonprofits can use without new law.

## Maryland framework

**Existing land-trust law.** Maryland's 2010 Affordable Housing Land Trust Act is codified at Real Property §§14-501–14-511. It defines eligible trusts, permits agreements with repurchase or transfer restrictions, and provides for recording and enforcement. Section 14-502 says an agreement under this subtitle **is not a ground lease**. This corrects the question in César's earlier brief about whether Maryland has a land-trust-specific enabling framework. It does not determine which legal structure would be best for a particular Frederick project.[^md-landtrust]

**Cooperative law.** Maryland enacted **HB 85, Chapter 260 (2026)**, cross-filed with SB 511, concerning establishment of cooperative limited-equity housing corporations through conversion of qualifying nonstock corporations. Its stated effective date is October 1, 2026. This is a separate statute from the 2010 land-trust framework. Neither statute alone settles whether Frederick can count a CLT unit toward an MPDU obligation or transfer particular City parcels on particular terms.[^md-hb85]

The College Park trust demonstrates that an affordable housing land trust can operate in Maryland, but its institutional design and local funding package should be studied before extrapolating to Frederick.[^college-2024][^college-2025]

## Frederick policy context — attributed analysis

This section records local facts and César's questions and proposals. It does not recommend a Council vote.

- **Existing local research:** César's [[Frederick Community Land Trust -- Policy Brief]] proposes investigating City-owned land, Maryland partners, faith-owned land, housing contributions, and a governance model. These are proposals and research priorities, not enacted City policy.
- **Lucas Village:** The City's legislation index confirms that Resolutions 26-7 (rights-of-way) and 26-8 (parkland waiver) received final action on April 16, 2026. César's [[Hearing Notes 2026-04-16]] also records a 6–1 parcel-transfer vote and Nash's objection to the disposition process. The City index cited here does **not** establish the parcel-transfer instrument or its terms; do not treat the personal vote note as a verified land conveyance. Even a completed LIHTC land transfer would not itself establish a CLT policy.[^city-lucas]
- **Land disposition:** The pre-meeting [[Government Operations Committee Brief 2026-09-14]] describes a **proposed** nonprofit right of first refusal and longer public notice; it explicitly says no vote was scheduled at that workshop. This is a proposal as described in the packet-based brief, not adopted Charter text. The “RFPs for City Land” status comes from the [[Council Tracker]], not an adopted ordinance.
- **MPDU intersection:** Whether land or money contributed to a CLT can satisfy Frederick's existing MPDU requirements requires the current Chapter 19 text, applicable development agreements, and City legal analysis. The proposition in César's brief is a policy option, not a present entitlement.
- **Choices for future analysis:** A Frederick proposal would need an identified population and price target, parcel inventory and title review, cost and subsidy model, partner and board design, measurable affordability and equity goals, and a comparison with alternative uses of the same land or funds. Track tasks in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]] so the article can remain a reference rather than a campaign plan.

## Questions for future decisions and maintenance

- **Frederick legal application:** Would a specified trust structure satisfy City MPDU rules, and on what terms could the City dispose of a specified parcel? These are project-specific questions for current City code, transaction documents, and legal review.
- **Cost and outcomes:** Obtain College Park's agreement, governance documents, audited finances, and resale results before estimating a Frederick budget or comparing cost per home.
- **Evidence updates:** Revisit the 2022 census when a new census appears; add direct comparative evidence on civic participation if found. A lack of evidence for a broad claim is not evidence that its opposite is true.
- **Case-study updates:** Add completed construction, actual affordability agreements, or performance results as dated facts; keep plans labeled as plans.
