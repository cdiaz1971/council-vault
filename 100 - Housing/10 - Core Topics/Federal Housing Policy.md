# Federal Housing Policy

## Purpose

This page covers the federal laws, funding, and HUD rules that reach Frederick, and what the City can and cannot do about them. Use it when a staff report, grant, or budget request depends on federal money, when a federal rule could affect City residents, or when judging whether a federal program is real funding or only an authorization.

Federal homelessness funding and the Continuum of Care litigation are on [[Homelessness]]. SNAP is on [[Food Access and SNAP]]. LIHTC and bank investment rules are on [[Housing Finance and Preservation]].

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Authorized | Congress has created a program and set a ceiling | No money exists until Congress appropriates it |
| Appropriated | Congress has provided funds for a fiscal year | Only appropriated programs can be budgeted against |
| Continuing resolution (CR) | Temporary funding, usually at the prior year's rate | Program changes and new programs generally wait for full-year bills |
| CDBG entitlement grantee | A city that receives Community Development Block Grant funds directly from HUD by formula | Frederick is one, so CDBG-linked provisions of new law apply to it directly |
| PHA | Public housing agency | In the City, the Housing Authority of the City of Frederick (HACF) makes voucher and public housing policy choices, not the Council |

## Current baseline (as of September 29, 2026)

**The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act is law, but most of its new programs are unfunded.** It passed the Senate 85-5 on June 22, 2026 and the House 358-32 on June 23, and became Public Law 119-101 on July 11, 2026 without the President's signature.[^plaw][^latham] The law authorizes several new programs but does not fund them; funding depends on later appropriations.[^nlc]

**FY2026 HUD funding was enacted at a higher level.** The final FY2026 bill provided $77.3 billion for HUD, $7.2 billion more than FY2025, including $38.4 billion for tenant-based rental assistance, $4.4 billion for homelessness programs, and restored funding for Family Self-Sufficiency (+$16 million).[^fy26]

**FY2027 is on a continuing resolution.** P.L. 119-103, signed September 2, 2026, funds the government through **December 11, 2026**.[^cr][^crs] Full-year HUD funding, and any money for ROAD Act programs, is unresolved. Recheck before counting on any federal amount after December 11.

**HUD's work-requirement and time-limit rule is proposed, not final.** Published March 2, 2026 (RIN 2501-AE15), with comments closed May 1, it would let PHAs and owners of public housing, voucher, project-based voucher, and project-based rental assistance units adopt work requirements of up to 40 hours a week for work-eligible adults (18 to 61, with exemptions for disability, caregiving, pregnancy, and parents of children under 6) and time limits of at least two years for non-elderly, non-disabled households. Adoption would be optional for each PHA or owner, and limits would apply prospectively.[^fr] Check the Federal Register for a final rule before citing its status.

## What the ROAD to Housing Act does that matters here

| Provision | What it does | Frederick relevance | Funded? |
|---|---|---|---|
| Build Now (Sec. 213) | Links part of CDBG to housing production: bonuses for faster-growing grantees, small reductions for lagging ones[^bpc] | Applies to Frederick as a CDBG grantee; production data will affect CDBG | Formula mechanics await HUD |
| Innovation Fund (Sec. 208) | $200 million a year in competitive grants for governments that show measurable supply increases, seven-year sunset[^bpc] | A possible competitive source if Frederick can document production gains | Authorized only |
| Public land database (Sec. 104) | Requires CDBG recipients to keep a searchable public online database of undeveloped grantee-owned parcels[^bpc] | Overlaps the parcel inventory for the CLT and public land disposition items | Compliance deadline to verify |
| CDBG for new construction (Sec. 204) | Allows CDBG funds to build new affordable housing[^bpc] | Widens what the City's CDBG allocation can pay for | Uses existing CDBG |
| Pre-reviewed designs (Sec. 209) | Grants for pre-reviewed ADU, duplex, and townhouse plans, 10% reserved for rural areas[^bpc] | Matches the pre-approved plan library in [[Building Types and Codes]] | Authorized only |
| Single-stair guidelines (Sec. 102) | HUD guidelines for single-stair buildings up to six stories[^bpc] | Guidance only; Maryland controls the building code. See [[Building Types and Codes]] | Guidance |
| NEPA streamlining (Sec. 206) | Expands categorical exclusions for housing[^bpc] | Faster federal environmental review on HUD-funded projects | No cost |
| HOME reauthorization (Sec. 501) | Reauthorizes HOME, expands eligibility, allows use for housing-related infrastructure, streamlines small-project NEPA[^bpc] | Depends on how HOME reaches the City (see below) | Existing HOME appropriations |
| Manufactured housing | Expands the federal definition to include homes built without a permanent chassis[^mwc] | Relevant to defining mobile and modular units in the LMC | No cost |

**Institutional investors.** Investors that control 350 or more single-family homes are barred from buying more single-family homes, with exemptions for new construction, substantial renovation, build-to-rent, renovate-to-rent, rent-to-own programs, loss mitigation, and purchases from other large investors. Homes bought earlier need not be sold; the build-to-rent forced-sale requirement in an early Senate version was dropped. Penalties reach $1 million per violation or three times the purchase price. Treasury, with HUD, FHFA, and the SEC, may write rules. The restrictions sunset after 15 years.[^morgan][^latham] Sources differ on when the purchase bar starts: one law firm describes it as applying from enactment, another gives a January 7, 2027 effective date.[^morgan][^latham] **Verify** against the statute before citing a date. The provision is federal and enforced federally; it gives the City no new authority. See [[Corporate Landlords and Private Equity]].

## How federal housing money reaches Frederick

- **CDBG.** The City is a CDBG entitlement grantee and adopts an Annual Action Plan for each program year.[^city-cdbg] The current allocation and planned uses should be taken from the adopted Annual Action Plan; the City's site was not reachable during this review. **Verify.**
- **HOME.** Whether HOME reaches City projects directly, through Frederick County, or through Maryland DHCD is not confirmed in the vault. **Verify** before relying on HOME for a City project.
- **Rental assistance.** HACF runs public housing, Housing Choice Vouchers, and subsidized rental housing in the City.[^hacf] Frederick County also runs a separate voucher program. Unit and voucher counts should come from HACF. **Verify.**
- **Homelessness funding** flows through the Continuum of Care and HHS programs. See [[Homelessness]].

## Authority map

| Question | Who decides | City role |
|---|---|---|
| Whether ROAD Act programs get money | Congress (appropriations) | Advocacy through NLC and the congressional delegation |
| CDBG formula bonus or reduction | HUD under Sec. 213 | Production outcomes and data the City reports |
| How the City spends CDBG | City, within HUD rules | Annual Action Plan and budget |
| Work requirements or time limits for vouchers and public housing | Each PHA or owner, if the rule becomes final | The Council can ask HACF its intentions and weigh in publicly; it does not set HACF policy |
| Institutional investor purchases | Treasury and federal enforcement | None beyond local data and advocacy |
| Single-stair building code | Maryland | Advocacy; see [[Building Types and Codes]] |

## Policy debate

- **Incentive size.** Before enactment, an Urban Institute analysis summarized in the vault found the CDBG-linked bonus too small to change local behavior (a median of about $55,000 a year per recipient) and warned that new duties land on a HUD with deep staffing cuts ([[Federal Housing Bills ROAD Act and H21 -- Incentive Structures and Their Weaknesses]]). The figure predates the final Sec. 213 design and has not been checked against it. **Verify.**
- **Supply without subsidy.** Critics argue the law helps middle-income renters but does little for extremely low-income households, most of whom receive no voucher; the National Housing Law Project called it "progress but … no silver bullet" ([[21st Century ROAD to Housing Act Critique Supply Without Subsidy Won't Fix the Crisis]]). This is an advocacy position, not a measured outcome.
- **No pass-through requirement.** Most of the law's provisions cut soft costs and review time, and nothing requires those savings to reach renters or buyers. PolicyLink's Tram Hoang argues deed restrictions for ownership units and rent regulation for rentals are what would carry savings through; LISC's Mark Kudlowitz counters that time savings will let marginal projects pencil. The Innovation Fund pays localities $10,000 to $250,000 for demonstrated supply growth only if Congress appropriates the money, which it has not, and HUD staffing has fallen from about 8,800 to 5,800, which analysts doubt can support new programs.[^nextcity-road] The same question applies to any Frederick permitting reform sold as an affordability measure: what carries the savings to residents? ([[ROAD to Housing Act Savings Carry No Requirement to Reach Tenants]]; [[ROAD to Housing Act Innovation Fund Rewards Local Zoning Reform Only If Congress Funds It]])
- **Time limits.** CBPP estimated in July 2025 that a two-year time limit, applied to current households, would put 3.3 million people at risk nationally, including 1.7 million children, and 69,500 people in Maryland.[^cbpp] That estimate models a strict mandatory limit; the proposed rule is optional for each PHA, so actual exposure depends on local adoption.
- **Family Self-Sufficiency.** The administration has moved to defund FSS, citing a HUD evaluation that found no earnings gain; program rules changed during that study, which weakens the comparison ([[Family Self-Sufficiency Program -- HUD Defunding Context]]). Congress funded FSS in FY2026.[^fy26]

## Decision framework

Before the Council votes on anything that depends on federal housing policy, answer:

1. Is the federal program funded now, or only authorized? Does the funding last past December 11, 2026?
2. Is the City the grantee, or does the money flow through the County, the State, or HACF?
3. For CDBG, how will Sec. 213's production measure treat Frederick, and is the City collecting the permit and completion data it needs?
4. Does the proposal rely on a proposed federal rule that could change or be withdrawn?
5. If a federal cut or rule falls on City residents, which local lever is real (HACF, the City budget, advocacy) and which is not?

## Metrics

- CDBG allocation by program year, and any Sec. 213 bonus or reduction once HUD sets the formula
- Federal dollars committed versus spent on City housing projects
- Voucher and public housing households in the City, and any change if HACF adopts work requirements or time limits
- Competitive federal grants applied for and won (Innovation Fund, pre-reviewed designs)
- Status of the public land database required of CDBG grantees

## Council checklist

- [ ] Funding source identified and confirmed as appropriated, not just authorized
- [ ] Grantee identified (City, County, State, HACF)
- [ ] CR expiration and full-year appropriation status checked
- [ ] Any HUD rule relied on confirmed as final
- [ ] Effect on voucher and public housing households assessed
- [ ] Production data compatible with Sec. 213 reporting

## Evidence notes

The enacted statute and Federal Register notice control. Law-firm and policy-center summaries were used to locate provisions and are cited as secondary. Section numbers follow the Bipartisan Policy Center's summary of the final law. Facts to recheck before any decision are the CR status, the final status of the HUD work-requirement rule, the institutional-investor effective date, the City's CDBG allocation, and how HOME reaches the City.
