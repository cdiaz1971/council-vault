# Parking Reform

Parking reform changes how much parking development must provide, how parking is priced and shared, and how public curb space is managed. For housing, the central question is whether a requirement adds useful access or makes homes unnecessarily expensive or difficult to build.

Removing a parking minimum lets a project provide less parking; it does not require zero parking. Nor does it necessarily allow unlimited parking: maximums, site design, accessibility, and other applicable requirements can remain.

> [!info] Scope and currency
> Reviewed October 6, 2026. Local references concern the **City of Frederick**, not Frederick County's zoning code. The published LMC version displayed July 17, 2025 during direct review; the City's subsequent legislation register was also checked and still lists no parking-minimum amendment beyond G-25-14. Pending proposals are kept separate from enacted rules. Original notes and links remain at the end.

## 1. The policy tools are different

| Tool | What it changes | Main decision |
|---|---|---|
| Reduce or remove minimums | The amount a developer must provide | Which uses and locations receive relief? |
| Parking maximums | The upper limit on provision | Where would excess parking undermine other goals? |
| Shared or off-site parking | Where and when parking needs are met | Are capacity, access, and continued availability secured? |
| Unbundled pricing | Whether a space is charged separately from a home | Do residents have a genuine choice, with transparent total charges? |
| Curb management | Use of public street space | How are residents, deliveries, visitors, disabled users, and businesses served? |
| Travel-demand measures | Alternatives to storing and using a private car | Are services practical, funded, and usable at the times people need them? |

Minimums are a development requirement. Curb permits, meters, loading zones, and enforcement address the operation of public streets. A reform package may need both, but changing one does not automatically change the other.

## 2. Frederick's published requirements and existing relief

### Base residential schedule

The parking schedule in LMC §607 lists the following base ratios, before applicable reductions and modifications:[^lmc]

| Use as named in the code | Minimum vehicle spaces | Maximum vehicle spaces |
|---|---:|---:|
| Single-family | 2 per unit | Not applicable |
| Duplex | 1.5 per unit | 2.5 per unit |
| Townhouse | 2 per unit | 3 per unit |
| Two-family | 2 per unit | 3 per unit |
| Multi-family | 1.5 per unit | 2.5 per unit |
| Multi-family housing for elderly persons | 0.3 per room | 1 per room |
| Rooming house | 0.3 per room | 1 per room |

“Duplex” and “two-family” are distinct Frederick categories. Do not use them interchangeably when calculating parking; see [[Zoning Reform]] for the underlying definitions. A parking-table entry does not itself establish that a use is permitted on a particular parcel.

The published section has inconsistent table numbering: the displayed parking schedule is labeled **Table 607-1**, while some internal references call it **607-2**. Identify the schedule by its title and use row as well as its number.

The residential ADU provisions in §802(c)(1)(B) require an additional space when the primary residence's parking is at or below its required minimum. Conversion provisions can also require replacement of parking removed from the primary residence. Apply the ADU section alongside §607 rather than interpreting the schedule's accessory-use rows alone.[^adu]

### Relief already in the code

Frederick does not start from a single uniform requirement across the city:[^lmc]

- **DB and DBO districts:** §607(c)(1) generally halves the base minimum.
- **Mapped downtown exemption:** §607(c)(2) exempts qualifying new buildings or additions of 40,000 square feet or less on DB, DBO, or M1 parcels within Figure 607-1. The boundary and size conditions matter.
- **Certain smaller downtown projects:** §607(c)(3) provides additional relief tied to project size and the January 18, 2007 building or lot-of-record conditions.
- **Transit proximity:** §607(c)(4) permits a Planning Commission reduction of 25% in DB and DBO within 1,320 feet of public transit or a bus stop, subject to pedestrian-route conditions. This is not a general citywide bus-stop exemption.
- **Shared and joint-use parking:** §607(b)(8) and (d)(1) address compatible uses and different demand periods.
- **Off-site parking:** §607(d)(2) allows qualifying spaces within 1,320 feet, with capacity and continuing-access conditions. Outside DR, DB, and DBO, the credited share generally cannot exceed half the requirement.
- **Other tools:** the section includes a conditioned downtown payment-in-lieu process and a limited credit for bicycle spaces above the required amount.

A proposal should state which of these provisions it changes and which continue to apply. The 2025 ordinance titled “Concerning Parking,” G-25-14, amended the City Code's rules on stopping, standing, and parking vehicles; it was not a repeal of the LMC's development parking minimums.[^g2514]

## 3. How parking affects housing cost and delivery

Parking can affect a project through land consumption, construction, drainage and landscaping, financing, maintenance, and the layout of entrances and buildings. On a constrained parcel, the relevant cost may be a home that cannot fit or a building that cannot be reused, rather than simply the price of paving one stall.

These mechanisms do not establish a universal rent reduction. Depending on the project and market, lower requirements may permit additional homes, improve feasibility, reduce a subsidy need, improve returns, or support lower prices. The eventual distribution of savings needs evidence.

The original notes quote several surface- and structured-parking cost ranges. They are retained as historical leads, not as Frederick cost estimates. A useful local comparison should specify:

- Price date, site, parking type, and whether land is included.
- Stalls, circulation aisles, ramps, structures, drainage, and accessible routes.
- Required versus voluntarily supplied spaces.
- Changes in dwelling count, bedroom mix, and usable open space.
- Financing and operating costs, not just construction.
- Any enforceable affordability commitment and the full resident charge.

**Illustration, not a project forecast:** at a base ratio of 1.5 spaces per home, 20 homes require 30 spaces before relief. A ratio of 1 requires 20. Removing the minimum lets the project propose an appropriate amount; it does not demonstrate that all 30 spaces disappear or that the savings reduce rents.

### What the research establishes

Hess and Rehler studied 36 major developments in the first two years after Buffalo's 2017 repeal. Forty-seven percent supplied less parking than the former rules required; mixed-use projects supplied 53% less. Single-use projects collectively supplied more than their former minimums. The response therefore varied by project type.[^buffalo]

This supports the proposition that minimums can bind some projects while developers continue supplying parking voluntarily elsewhere. The study is not a Frederick rent forecast or proof that repeal alone produces a particular number of homes. Evaluate completed and occupied housing separately from approvals and proposed parking reductions.

## 4. Maryland's transit law: scope and timing

HB 894 became Chapter 592 of 2026. Its relevant land-use provisions took effect **October 1, 2026**. Subject to exceptions, it prohibits minimum off-street parking requirements for residential or mixed-use development within 0.25 mile of a rail station averaging at least hourly service between 8 a.m. and 6 p.m., Monday–Friday.[^md] A post-effective-date tally counts roughly 98 qualifying rail stations, all in Montgomery, Prince George's, Anne Arundel, and Baltimore Counties and Baltimore City; no Frederick County station is on that list.[^ggwash]

The enacted text excludes campus stations, parcels partly outside the applicable radius, and areas zoned single-family residential on January 1, 2026. It also permits a minimum supported by an adequate local parking study completed within three years before application, with MDOT concurrence; no MDOT decision within 45 days counts as concurrence. A related mixed-use provision uses a half-mile radius.[^md]

**The statutory test is station service, not a fixed list of named lines.** The earlier page's rail-line list was an interpretation of service patterns, not the wording of the law.

Frederick and Monocacy are Brunswick Line stations. MTA's published schedule continues to show commuter service, not the statutory all-day frequency. The September 28 Brunswick–Camden through service is a weekday round trip and does not establish hourly service at Frederick or Monocacy. With the preemption now in force, it still does not supply relief at those stations; future coverage should be assessed against the actual schedule and parcel conditions.[^marc]

Frederick can consider local reform without waiting for a station to qualify.

## 5. Comparative reforms: what was actually adopted

These examples offer different designs for reform. Their adoption establishes a legal change, not a measured housing outcome.

| Jurisdiction | Verified approach | Lesson for Frederick |
|---|---|---|
| Washington | ESSB 5184 combines caps on required parking with exemptions for defined uses; it includes population, safety, and other limitations and phased compliance. | Use-based relief can operate without making every exemption depend on transit proximity. |
| Dallas | May 2025 reform removed many requirements and lowered others. Multifamily thresholds retain requirements for larger developments outside applicable exemptions. | Describe the actual categories rather than calling it total repeal. |
| North Carolina | SL 2026-39 prohibits minimum off-street lot counts, with coastal-area exceptions; the parking provision takes effect January 1, 2027. | Broad relief can coexist with other site regulations. |
| Asheville | Ordinance 5257, adopted August 25, 2026, removes minimums for all development while retaining simplified maximums. | Minimums and maximums are separate policy choices. |
| Illinois | The People Over Parking Act restricts minimums near defined transit hubs and corridors, subject to statutory exceptions. | A geographic model needs precise definitions and mapping. |

**Washington details.** Covered jurisdictions generally may not require more than 0.5 space per multifamily home or one per single-family home. Exemptions include residences under 1,200 square feet, commercial spaces under 3,000 square feet, affordable and senior housing, qualifying childcare, ground-level nonresidential space in mixed-use buildings, and specified changes of use. Accessible parking is separately protected. Cities of 30,000 or fewer are excluded; cities over 50,000 must adopt compliant regulations by January 2027, with a later deadline for the 30,000-50,000 tier.[^wa] Renton and Kirkland are both running compliance projects under this deadline; Kirkland adopted its implementing ordinance, O-4935, with an effective date of April 18, 2026, showing the law operating through local code amendments rather than taking direct effect on its own.[^kirkland]

**Dallas details.** The City's summary lists no minimum for multifamily developments of up to 20 homes, half a space per home for 21–199 homes, and one per home for 200 or more, alongside transit, downtown, and other exemptions. Loading and design requirements remain. The senior-housing anecdote in the retained clipping is an illustration, not a citywide impact evaluation.[^dallas]

**North Carolina details.** The parking prohibition generally exempts coastal-area local governments, with specified historic properties still covered. The law also changes stormwater regulation and authorizes voluntary incentives for additional treatment. Those are separate provisions with different effective dates; it should not be summarized simply as requiring greener parking lots.[^nc]

**Asheville details.** Council minutes confirm unanimous approval of the parking amendment and retention of maximums and bicycle requirements. This is broader than the earlier page's description of residential-only removal.[^asheville]

**Illinois details.** The core prohibition took effect June 1, 2026 and applies within half a mile of a defined transit hub or one-eighth mile of a qualifying transit corridor. The act preserves local regulation of on-street parking, maximums, and bicycle parking, and includes an exception for certain existing agreements and approved plans. Its definitions determine coverage; a nearby bus stop alone is insufficient.[^illinois] A separate bill, HB2335, passed both General Assembly chambers on May 31, 2026 and would narrow the Act's coverage by amending its definitions; whether the Governor has signed it is unconfirmed (verify before relying on the current scope of the Illinois model).[^illinois-hb2335]

## 6. Accessibility, spillover, and distribution of costs

The following are recommended evaluation criteria, not findings that Frederick already has a particular shortage.

### Accessibility and essential travel

A general parking ratio is not a substitute for an accessibility plan. The U.S. Access Board explains that accessible-space scoping depends on the facility, including special residential provisions, and that access aisles and accessible routes are integral to usable parking.[^access]

A reform should identify the applicable ADA, Fair Housing, and state-code obligations and preserve them explicitly. It should also examine accessible passenger loading, paratransit, caregivers, service workers, and people whose work hours are poorly served by transit. “Senior housing” does not mean every resident can walk to a bus stop.

### Spillover and curb management

Where a development supplies fewer spaces than residents and visitors seek, demand may move onto nearby streets. The response should start with observed occupancy, turnover, loading activity, and unsafe or illegal parking at representative times.

Potential tools include time limits, pricing, loading zones, permits, shared facilities, wayfinding, and better pedestrian connections. Specify administration, enforcement, and review triggers. Permit eligibility should address renters, new residents, visitors, and caregivers rather than treating existing homeowners as the only legitimate users.

San Francisco's SFpark pilot evaluation provides an example of measuring search time and availability under active parking management. It evaluates a package of pricing, information, and other changes; it is not evidence that minimum repeal alone resolves congestion.[^sfpark]

### Affordability and unbundling

Separate parking charges can give a household without a car a choice about purchasing a space. But a lower advertised rent paired with mandatory fees is not meaningful unbundling. Assess total housing-plus-parking cost for households with and without vehicles, and any treatment of accessible spaces or reasonable accommodations.

Likewise, public spending on garages should be shown alongside any private development savings. If public facilities absorb demand, estimate operating and capital costs and identify who pays.

## 7. Parking, stormwater, and urban form

EPA describes how paving, limited vegetation, and heat-absorbing surfaces contribute to urban heat, and how vegetation and permeable surfaces can support cooling and runoff reduction. Green infrastructure also requires inspection and maintenance.[^environment]

Less required parking can create room for homes, trees, or stormwater facilities. The environmental result depends on what is actually built: replacing a lot with a building does not automatically reduce total impervious cover.

For a site comparison, measure total impervious area, tree retention and canopy, drainage treatment, pedestrian routes, and maintenance responsibility. Parking quantity, surface design, and building form should be evaluated together. See [[Frederick Water, Sewer, and Stormwater]] and [[Frederick Urbanism and Placemaking]] for the broader frameworks.

## 8. Frederick context and a Council decision framework

The Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force recommends reducing parking minimums for affordable housing in Strategy 2.2, printed page 16. This is a policy recommendation, not an enacted change to §607.[^taskforce]

The Himes Avenue meeting note records a request to reduce a proposed affordable project's ratio from 1.5 to 1.25 spaces per home. César reported on September 24, 2026 that the variance was granted. Retain that as an attributed local case, not as proof that all affordable developments have identical demand or that the written approval contains no other conditions. See [[Himes Ave -- Conifer LIHTC Development Meeting Notes (2026-03-16)]].

Pending ADU provisions and César's proposed LMC amendments belong in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]]. A draft's transit exemption or proposed parking dimensions should not be quoted as current law.

For a concrete proposal, compare the following choices:

| Choice | Potential advantage | Main tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| Relief for affordable housing, small homes, ADUs, or small projects | Focuses on defined housing objectives | Eligibility thresholds can create complexity or exclude similar projects. |
| Geographic relief | Coordinates reform with walkable areas and transportation options | Boundary choices and service quality require justification. |
| Broad removal of minimums | Gives flexibility across projects and reduces exceptions | Requires a deliberate approach to curb demand, accessibility, and public costs. |

A decision-ready staff analysis should provide:

1. **A clear legal change:** affected uses, districts, tables, exceptions, and remaining maximums.
2. **Representative site comparisons:** current-rule and reform scenarios using the same assumptions, with homes, parking, cost, and site impacts.
3. **Access and equity measures:** disabled users, car-free households, workers who need vehicles, renters, deliveries, and nearby businesses.
4. **A management plan:** responsible department, budget, implementation date, and response to documented parking problems.
5. **An outcome review:** actual parking built and used, housing completed, total resident charges, curb occupancy, safety/access complaints, and public expenditure.

Report approvals separately from construction and occupancy. Compare outcomes across project types and neighborhoods, and record concurrent zoning, financing, or transit changes before attributing results to parking reform.
