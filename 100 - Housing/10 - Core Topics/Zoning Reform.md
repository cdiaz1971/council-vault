# Zoning Reform

Zoning reform changes the rules governing permitted uses, building intensity and form, and the process for obtaining development approval. For housing policy, the central question is whether those changes allow useful homes to be built in places people need them, with manageable public costs and protections for affected residents.

Zoning permission, project feasibility, completed housing, and affordability are different outcomes. A reform can increase legal capacity without immediately producing construction. New construction can ease market pressure without providing a home affordable to a household with very low income. Those distinctions determine which companion policies are needed.

> [!info] Scope and currency
> Reviewed September 27, 2026. This article separates research findings, enacted rules, dated proposals, and analytical recommendations. Local examples concern the **City of Frederick**, not Frederick County's separate zoning code. Original research links are retained at the end.

## 1. What zoning reform changes

| Instrument | What changes | Practical test |
|---|---|---|
| Use permissions | Housing types or residential uses allowed in a district | Does the intended building fit a permitted code category? |
| Lot size and frontage | Land required for each lot | Can existing parcels be divided into viable lots with access and utilities? |
| Density, height, setbacks, lot coverage, and floor area | The building envelope and number of homes | Do the standards work together, or does one cancel the others? |
| Parking and access | Land and construction devoted to vehicles | Can access, curb management, and mobility needs be met without making the housing infeasible? |
| Approval procedure | Who decides, against which standards, with what discretion | Is the route predictable, adequately staffed, and proportionate to the project? |
| Affordability conditions and incentives | Required restricted homes, bonuses, or contributions | What can be delivered at each income level after accounting for costs and available subsidy? |

**Upzoning** generally increases permitted development intensity. **Missing-middle reform** enables smaller multi-unit forms between detached houses and large apartment buildings. **By-right approval** means a conforming use does not require a discretionary rezoning or special permission for that use; it does not eliminate permits, objective standards, or applicable safety and environmental review.

A useful analysis tests complete development scenarios. Changing a unit limit alone may achieve little if setbacks, parking, lot coverage, or construction costs prevent a viable building.

## 2. What the evidence supports

### Zoning reform can increase supply; effects vary

A 2023 study by Stacy and colleagues examined 180 land-use reforms across 1,136 cities in eight metropolitan regions. Loosening restrictions was associated with a **0.8% increase in housing stock within three to nine years**, concentrated toward the higher end of the rent distribution. The study did not find statistically significant evidence of more or cheaper lower-cost units over that interval. This is a measured association across the studied reforms, not an annual production rate or a forecast for Frederick.[^urban]

Greenaway-McGrevy and Phillips studied Auckland's 2016 reform, which upzoned approximately three quarters of its residential land. Their quasi-experimental analysis found strong evidence of increased construction, including after accounting for possible shifts in construction between areas. This supports the possibility of a meaningful supply response to broad reform; it does not establish an equivalent response to every small local amendment.[^auckland]

### Additional construction can moderate nearby rents

Asquith, Mast, and Reed's working paper on large new market-rate apartment buildings in low-income areas estimated nearby rents **5–7% lower relative to comparison locations**. This means rents were lower than the estimated counterfactual, not necessarily that every tenant's nominal rent fell. It studies completed buildings, not zoning enactments, and does not establish that all individual tenants avoid displacement.[^apartments]

### Land values and housing affordability are not the same measure

Freemark's review of zoning-change research finds mixed short-term construction responses and stronger evidence of longer-term increases. Development rights can raise the value of parcels suitable for redevelopment. That can coexist with additional homes and lower rents than would otherwise occur. Evidence on neighborhood demographics is mixed and depends on market conditions and reform scale.[^review]

The policy implication is to evaluate **land value, homes delivered, rents, and resident outcomes separately**. A rise in the sale price of an upzoned parcel alone neither proves nor disproves a housing-affordability benefit.

### How to use this evidence

For a Council proposal, specify the expected mechanism and time horizon. Pair production reforms with direct affordability measures when the objective includes households the market will not serve at affordable rents. See [[Housing Finance and Preservation]] and [[Housing Affordability and Inequality]].

The original news examples—expensive starter homes, a monthly decline in starts, or a single approved building—remain in the research trail. They are illustrations of conditions or events. They do not isolate the causal effect of zoning from financing costs, demand, infrastructure, or other policy changes.

## 3. Frederick: read definitions, permissions, and dimensions together

### Code terminology matters

Frederick's published LMC definitions distinguish:

- **Duplex dwelling:** two units on adjoining, separate lots, divided by a vertical party wall.
- **Two-family dwelling:** two units on one lot, stacked or side by side.
- **Multiple-family dwelling:** a building containing three or more dwelling units.[^definitions]

Consequently, a proposal to allow “duplexes” must state whether it means the City's separate-lot category, two units on one lot, or both. Likewise, a triplex or fourplex may fall within the multifamily definition even when it has no separately named dimensional-table row. Absence of a separate label does not establish that the form is prohibited.

Use the **§404 use matrix**, **§405 dimensional standards**, **§1002 definitions**, applicable overlays, and subsequent ordinances together. A dimensional row alone is not an authorization for a use; a permitted use alone is not proof that it can fit.

### The Housing Policy Baseline

[[Frederick Housing Policy Baseline]] summarizes the LMC and Chapter 19 from the March 2025 code supplement, with the 2026 state and local changes noted. Its R4/R6 single-family minimum-lot figures—8,000 and 7,000 square feet—also appear in the City's older Article 4 PDF. Neither the older PDF nor the vault summary substitutes for a current parcel determination.[^baseline]

The prior article's categorical statement that multifamily is prohibited in R8 is not retained: the material reviewed does not adequately establish that conclusion, and the City's older dimensional table contains an R8 multifamily row. The revised article therefore does not reproduce that summary as a current permission matrix.

For a particular amendment, attach a before-and-after table drawn from the controlling code and identify each changed use, lot, density, and dimensional provision. The City's code hub links the published LMC and recently adopted ordinances.[^codehub]

### Vertical mixed use: an enacted change with a specific threshold

**Ordinance G-26-10** was approved August 20, 2026 and took effect August 30. It allows first-floor residential uses in certain mixed-use buildings in MU-1 and MU-2, subject to the ordinance's standards. The adopted §838(d)(1) requires the building's **total first-floor area to be at least 35,000 square feet**.[^mixed]

This is more limited than a general permission for ground-floor housing in every mixed-use building. When applying the example to commercial corridors, test the actual building and district against the adopted text.

## 4. ADUs and Maryland's requirements

An accessory dwelling unit adds a secondary home associated with a primary dwelling. It can serve family members, renters, or caregivers, but permission does not ensure that a homeowner can finance construction.

Maryland's **2025 HB1466** took effect October 1, 2025 and requires compliant local ADU laws by **October 1, 2026**. The Department of Planning's guidance explains that ADUs must be excluded from density and applicable residential-growth limits. Additional parking requirements require a study and a waiver process; side and rear setbacks cannot exceed those for accessory structures. The law does **not** require every local approval pathway to be by right.[^adu-state]

MDP interprets the state definition as requiring local authorization up to **75% of the primary detached home's size**, rather than allowing a lower universal size cap. This is agency guidance relevant to reviewing a proposed local cap; distinguish it from the wording and status of a City draft.[^adu-state]

For Frederick's **PC25-614ZTA**, the March 9, 2026 staff report identifies proposed changes to §802 and states that no detached ADUs had been **successfully constructed by permit since the 2021 amendment**. It does not say that no permits had been issued. The proposal was subsequently described as being at workshop in [[ADU Ordinance Brief 2026-08-13]] and the prior page's September 24 status record; those dated records are not an adoption certificate.[^adu-local] HB1466's compliance deadline for local ADU laws passed October 1, 2026. The City's published pending/adopted-legislation tracker shows no ADU ordinance, case number, or LMC §802 amendment as of this review; it lists no action on PC25-614ZTA at all. This review could not confirm whether the City enacted a compliant ADU ordinance by the deadline, adopted one under a different case number, or missed it. *Verify current status directly with the Planning Department before citing Frederick as compliant or noncompliant.*

Evaluate an ADU policy on legal capacity, usable designs, financing, utility connections, approval cost, rental use, and completed units. Preserve the distinction between state compliance and optional local choices that further facilitate production.

## 5. Housing Certainty Act: which rules apply, and when?

Maryland's **Chapter 591 of 2026**, effective October 1, 2026, separates two protections:

1. **Application-stage rules:** subject to the statute, review is governed by laws in effect when a substantially complete application is submitted.
2. **Post-approval vesting:** after all required approvals, authorized use and development receive protection for the longer of five years or the locally determined period.

The Act includes procedural requirements, phased-project provisions, and exceptions. It does not confer an unconditional five-year building entitlement merely because any application is on file.[^certainty-state]

Frederick's **G-26-11** is an interim, uncodified implementation ordinance. Its official document records introduction on **September 3** and approval on **September 17, 2026**. It states that state law controls in a conflict. The City's summary tracker lists September 3 under final action; the ordinance itself resolves that date discrepancy.[^certainty-local]

For zoning changes, the decision question is which applications and approvals are affected by the transition provisions. Address that explicitly in the implementation analysis rather than assuming every pending project either changes rules or is fully exempt.

## 6. Compare reform options as packages

The following is a decision framework, not a finding that each option is already authorized or adopted in Frederick.

| Option | Potential benefit | Main design tradeoff or dependency |
|---|---|---|
| Smaller lots and more homes per residential parcel | More choices in established neighborhoods | Coordinate density, frontage, subdivision, setbacks, access, and utility capacity |
| Small multifamily by right | A predictable route for modest apartment buildings | Test fire/building requirements, accessibility, financing, and usable floor plans |
| Size-for-units incentives | Makes several smaller homes more attractive relative to one large replacement | Model whether the added floor area outweighs extra construction and operating costs |
| Housing on commercial parcels | Reuses accessible sites and supports corridor activity | Assess contamination, infrastructure, business displacement, retail demand, and safe walking routes |
| Faith-owned or nonprofit land pathways | Connects mission-driven landowners with housing opportunities | Establish eligible owners, affordability, stewardship, financing, and land-control terms |
| Adaptive reuse of offices or schools | Reuses structures and existing locations | Test floor depth, daylight, egress, accessibility, structural condition, and conversion cost |
| Objective standards and administrative adjustments | Reduces avoidable delay for compliant or constrained infill | Define discretion, appeal routes, transparency, and staff capacity |
| Household-definition reform | Expands options for shared living | Separate occupancy and safety standards from assumptions about family relationships; evaluate the actual local text |
| Inclusionary requirements with incentives or subsidy | Produces income-restricted homes within development | Balance income depth, share, duration, feasibility, and public cost |

The archived Denver material is a design lead for size-for-units incentives, not a verified estimate of what that model would produce in Frederick. Likewise, a proposal in another state is not evidence of Maryland legal authority.

### Minimum lot size: a statewide ballot test

Massachusetts Question 7, an initiative statute on the **November 3, 2026** ballot, would bar municipalities from requiring more than **5,000 square feet** of lot area or **50 feet** of frontage for a single-family home on lots served by public water and sewer. Cities and towns would keep control of height, setbacks, open space, parking, and short-term rentals, and the Executive Office of Housing and Livable Communities would oversee compliance. The measure reached the ballot after the Legislature did not act by its May 2026 deadline. The Massachusetts Municipal Association opposes it on local-control grounds.[^ma-q7]

It is **pending, not enacted**, and has no effect in Maryland. The Frederick comparison is the LMC's own minimum lot areas in R4 and R6 (8,000 and 7,000 square feet in the dated baseline), which are larger than the proposed Massachusetts cap.[^baseline] Recheck the result after November 3 before citing it.

### Faith-owned land: what Virginia actually enacted

Virginia Code §15.2-2288.9 establishes a qualifying by-right pathway effective **January 1, 2027 through January 1, 2031**. It has substantive conditions: five years of qualifying ownership, at least 60% affordable units with rental and ownership income limits, and at least 30 years of restrictions. Infrastructure, environmental, historic, and other requirements remain; industrial-location and other exclusions apply.[^virginia]

This is an enacted but not-yet-effective comparative model at the review date. It is not a blanket right for every religious parcel and does not apply in Maryland. A Frederick counterpart should specify the eligible parcels, public benefit, and enforceable obligations.

### Inclusionary zoning: requirements and feasibility belong together

Frederick's published Chapter 19 generally applies at **25 or more homes at one location**, subject to exceptions, and calculates the MPDU obligation at **12.5%**, with rounding rules. Section 19-6.1 permits construction, payment in lieu, or a combination. It establishes a **$2 per-square-foot fee as of November 2, 2023**, and a recalculation process after three years of the specified data have been obtained. That wording is not an automatic November 2026 fee increase.[^mpdu]

Baltimore provides a comparison in which requirements and fiscal support are paired. Its code imposes affordability requirements on qualifying projects of at least 20 homes receiving a major public subsidy or significant land-use authorization, with other conditions and exclusions. Its separate high-performance inclusionary tax credit is based on the actual rent differential for affordable units, but is limited by property tax liability and eligibility requirements.[^baltimore]

For Frederick, model market-rate production, restricted units, rents, density bonuses, fee alternatives, and subsidy together. A nominal density bonus has little value when the resulting building cannot fit or obtain financing. A tax credit also represents foregone public revenue, so show its fiscal cost alongside the housing benefit.

## 7. Displacement, inclusion, and public participation

Distinguish three questions:

- **Direct displacement:** Will occupied homes be demolished or residents required to move?
- **Economic pressure:** How might rents, property costs, or redevelopment incentives affect residents and small businesses?
- **Access:** Who can move into the new homes, and who remains excluded by price, eligibility, or location?

Average supply benefits do not answer every household-level question. Conversely, one expensive replacement project does not establish the regional effect of allowing more homes. Evaluate both scales.

A proposal should identify existing occupied units, rents and restrictions where available, relocation needs, and opportunities for acquisition or preservation before redevelopment. Consider enforceable affordability and long-term stewardship alongside zoning changes. [[Tenant Protections]] and [[Community Land Trusts and Social Ownership]] describe companion approaches.

For participation, include renters, prospective residents, people with disabilities, small builders, and affected businesses as well as nearby homeowners. Ask participants to compare feasible alternatives and their consequences. Record whose needs each alternative serves and whose costs it increases.

The original historical notes are retained. New York City's own industrial-land-use history links the 1916 zoning framework to efforts to separate factories from high-end retail areas. That supports treating zoning as a distributional choice as well as a technical system; it does not establish a single origin or motive for every later zoning provision.[^history]

## 8. Fiscal and environmental evaluation

EPA's smart-growth work identifies potential infrastructure and service efficiencies from compact development and higher revenue per acre. These are reasons to examine infill, not proof that a particular Frederick project requires no additional public investment.[^fiscal]

For a fiscal comparison, include capital upgrades, operating services, lifecycle maintenance, fee timing, tax incentives, and which government receives revenue or bears costs. Existing pipes and roads are assets, but their presence does not establish spare capacity or adequate condition. Revenue per acre is informative; net public cost and service obligations still matter.

EPA also identifies potential benefits from compact development, transportation choices, and reduced land consumption.[^environment] For a project or zoning district, evaluate location, travel, building size and performance, tree cover, stormwater, and reuse of existing structures.

The previous page's universal **82% energy-efficiency** comparison and city-to-city lane-mile/tax-yield figures are not used as general decision facts here. Comparable units, geography, time periods, and methods are essential before transferring such numbers to Frederick.

## 9. A Council decision record

Use this page as the reference foundation, then prepare a proposal-specific record:

1. **Problem and beneficiaries:** Identify the housing need and households the change is intended to serve.
2. **Exact rule change:** Attach the affected code sections and a before-and-after scenario on representative parcels.
3. **Expected delivery:** Separate newly legal capacity from feasible projects, starts, completions, and net additions.
4. **Affordability and resident effects:** Specify income levels, restrictions, existing occupied homes, and companion protections.
5. **Infrastructure and fiscal impact:** Identify upgrades, responsible parties, public subsidy, and recurring obligations.
6. **Administration and evaluation:** Assign ownership, define the approval route and transition rules, and set review dates.

Monitor permits, starts, completions, housing type and size, restricted affordability, demolished homes, review times, and resident outcomes. Use the counting framework in [[Housing Production Strategies]].
