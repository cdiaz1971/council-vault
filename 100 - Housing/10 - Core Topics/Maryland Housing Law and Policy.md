# Maryland Housing Law and Policy

## Purpose

This page explains which Maryland housing measures are binding law, which are executive-branch benchmarks, which proposals failed, and what each means for City of Frederick decisions.

The practical rule is: **identify the legal instrument, confirm its final text and effective date, map it to Frederick's code and procedures, and separate legal requirements from policy interpretation.** Bill summaries, testimony, press releases, and introduced text are useful background but do not replace enacted chapter text.

## Frederick quick reference

| **Short-Term Rental Safety Act** | HB 1221/SB 624 became law; effective October 1, 2026 | Sets a statewide life-safety floor for short-term rentals; Frederick's Rental Licensing Ordinance rewrite should confirm whether short-term rentals are currently licensed or tracked at all |

## How to read Maryland housing policy

| Instrument | Legal effect | Source to cite |
|---|---|---|
| **Enacted statute** | Binding after its effective date; may preempt or constrain local law | Chapter text and codified Maryland Code |
| **Executive order** | Directs executive-branch agencies within the governor's authority | Signed executive order and implementing agency materials |
| **Agency target or guidance** | A benchmark, funding criterion, interpretation, or administrative practice; effect depends on authorizing law | Final agency report, regulations, or program rules |
| **Local ordinance** | Binding within local authority and subject to state and federal law | Signed ordinance and current local code |
| **Introduced or failed bill** | No current legal effect | Final MGA bill-status page, retained for legislative history |
| **Fiscal note** | Nonbinding legislative analysis of operations, costs, and existing law | Final revised Department of Legislative Services note |
| **Advocacy or press account** | Evidence of a position or political argument | Cite as perspective, not as the law |

This distinction matters because Maryland uses strong state-level land-use rules alongside local planning and zoning authority. A policy can be locally desirable yet require state enabling legislation; a state law can also remove or condition local discretion.

## Housing Starts Here

Governor Wes Moore signed Executive Order 01.01.2025.19 on September 3, 2025. It directs executive agencies to identify suitable state land, improve state permitting and funding processes, coordinate transit-oriented housing, establish production targets, provide a state housing ombudsman, and create incentives for local action. [DHCD: Housing Starts Here Executive Order](https://dhcd.maryland.gov/policy-and-legislation/housing-starts-here-executive-order)

### Frederick production target

DHCD's January 2026 report assigns the City of Frederick these annual targets:

| Year | Target units |
|---:|---:|
| 2026 | 824 |
| 2027 | 931 |
| 2028 | 1,039 |
| 2029 | 1,146 |
| 2030 | 1,254 |
| **2026–2030** | **5,194** |

The allocation uses Frederick's estimated 34,181 housing units—32 percent of Frederick County's housing stock—to assign the city a proportional share of the county target. The target report describes the numbers as common benchmarks for measuring progress and informing policy. It does not establish a statutory quota or automatic penalty for missing the target. The executive order instead links local performance and pro-housing policies to recognition and potential bonus points in DHCD funding competitions. [DHCD: 2030 Housing Production Targets](https://dhcd.maryland.gov/media/1204) · [DHCD: Maryland Housing Leadership Awards](https://dhcd.maryland.gov/policy-and-legislation/housing-starts-here-executive-order/maryland-housing-leadership-awards)

### What the target measures—and misses

DHCD intends to track progress primarily through the U.S. Census Bureau Building Permits Survey. Permits are consistent and timely, but a permit is not a completed or occupied home. Reporting gaps, demolitions, project delays, affordability, bedroom mix, accessibility, location, and displacement can change the policy meaning of the raw count.

The municipal method also assumes future growth should follow the municipality's existing share of county housing stock. DHCD identifies that as a limitation because municipal boundaries, growth areas, infrastructure, and permitting patterns differ.

For Frederick, a council dashboard should therefore report:

- permits issued, units started, certificates of occupancy, and net units added;
- income-restricted units by AMI tier and affordability term;
- market-rate units by type and bedroom count;
- location relative to transit, jobs, schools, utilities, and displacement risk;
- demolitions, conversions, and expiring affordability restrictions; and
- progress against both the DHCD benchmark and locally adopted affordability goals.

Production and affordability should be reported together. DHCD itself states that greater supply is necessary but insufficient for the lowest-income households and calls for subsidies, dedicated affordable housing, and renter protections.

## Maryland Housing Certainty Act

HB 548/SB 325 became Chapters 591 and 590 of the 2026 Laws of Maryland. The law takes effect October 1, 2026. [MGA: HB 548 / Chapter 591](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/hb0548?ys=2026RS) · [Chapter 591 text](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_591_hb0548E.pdf)

### The two timing rules

The law creates two related but distinct protections:

1. **Rules fixed at application.** Approval, conditional approval, or denial must be governed by the laws and regulations in effect when a housing-development application is determined to be substantially complete. When a project requires multiple application stages, the relevant date can be the first qualifying application in a process that may culminate in project completion. A zoning text amendment or rezoning application does not qualify as that first process.
2. **Vested right after approvals.** After the project receives all required approvals, the proponent receives a vested right to the authorized use and development for at least five years, or longer if the local authority provides a longer period.

The former concept page compressed these into “vesting starts at complete application.” That was inaccurate. **The regulatory snapshot occurs at the qualifying application; statutory vesting occurs after all required approvals.**

### Completeness procedure

Under the enacted text:

- a complete application includes the materials and information required for processing and substantive review, while allowing inconsequential deficiencies;
- the local authority generally has 30 days after receipt to determine completeness and notify the applicant;
- if incomplete, the notice must list deficiencies and provide a reasonable cure period; and
- failure to notify within 35 days results in the application being deemed substantially complete for this purpose.

Each qualifying phase of a phased development is treated as a discrete project. The law preserves the ability to require phase-specific approvals, enforce health and safety rules addressing immediate threats, use development-rights agreements, and approve later density increases.

### Development fees

Chapter 591 also changes when covered development excise taxes and impact fees may be collected for residential projects. It generally defers collection until construction is complete and occupancy requirements are met, while allowing collection as a condition of final inspection up to 30 days before the inspection. An exception applies to certain fees financing county debt incurred on or before January 1, 2026. The final text should be used when determining whether a particular City fee and project are covered.

### Frederick implementation

The City approved Ordinance G-26-11 on September 3, 2026, concerning the Maryland Housing Certainty Act. The City's official legislation page corrects the former page, which listed September 17. [City of Frederick: Pending and Recently Adopted Legislation](https://www.cityoffrederickmd.gov/1689/Pending-and-Recently-Adopted-Legislation) · [Ordinance G-26-11](https://www.cityoffrederickmd.gov/DocumentCenter/View/27948/G-26-11-Concerning-the-Maryland-Housing-Certainty-Act-)

Implementation questions for staff and counsel include:

- Which City application is the first qualifying application for each approval path?
- What checklist establishes completeness, and who makes the determination?
- How are the submission date, determination date, governing code version, approvals, phases, and expiration dates recorded?
- Which fee provisions changed, and what cash-flow or capital-program effects follow?
- How will applicants, boards, and the public see which code version governs a project?

## LMC review and development timing

The Housing Certainty Act creates a real timing issue for the Land Management Code review, but the analysis must use the law's two separate triggers.

- A project with a qualifying substantially complete application before an LMC amendment may be reviewed under the earlier rules even though it has not yet obtained all approvals.
- A rezoning or text-amendment request by itself does not establish the statutory regulatory snapshot.
- Once all required approvals are obtained, the authorized use and development receive the statutory vesting period.
- Later code changes can therefore have different effects on unfiled projects, incomplete applications, qualifying complete applications, approved projects, and separate future phases.

For any contemplated moratorium or transition rule, request a written project-stage matrix from the City Attorney. It should identify which pending applications are covered, whether the local action is authorized, how state and constitutional limits apply, and what exceptions are required. Political concern about a rush to file is plausible, but the effect on a particular project is a legal determination rather than an automatic consequence.

The operational response is to complete the LMC review on a defined schedule, publish transition rules, and maintain an auditable application log. That reduces uncertainty for applicants, residents, staff, and the Council.

## Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act

HB 894/SB 389 became Chapters 592 and 593 and takes effect October 1, 2026. The law addresses enterprise-zone designation, state infrastructure financing, local land-use rules near qualifying rail stations, state transportation land, and the timing of certain fees. [MGA: HB 894 / Chapter 592](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/HB0894?ys=2026RS) · [Chapter 592 text](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/chapters_noln/Ch_592_hb0894E.pdf)

Relevant land-use provisions apply around a rail station that receives at least hourly service on average from 8:00 a.m. through 6:00 p.m., Monday through Friday:

- within 0.25 miles, a local authority generally may not impose residential or mixed-use parking minimums unless it completes an adequate parking study and MDOT concurs;
- within 0.5 miles, local zoning must allow mixed-use development on qualifying residential or commercial land; and
- special provisions apply to transportation land owned by the state, Baltimore City, or WMATA when there is an MDOT-approved transit-oriented-development plan coordinated with the locality.

The law contains property, zoning, institutional-campus, infrastructure, safety, and other qualifications. Frederick and Brunswick MARC locations should not be classified from the station name alone. Apply the statutory service-frequency test to the current timetable, map the distance boundary parcel by parcel, and then test each exception.

## Short-Term Rental Safety Act

The Jillian and Lindsay Wiener Short-Term Rental Safety Act, HB 1221/SB 624, took effect October 1, 2026. It requires every short-term rental in Maryland to have working smoke detectors, carbon monoxide alarms, and fire extinguishers, plus posted evacuation routes and emergency numbers. [MGA: HB 1221](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/HB1221?ys=2026RS) · [MGA: SB 624](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/SB0624?ys=2026RS)

Before this law, a whole-unit short-term rental could fall between residential rental licensing and commercial lodging fire code and be covered by neither. The act closes that gap at the state level for basic life-safety equipment, but it creates a practical question for Frederick: the Rental Licensing Ordinance rewrite in committee should confirm whether the City currently licenses or registers short-term rentals at all, and if so, whether its inspection checklist already covers these items. If Frederick does not track short-term rentals, the state mandate exists with no local mechanism to know which properties are subject to it. A registration requirement justified on life-safety grounds would also give the City a count of units converted from long-term to short-term use, which bears on the housing-supply and production-target questions in [[Housing Production Strategies]]. [Maryland Matters, "New laws: No Kings, no trying youths as adults, no 'dynamic pricing' and a prehistoric shark," October 1, 2026](https://marylandmatters.org/2026/10/01/new-laws-no-kings-no-trying-youths-as-adults-no-dynamic-pricing-and-a-prehistoric-shark/)

## State and local roles

| Policy area | Maryland role | Frederick role |
|---|---|---|
| Housing targets | Establish statewide methodology, reporting, and incentives | Track permits and completions; set local affordability and location goals |
| Land use | Set statewide limits, mandates, and enabling authority | Adopt zoning, subdivision, design, parking, and process rules within state law |
| Development review | Establish statutory timing and vesting rules | Maintain applications, completeness review, hearings, approvals, and transition records |
| Infrastructure finance | Authorize taxes and fees and regulate collection timing | Adopt lawful fees, program capital work, and disclose fiscal effects |
| Affordable housing | Fund DHCD programs, tax credits, rental assistance, and preservation | Use MPDU rules, land, local funds, incentives, and partnerships |
| Tenant protections | Set landlord-tenant law and authorize some local options | Enforce local licensing, property maintenance, and any expressly authorized protections |
| State land and transit | Control state property, transportation programs, and TOD approvals | Coordinate plans and utilities and exercise remaining local authority |

State and local actions should be evaluated as a package. Faster approvals without infrastructure planning can create service gaps; production targets without affordability tools may miss the households in greatest need; and local protections without state authority can be vulnerable to preemption.

## Council decision framework

For a Maryland housing bill, executive action, or local implementation measure, answer:

1. **Status:** Is it introduced, amended, enrolled, enacted, effective, regulated, or merely proposed?
2. **Controlling text:** Which chapter, code section, regulation, order, or ordinance governs?
3. **Scope:** Which jurisdictions, stations, parcels, projects, tenures, and dates are covered?
4. **Local authority:** Is Frederick required, permitted, prohibited, or unaffected?
5. **Transition:** What happens to pending, complete, approved, phased, or vested projects?
6. **Administration:** Which department, form, deadline, dataset, and appeal process must change?
7. **Fiscal effect:** When is revenue collected, what costs shift, and how is infrastructure funded?
8. **Equity effect:** Who gains housing access, who faces displacement or cost shifts, and which income levels are served?
9. **Evidence:** Which outcome will be measured against what baseline?
10. **Sunset or update:** When should the Council revisit the policy?
