# Housing Politics and Advocacy

## Purpose

Use this page to understand **who is participating, which claims are supported, where interests can be reconciled, and how Council can build a legitimate decision record**. It is not a playbook for dismissing opposition or treating hearing testimony as a representative poll.

## Three records must remain distinct

| Record | Core question | Strongest evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Policy | What are the likely effects of the proposal? | Ordinance text, administrative data, fiscal/transport/environmental analysis, comparable evaluations |
| Political | Who supports or opposes it, with what intensity and interests? | Stakeholder map, meeting records, surveys, outreach logs, organized positions |
| Legitimacy | Was the process accessible, understandable, responsive, and consistent with law? | Notice, language/access measures, alternatives considered, written responses, findings |

A crowded hearing can be politically important without being statistically representative. A technically strong proposal can still fail if the process hides tradeoffs or asks the public to react after the meaningful choices have already been made.

## What participation research shows

Research based on thousands of planning and zoning meeting comments found that participants were disproportionately older, male, long-time residents, voters, and homeowners—and much more opposed to new housing than the general public. [Einstein, Palmer & Glick, *Perspectives on Politics*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/perspectives-on-politics/article/abs/who-participates-in-local-government-evidence-from-meeting-minutes/C6505940E607B6392C4A8F53A9363DB1)

A separate causal study found that becoming a property owner increased participation in city-council meetings and political giving. [Yoder, *American Political Science Review*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/american-political-science-review/article/abs/does-property-ownership-lead-to-participation-in-local-politics-evidence-from-property-records-and-meeting-minutes/E3BAEB8B52992D8FCF37FF3166BB2E77)

The implication is not that homeowners’ views count less. It is that Council should not mistake the visible participant pool for the affected population. Renters, future residents, younger adults, workers with evening obligations, caregivers, and people with limited English may carry equal stakes but face higher participation costs.

## Build the stakeholder map before the hearing

| Stakeholder | Likely stake | Participation barrier | Useful evidence or engagement |
|---|---|---|---|
| Current nearby owners | Property use, parking, construction, neighborhood change | Often low | Site plan, traffic/parking facts, design alternatives |
| Renters and cost-burdened households | Rent, displacement, access to opportunity | Schedule, mobility, distrust | Tenant outreach, translated survey, displacement analysis |
| Future residents | Potential access to housing | Not yet present or identifiable | Regional need data and fair-housing analysis |
| Builders and property owners | Feasibility, timing, return, predictability | Low | Pro forma assumptions and development pipeline |
| Affordable-housing providers | Subsidy, land, operating feasibility | Staff capacity | Project pipeline and financing gaps |
| Employers/workers | Commute, recruitment, retention | Diffuse interest | Workforce and commute data |
| Neighborhood/civic groups | Place quality and constituent voice | Varies | Structured issue-by-issue response |
| Historically excluded groups | Displacement, discrimination, access | Language, time, trust, disability | Trusted intermediaries and accessible formats |

For each major proposal, publish who was contacted, how, when, in which languages, and what changed as a result.

## Diagnose the dispute

Do not label every objection “NIMBY.” Classify it:

- **Factual:** disagreement over traffic, schools, utilities, rents, or fiscal effects.
- **Distributional:** benefits are broad while burdens are concentrated, or vice versa.
- **Procedural:** late notice, inaccessible material, or perceived predetermined outcome.
- **Value-based:** disagreement about growth, property rights, public investment, or neighborhood form.
- **Project-specific:** massing, access, trees, drainage, parking, construction, or management.
- **System-level:** concern about cumulative supply, affordability, segregation, displacement, or climate.

The response should match the dispute. More data can resolve a factual question; it will not by itself resolve a value conflict.

## Evidence standard for advocacy claims

Every public claim should be reducible to five elements:

1. **Claim:** What exactly is asserted?
2. **Source:** Is it law, local data, analysis, research, or anecdote?
3. **Baseline:** Compared with what geography and time period?
4. **Tradeoff:** Who benefits, who bears cost or risk, and what is uncertain?
5. **Ask:** What action is within this body’s authority?

Council packets should correct material errors consistently whether they favor or oppose the proposal.

## Engagement design

Use multiple channels because no single channel is representative:

- early issue-framing session before a preferred alternative;
- plain-language one-page proposal and change log;
- mapped and project-specific information;
- online and paper comment options;
- targeted outreach through tenant, disability, immigrant, faith, school, employer, and service networks;
- accessible locations, remote access, interpretation, and child-friendly timing where feasible;
- a formal hearing with an indexed staff response to material issues; and
- a post-decision explanation linking findings to evidence.

Surveys should disclose recruitment method and respondent profile. Open online surveys measure participants, not public opinion, unless a valid sampling method is used.

## Negotiation: protect outcomes, not just positions

Potentially negotiable items include setbacks, transitions, construction management, tree retention, pedestrian connections, affordability duration, tenant protections, public realm, and implementation phasing. Do not trade away a core legal or policy objective for conditions that are unenforceable, unrelated, or unsupported by authority.

For every offered condition, record:

- responsible party;
- legal instrument;
- measurable standard;
- monitoring agency;
- duration; and
- remedy for nonperformance.

## Project decisions versus system decisions

| Project record | System record |
|---|---|
| Does this application meet adopted standards? | Are the standards producing enough homes of needed types? |
| What site impacts require mitigation? | Are approval time, cost, and uncertainty discouraging feasible projects? |
| What findings does the law require? | Are burdens and benefits fairly distributed citywide? |
| Which conditions are lawful and enforceable? | Should zoning, fees, subsidies, or process rules change? |

Do not use a single project hearing to substitute for a citywide policy debate, or a citywide housing goal to erase required project findings.

## Frederick working case: accessory dwelling units

The prior concept page tracked case **PC25-614ZTA** and disagreement over owner-occupancy rules. Because procedural status and ordinance language can change, use `[[ADU Ordinance Brief 2026-08-13]]` as the case file and verify the current legislative tracker, staff report, Planning Commission recommendation, and adopted text before citing the result.

Questions for the case:

- What problem is the owner-occupancy rule intended to solve?
- What evidence shows the rule changes management, neighborhood effects, financeability, or production?
- Can the concern be addressed with generally applicable safety, nuisance, licensing, or enforcement rules?
- How many ADUs have been applied for, approved, built, and occupied under the current code?
- Are attached and detached ADUs affected differently?

## Decision record template

Before the vote, the packet should include:

- the precise policy objective;
- alternatives, including no action;
- authority and required legal findings;
- local baseline and assumptions;
- distributional and fair-housing effects;
- fiscal and administrative effects;
- stakeholder/outreach record and material themes;
- staff response to disputed factual claims;
- enforceable mitigation or conditions; and
- monitoring and review date.

## Metrics for the political process

Track more than attendance:

- participant geography and renter/owner status when voluntarily provided;
- outreach channels, language, disability access, and timing;
- share of comments received before versus after the preferred alternative;
- material changes attributable to engagement;
- response time to factual questions;
- participation by underrepresented groups;
- implementation outcomes promised during adoption; and
- whether recurring objections reveal a policy-design or trust problem.

## Council checklist

- [ ] Separate policy evidence, political intensity, and procedural legitimacy.
- [ ] Identify missing affected groups; do not treat hearing speakers as a sample.
- [ ] Publish the exact proposal and a change log early enough to respond.
- [ ] Distinguish factual disputes from value and distributional conflicts.
- [ ] Test proposed conditions for legality, enforceability, and monitoring.
- [ ] State which feedback changed the proposal and which did not—and why.
- [ ] Set an implementation review tied to outcomes, not only complaints.
