# Building Types and Codes

Building types describe how homes are arranged. Building codes establish requirements for their construction and use, including structural safety, fire protection, escape routes, and accessibility. Housing policy connects the two: a form may fit a neighborhood and be allowed by zoning but still face construction, financing, or operating constraints.

This article explains those connections and provides a framework for evaluating changes. A cheaper building does not automatically produce a lower rent, and a smaller building does not automatically qualify for a simpler code.

> [!info] Scope and currency
> Reviewed September 27, 2026. Local references concern the **City of Frederick**, not Frederick County's separate development regulations. Enacted requirements, research findings, examples from other cities, and suggested evaluation criteria are distinguished below. Original research notes remain linked at the end.

## 1. Distinguish form, tenure, construction, and code classification

These are separate dimensions of a housing proposal:

| Dimension | Examples | Why the distinction matters |
|---|---|---|
| Physical form | Rowhouse, duplex, courtyard building, small apartment building | Determines relationships among homes, circulation, open space, and the site. |
| Tenure and governance | Rental, condominium, cooperative, community land trust | Determines ownership, management, and possible affordability restrictions; it is not a building form. |
| Construction method | Site construction, panelization, modular construction | Changes production and inspection arrangements without automatically changing land-use permission. |
| Residential arrangement | Independent apartments, shared housing, single-room occupancy | Affects facilities, management, privacy, and the applicable occupancy analysis. |
| Regulatory classification | Zoning use, building-code occupancy, code scope | Determines which requirements apply; a marketing label does not settle classification. |

“Missing middle” is a planning description for housing between detached houses and larger apartment development, not a universal legal category. “Pocket neighborhood” describes a layout; “cohousing” describes intentional shared living and governance. Neither establishes an affordability level or a code exemption.

For Frederick zoning definitions, permitted uses, and recent amendments, use [[Zoning Reform]]. The absence of a separate “triplex” or “sixplex” label is insufficient evidence that a building is prohibited: the applicable broader use category and district rules must be checked.

## 2. Frederick's construction-code baseline and authority

The City's published code list applies the **2021 IBC, IRC, and IECC** to permit applications submitted after December 31, 2025, with Maryland requirements and applicable amendments. It also lists the 2021 mechanical code, 2018 plumbing and fuel-gas codes, 2023 electrical code, and the Maryland fire code based on 2024 NFPA 1 and NFPA 101. A project must identify all applicable requirements, rather than treating the IBC alone as the entire code system.[^city]

The model IRC generally covers qualifying one- and two-family dwellings and townhouses up to three stories. An apartment building does not become an IRC building simply because it is small. The IBC also regulates residential buildings; describing it as exclusively a “commercial code” obscures that distinction.[^irc]

**Maryland does allow local building-code amendments within statutory limits.** Public Safety §12-504 prohibits specified weakening of minimum enforcement, energy conservation, residential sprinkler, and wind-design requirements. Maryland's code administration guidance also identifies accessibility protections. This corrects the earlier blanket statement that Frederick cannot amend its building code.[^authority][^state]

That authority does not establish that a particular single-stair or sprinkler amendment is lawful. A proposed change needs a provision-by-provision comparison with Maryland building and fire requirements, its adoption procedure, and the officials responsible for enforcement. Zoning reform alone cannot authorize a noncompliant building.

For a representative housing proposal, a useful staff code summary would identify:

- The zoning use and parcel constraints.
- The applicable code edition, scope, and occupancy classification.
- Height, area, construction type, and dwelling-unit assumptions.
- Required exits, fire protection, accessible routes, and accessible units.
- Existing-building or change-of-use provisions, where relevant.
- Which requirements are local choices and which come from state or federal law.

This is a recommended evaluation format, not an assertion that the City currently publishes such a summary.

## 3. Housing forms and the problems they may solve

The following is an analytical comparison, not a schedule of permitted uses.

| Form or arrangement | Potential contribution | Main matters to evaluate |
|---|---|---|
| Rowhouses and townhouses | Individual entrances and several homes on a block | Lot pattern, accessible options, separation requirements, and total land cost |
| Duplexes and small apartment buildings | Additional homes on smaller sites | Actual code pathway, utility arrangements, fire protection, and financing |
| Courtyard or pocket neighborhoods | Shared outdoor space and a range of smaller homes | Density, maintenance responsibility, access, and site infrastructure |
| Cohousing | Private homes with shared facilities and resident participation | Governance burden, entry costs, inclusive recruitment, and long-term affordability |
| ADUs | Another home on an existing residential property | State/local land-use rules, site access, utilities, and owner financing |
| SROs and co-living | Smaller private accommodation with some shared facilities | Resident rights, sanitation, privacy, management, and building classification |
| Small buildings with one protected stair | Potentially more flexible plans on constrained sites | Legal authority, full fire-safety design, accessibility, and local response capability |

The relevant comparison is the **total cost and usefulness of an occupied home**. A form with fewer common areas may save construction space but sacrifice amenities. A walk-up may have lower capital costs but serve fewer people with mobility limitations. Shared facilities may reduce private floor area while increasing management needs.

Do not apply the original research notes' elevator, parking, staircase, or prefab prices as Frederick estimates. They lack a common local specification, price date, and cost boundary. Request comparable designs and estimates instead.

**A reported by-right form change: Raleigh's Method Road conversion.** CASA, a Raleigh nonprofit, replaced a deteriorating 1960s triplex with a nine-unit building for young adults aging out of foster care without a rezoning hearing, according to a planning newsletter's account of the project. The reported mechanism combines three tools already in Raleigh's base zoning: a frequent-transit-area density allowance that set the unit count by lot area rather than a use-specific cap, the citywide elimination of parking minimums (so no parking waiver was needed), and a pre-2001 stormwater exemption that applied because the lot predated 2001 and impervious surface stayed under the exemption's limit. CASA reportedly learned the rezoning was unnecessary only when city staff mentioned it during an unrelated pre-application meeting, which is itself a caution that even development-friendly code tools can go unused if they are not visible to applicants and reviewers. The account also names an implementation problem worth stating directly: a by-right reform can underdeliver if staff review still proceeds as discretionary review, since the project reportedly went through four rounds of site-plan review for a half-acre parcel that had neither a parking nor a stormwater question to resolve, with substantive comments arriving only in the final round.[^casa] This is a single case from a specialized planning newsletter, not an evaluated program or a primary document; it illustrates how transit-area density, parking policy, and a legacy exemption can combine to allow a form change like this one, and it is a caution that a Frederick missing-middle or parking package should pair any by-right allowance with a defined, front-loaded review timeline.

## 4. Single-stair buildings: mechanism, evidence, and legal status

A single-stair or point-access apartment building uses one principal exit stair to serve homes, typically with a compact circulation layout. Eliminating a second stair and some connecting corridor can improve the use of a narrow lot and permit different apartment layouts.

Pew and the Center for Building in North America's February 2025 report found a strong safety record in the small single-stair buildings it examined. It also discusses concerns raised by fire-service organizations about evacuation and firefighting sharing a stair, smoke control, maintenance, and response capability. Its cost estimates are illustrative: the often-cited 6–13% figure concerns modeled hard costs for a hypothetical building, not measured savings across Frederick projects.[^pew]

**Evidence interpretation:** the report supports serious evaluation of a defined design package. It does not demonstrate that every single-stair design is safe, that safety systems can be removed independently, or that resulting homes will rent for a specified discount. Evaluate the building and its operating conditions together.

### What has actually been enacted

**Maryland:** Chapter 504 of 2025, HB 489, requires a study of single-stair residential buildings up to six stories above grade and recommendations concerning relevant code changes. The report is due **December 1, 2026**. The study must consider matters including water supply, professional fire response, modern safety measures, infill, reuse, and cost. This is a study mandate, not permission to construct a six-story single-stair building in Frederick.[^mdstudy]

**Federal:** The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act became Public Law 119-101 on July 11, 2026. Section 102 directs HUD to develop point-access-block guidance within 18 months and permits a competitive pilot program. It explicitly preserves state and local building-code authority. Enactment therefore does not itself approve a Frederick project or establish that grant applications are currently available.[^road]

### What a Frederick evaluation should compare

Use the same site and housing program for a compliant baseline and a proposed alternative. Compare net residential area, bedroom mix, usable open space, accessibility, construction and operating costs, and required public infrastructure. Have building and fire officials assess exit protection, travel distances, compartmentation, sprinklers, smoke control, rescue access, inspection, and maintenance.

Record which claimed savings come from smaller circulation areas, which come from changed safety specifications, and which rely on additional rentable space. Those are different mechanisms with different implications.

## 5. Dallas: a conditional small-multifamily pathway

Dallas's 2025 ordinance expanded its residential-code framework to qualifying buildings with up to eight dwelling units. Its consolidated-dwelling provisions include height, area, fire-resistance, egress, accessibility, and other requirements.

The original clipping's claim that Dallas “eliminated sprinkler requirements” was too broad. Section R334.3 requires sprinklers but provides an exception for buildings no more than three stories **including the basement** that meet specified fire-resistance provisions. The ordinance also retains accessibility, fire-access, and other obligations.[^dallas]

For Frederick, this is a comparative policy example, not an available code pathway. The useful lesson is to examine a complete package for small buildings and determine whether Maryland permits it. Copying the unit threshold or sprinkler exception alone would omit the conditions that make the Dallas pathway work.

## 6. SROs and co-living: design and operating policy

A single-room occupancy arrangement generally provides a small private room with some facilities potentially shared. Co-living is a broader description and may involve shared kitchens, living areas, or other amenities. Neither label determines the legal occupancy classification, tenancy arrangement, or level of supportive services.

Frederick's 2026 Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force identifies SROs and co-living as forms that can expand housing opportunities. That recommendation is evidence of a local policy priority; it is not an adopted ordinance authorizing every such arrangement.[^taskforce]

The following framework develops César's [[SRO thread]] into explicit policy choices:

| Policy element | Decision to specify | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Private space and shared facilities | Room standards; kitchen, toilet, and bathing arrangements | Residents need usable facilities, privacy, and sanitation. |
| Management | Cleaning, repairs, responsible contact, and complaint response | Shared facilities create continuing operating obligations. |
| Tenancy | Lease terms, deposits, fees, and applicable resident protections | A low advertised rent can conceal insecurity or extra charges. |
| Affordability | Total monthly charge and any income or rent restrictions | Small rooms do not guarantee affordability for intended households. |
| Existing-building conversion | Net housing effect, displaced residents, and relocation measures | Conversion may remove other useful or inexpensive homes. |
| Services | Whether services are optional, funded, or required | Supportive housing is a service model, not an automatic feature of an SRO. |
| Oversight | Inspections, enforcement capacity, and reporting | Standards are useful only if they can be maintained. |

This table is an analytical recommendation. Specific occupancy, licensing, tenancy, and enforcement provisions need to be grounded in the adopted ordinance and applicable law when a proposal is prepared.

Evaluate rents including utilities and mandatory fees, resident stability, maintenance complaints, and net additions to the housing stock. Avoid the unsupported conclusions that SROs are universally the cheapest unsubsidized housing or that their loss alone explains homelessness. See [[Housing Affordability and Inequality]] for the broader affordability framework.

## 7. Factory construction: distinguish the regulatory systems

**Modular buildings** are factory-produced components or assemblies regulated through Maryland's industrialized-building program. Maryland regulates factory construction; local officials retain responsibilities for installation and site work, including foundations, utility connections, and site preparation. Factory approval therefore does not replace development approval for a parcel.[^modular]

**HUD-code manufactured homes** use a separate federal construction framework. Maryland's explanatory material distinguishes them from modular buildings.[^definitions] However, its permanent-chassis description must be read alongside the newer federal law: ROAD Act §301 changes the federal definition to accommodate homes with or without a permanent chassis and provides related implementation and state-certification requirements. A chassis-free design still needs the appropriate approvals; the statutory change is not proof that a particular product is certified.[^road]

**Tiny homes and movable dwellings** are descriptive terms. Wheels, small size, or factory manufacture do not by themselves establish a lawful permanent-residence pathway. Identify the product's certification, intended occupancy, installation, and applicable land-use rules before treating it as a housing option.

For any factory-built proposal, compare the full installed price:

- Factory work and design changes.
- Transportation, crane access, assembly, and inspection.
- Land, foundations, utilities, drainage, and other site work.
- Financing, insurance, contingency, and warranties.
- Operating costs, repairability, and replacement components.

A rapid on-site assembly video does not measure the complete development schedule. The original Ireland and China examples remain in the research archive; they are not used here as local cost or delivery forecasts.

## 8. Preapproved plans, accessibility, and building life

### Preapproved plans

Standard plans can reduce repeated design work and review of repeated details. Eugene's ADU program is a useful official example; using a preapproved design still requires the project's site-specific permitting process.[^plans]

A Frederick program should identify which reviews are already satisfied, who maintains designs when codes change, licensing costs, accessible variants, and remaining site submissions. Measure elapsed time from a complete application and distinguish government review from applicant revision time. “Months to weeks” should be a measured result with a defined starting point, not a promise borrowed from a clipping.

### Accessibility

Code simplification should retain a clear accessibility analysis. HUD identifies Fair Housing Act design and construction requirements for covered multifamily housing with four or more units first occupied after March 13, 1991. The applicable unit and route requirements depend on the building; other accessibility obligations may also apply.[^hud]

For Council comparisons, show the number and location of accessible homes, access to common facilities, and who cannot use the proposed layout. A reduction in elevator or circulation costs should be evaluated alongside those consequences.

### Reuse and deconstruction

Treat adaptation, deconstruction, and material reuse as lifecycle questions. Compare retaining and improving an existing building with replacing it, considering usable homes, accessibility, operating performance, embodied impacts, salvage value, and displacement.

For a proposed reuse requirement, request a local assessment of labor, storage, resale markets, hazardous-material handling, and schedule effects. The original archive contains useful leads, but its national waste figures and other cities' diversion percentages are not presented as Frederick performance estimates.

## 9. Council decision framework

Use this sequence to turn the article into a basis for a concrete decision:

1. **Define the housing need.** Identify the households, bedroom mix, locations, tenure, and affordability target.
2. **Identify the actual constraint.** Determine whether zoning, code design, site infrastructure, financing, or ongoing operations prevents delivery.
3. **Identify the responsible authority.** Separate City actions from Maryland requirements and federal product or accessibility standards.
4. **Compare complete alternatives.** Use representative sites, equivalent cost boundaries, safety systems, and realistic schedules.
5. **Specify resident outcomes.** Explain affordability commitments, accessibility, tenancy, maintenance, and displacement safeguards.
6. **Measure delivery and revise.** Track applications, approvals, completed and occupied homes, total cost, rents, and relevant safety or maintenance outcomes.

Suggested first applications are a staff code-pathway guide for small housing projects, a costed review of representative small-building designs, and an SRO/co-living policy specification. These are proposed next steps, not completed City initiatives.
