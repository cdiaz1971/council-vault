# Tenant Protections

Tenant protections are laws, services, and enforcement systems intended to make renting safer, more predictable, and less vulnerable to discrimination or avoidable displacement. They address different problems: unsafe conditions, unaffordable increases, hidden charges, unfair screening, loss of possession, and difficulty exercising existing rights.

A useful policy comparison asks **which harm is addressed, who is covered, who enforces the rule, what remedy reaches the tenant, and what happens to the housing afterward**. A rental license, rent cap, fee schedule, legal-aid service, and emergency assistance grant answer different questions.

**Review basis: October 3, 2026.** Legal and program claims in the reference sections below are tied to the cited primary sources. Working proposals are explicitly attributed. The original news-based synthesis and its research links are retained as a historical trail, not as independently verified statements of current law.

## 1. Policy tools and their purposes

This table is an analytical framework for comparing proposals; it does not assert that Frederick has adopted every tool.

| Tool | Problem addressed | Main design question |
|---|---|---|
| Habitability standards and inspections | Dangerous or unhealthy housing | How quickly are defects corrected, and can residents remain safely housed? |
| Rental licensing | Accountability for operating rental housing | What consequences produce compliance without shifting the harm to tenants? |
| All-in pricing and fee restrictions | Misleading prices and avoidable charges | Which charges must be included, disclosed, limited, or prohibited? |
| Application and screening rules | Exclusion based on records, credit, or opaque criteria | What may be asked, when, and with what explanation and appeal? |
| Lease terms | One-sided or abusive contract terms | Which terms must appear, which are void, and when can a tenant leave early? |
| Rent stabilization | Large or unpredictable increases | Who benefits, and how are maintenance, investment, conversions, and new supply treated? |
| Good-cause renewal protections | Loss of housing without a specified justification | What grounds, exemptions, notice, proof, and remedies apply? |
| Counsel, assistance, and diversion | Eviction arising from legal or financial barriers | Can help arrive before a filing or displacement, and is the eventual rent sustainable? |
| Fair-housing enforcement | Discriminatory access or treatment | Can renters document and remedy screening, advertising, and accommodation violations? |
| Relocation support and preservation | Displacement from unsafe housing, closure, or ownership change | Who pays, where can households move, and is there a path back? |
| Enforcement architecture | Rights that exist on paper but are not enforced | Who investigates, what can they order, and how does money reach the tenant? |
| Tenant organizing and accessible information | Difficulty identifying shared problems and asserting rights | Can residents participate without retaliation, language barriers, or inaccessible processes? |

**Price transparency and price limits are different.** Requiring a landlord to advertise the full mandatory cost does not establish a ceiling on that cost. Likewise, assistance can reduce a household's burden without regulating rent.

## 2. Maryland legal baseline

The Office of Tenant and Landlord Affairs (OTLA) in DHCD publishes the Maryland Tenants' Bill of Rights, updates it annually, and requires landlords to attach the current edition to every residential lease. The current edition is effective October 1, 2026.[^billrights] It summarizes state law; the statutes control.

### Applications and screening

Landlords with five or more units may keep no more than $25 of an application fee unless actual application costs are higher, must refund any unused excess within 15 days, must disclose in writing whether they screen criminal history before taking a fee, and must give an itemized list of application expenses on request. A landlord who accepts a recent tenant-supplied screening report may not charge an application fee.[^billrights]

Criminal-history screening is optional, but a landlord who screens anyone must screen everyone. Landlords with more than five units who screen must make a **conditional offer before checking criminal history**, may ask only about specified serious offenses before the offer, may withdraw only for a substantial, legitimate, nondiscriminatory reason tied to those offenses or a felony in the prior five years, must give written reasons, and must reconsider on evidence of inaccuracy, rehabilitation, or mitigating factors. Landlords with more than five units may not advertise that they will not consider applicants with arrests or convictions unless federal law requires it.[^billrights]

Applicants paying with an income-based subsidy may not be refused because of income, credit score, lack of credit score, or negative credit history from a period without the subsidy; income is tested only against the tenant's share of rent, using the ratio applied to other tenants.[^billrights] Pet policies, including all pet fees and deposits, must appear in the application.[^billrights]

### Leases and early termination

Landlords with five or more units must use a written lease. A written lease must state the property's condition, who pays which utilities and repairs, include a deposit receipt and the current Bill of Rights, and identify the landlord or agent for notices. Leases may not include confession of judgment, waiver of legal rights or jury trial, or shortened notice periods. Automatic renewal terms must be separately signed. **Local jurisdictions may require additional lease content.**[^billrights]

To end a tenancy, a landlord gives 60 days' notice for fixed-term and month-to-month tenancies and 90 days for year-to-year; a tenant gives 30 days, or 90 for year-to-year.[^billrights] A tenant who has experienced abuse may end a lease early with a final protective or peace order or a qualified third-party report, has up to 30 days to move, and owes rent only until move-out (Real Property Title 8, Subtitle 5A).[^billrights]

### Rent increases, late fees, and security deposits

Maryland has no general statewide ceiling on renewal rent increases; some local jurisdictions have additional limits.[^billrights] Rent may be raised only at renewal. Real Property §8-209 generally requires advance written notice of 90 days for terms longer than one month, 60 days for terms longer than one week through one month, and 7 days for written weekly tenancies or 21 days without a written lease. Its scope and exceptions matter; it also preserves additional local protections.[^notice] Electronic notice is valid only if the tenant agreed to receive notices electronically.[^billrights]

Late fees may not exceed 5% of the late amount and may be charged only on the unpaid portion of a partial payment.[^billrights]

For security deposits, §8-203 generally sets a **one-month-rent maximum per dwelling unit**, regardless of tenant count. Two months is permitted only for leases signed before October 1, 2024, or when the tenant qualifies for specified utility assistance and pays utilities directly to the landlord. The statutory definition includes advance last-month rent and pet deposits; labels do not determine coverage. A landlord may not require more than the first month's rent plus the deposit before move-in, and may not ask for or induce a larger "voluntary" deposit. The statute also regulates accounting, interest, the tenant's right to attend the move-out inspection on certified notice at least 15 days ahead, itemized deductions, return within 45 days, and remedies up to three times the amount wrongly withheld.[^deposit][^billrights]

**Source conflict resolved:** Frederick's tenant-rights overview lists a general two-month maximum. For the limit, use the state statute rather than that summary.[^cityrights] Whether a particular older deposit was lawful when collected requires the applicable law and dates.

### Habitability, repairs, and relocation

Rent escrow gives tenants a court process for serious conditions after notice to the landlord. The court controls the escrow and the disposition of funds.[^courts] A code citation or violation notice counts as written notice to the landlord.[^billrights]

The separate warranty of habitability in §8-212 applies at the beginning of a covered tenancy and throughout it. Following statutory notice and an opportunity to correct conditions, remedies can include damages, rent abatement, lease termination, return of unused deposit, and **relocation expenses**. Relief under this section is not conditioned on paying rent into court escrow.[^habitability] A court-awarded remedy for a qualifying habitability breach is different from an automatic, readily available relocation fund. That distinction matters when designing a City response to an emergency closure.

State law also sets these condition rules:[^billrights]

- **Mold:** assessment within 15 days of written notice; if found, removal and repair of the cause within 45 days if possible, otherwise within a reasonable time, with the tenant kept informed. The Maryland Tenant Mold Protection Act took effect in 2025 without state implementation resources or a clear assignment of local enforcement responsibility.[^rvstaff]
- **Air conditioning, June 1 to September 30:** required in buildings with at least 10 rental units where the landlord provided AC after June 1, 2025, the lease requires it, or the unit is renovated with electrical or heating systems replaced; capable of cooling habitable space to 80°F.
- **Private wells:** test results at lease signing, testing every three years, drinkable water if contaminated, and within 60 days a permanent fix or the option to end the lease.
- **Entry:** written notice at least 24 hours ahead, Monday through Saturday, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m., except emergencies or with the tenant's written permission.
- **Utilities billed by the landlord:** monthly bills or the prior year's totals by utility at the start of each term.

**Water quality as a habitability question, not only a billing question.** A Dublin research project surveying 120 households found that a third relied on bottled water as their primary drinking source because aging building plumbing and water infrastructure eroded trust in the tap, even where tap water was free and the connection was not in dispute. The authors call this the "housing-water nexus": a household can be connected and billed fairly and still be water insecure if in-unit plumbing is degraded and no one is responsible for fixing it. This is Dublin-specific research, not a Frederick finding, but it reframes water as a third angle alongside access and billing.[^dublinwater] Frederick's rental licensing and code-enforcement inspection standards do not have a documented scope for in-unit water quality or building plumbing condition; that is a gap worth naming rather than a measured local problem. See also [[RUBS Strike and Utility Billing as a Tenant Protection Gap]] for the billing side of utility-related habitability concerns.

### Eviction procedure, representation, and retaliation

Maryland Courts distinguishes failure to pay rent, breach of lease, and tenant-holding-over cases. They have different requirements. A nonpayment case generally requires a ten-day notice of intent to file; eviction then requires a judgment of possession and a warrant of restitution executed by the sheriff or constable. A notice, filing, judgment, and completed eviction are different events.[^courts] **In a failure-to-pay-rent case, "rent" means base rent only, not utility charges, maintenance charges, or penalties.** After judgment the tenant has seven days before the landlord may seek a warrant. A tenant may usually redeem by paying past-due rent and court costs before the eviction, unless the tenant had three or more judgments in the prior 12 months and the landlord objects. Written notice before the scheduled eviction is six days under state law; **local jurisdictions may set between 4 and 14 days**.[^billrights] Lockouts, utility shutoffs, and other self-help efforts to remove a tenant are prohibited.[^billrights]

Maryland operates an **Access to Counsel in Evictions** program, reached through 211 or legalhelpmd.org.[^otla] The statute sets eligibility at income not greater than 50% of median income (Real Property §8-901); the Civil Right to Counsel coalition reads this as state median income, while the October 2026 Bill of Rights describes it as 50% of Area Median Income. verify before using the threshold in public materials.[^ace][^billrights] Eligibility and service access should be checked with the provider.

Real Property §8-208.1 prohibits specified retaliatory actions for protected complaints, litigation participation, tenant organizing, summoning police or emergency assistance, and lead paint reports. Retaliation includes suing or threatening to sue, **threatening to report immigration status**, raising rent, cutting services, failing to repair, and terminating the tenancy. Relief has conditions, including rent-payment rules and a six-month timing limitation. Protection against retaliation does not create a general right to renewal.[^retaliation][^billrights] Separately, it is a crime for anyone, including a landlord, to try to obtain money, property, labor, or services by threatening to report a person's immigration status (Criminal Law §3-701).[^billrights]

### Good cause: 2026 proposal did not become law

HB 774 passed the House and was referred to Senate Rules; SB 462 remained at the Senate hearing stage. Neither bill record shows enactment. They proposed authorizing **counties** to adopt local good-cause rules. The displayed proposed October 1 effective date is not evidence that the bills became law.[^goodcause]

Good-cause rules generally require a specified justification for covered nonrenewals or terminations. They need their own analysis of grounds, exemptions, enforcement, and interaction with rent increases. The failure of this bill does not, by itself, resolve every question about existing municipal authority.

### Fair housing and source of income

Maryland's protected classes are race, color, religion, sex, disability, marital status, familial status, sexual orientation, gender identity, national origin, **source of income**, and military status; local jurisdictions may add more.[^billrights] MCCR identifies discriminatory advertising, rental terms, refusals, and certain failures to accommodate disability as prohibited practices, and provides a complaint process.[^mccr] Assistance animals are not pets, and no added application fee or deposit may be charged for them.[^billrights]

For Frederick, the immediate question is implementation of Maryland protections: screening practices, documentation, referrals, and access to remedies. Litigation about another state's voucher law should not be described as invalidating Maryland law without a controlling decision applicable here. The original out-of-state litigation clippings remain in the research trail.

### Tenant purchase opportunities and rights information

Maryland's right-of-first-refusal framework covers qualifying sales of properties with one to three residential rental units to named tenants who have lived there at least six months. It includes an opportunity to offer to purchase; eligibility, excluded transfers (family, the landlord's own business, court order), notices, and the sequence of negotiation and refusal rights must be checked against §8-119 and the implementing regulations. A qualifying notice gives the tenant 30 days to offer or decline. Local jurisdictions may add their own purchase rights.[^purchase][^billrights]

A purchase right is not financing. Practical implementation may require counseling, acquisition funds, a lender, and a willing preservation partner. See [[Community Land Trusts and Social Ownership]] and [[Housing Finance and Preservation]].

### Municipal enforcement limits

Maryland raised the maximum fine a municipality may impose for a municipal infraction or a misdemeanor under local law from $1,000 to $5,000 (SB 820, Chapter 570 of 2025).[^infraction] This sets the ceiling for any Frederick tenant-protection penalty enforced as a municipal infraction.

## 3. Frederick: current administration and working proposals

### Existing rental licensing

The City's licensing page states a **$240 fee per unit for a two-year license**, with registration, renewal, exemptions, maintenance standards, and random inspection provisions. The public portal allows searches for existing licenses.[^licensing]

Do not interchange properties, buildings, units, registered units, and active licenses. The old synthesis's figure of about 7,800 "properties" is not used as the current denominator. Any workload or fee analysis should establish the date and unit of measurement.

### Real Rent: a working proposal

The vault's [[Real Rent Ordinance]], read September 28, 2026, is César's working text with blank enactment fields. It proposes all-in pricing through rental licensing and expressly disclaims a cap on the lawful amount of rent. Its major components are:

- Incorporating mandatory charges into advertised and charged Total Rent.
- Distinguishing genuinely optional services from required charges, including a more-than-25%-of-tenants presumption and adverse-treatment test.
- Restricting separately charged fees and circumvention through vendors.
- Regulating utility allocations and additional billing charges.
- Licensing consequences, graduated civil penalties, and a proposed private cause of action.

These are **draft provisions, not established tenant entitlements**. The earlier page recorded "In Committee" from a September 24 roadmap check. This review did not independently refresh that committee status; the public legislation register contained no matching "Rent" entry when checked.[^register] Absence from that page is not proof of every procedural event. Track live status in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].

Two drafting details are worth retaining accurately:

1. The working utility clause prohibits flat charges that do not reflect actual or estimated usage **unless incorporated into Total Rent**. The earlier summary omitted this exception.
2. Section 12.5-99 states 120 days after adoption, while the closing Section III says effective on mayoral approval. This is an internal timing inconsistency for the legislative draft to resolve.

This encyclopedia revision identifies those issues; it does not amend the ordinance.

### What the legal-review note does and does not establish

[[Real Rent Ordinance - Legal Review Prep]] records objections and arguments concerning private remedies, deposits, licensing, and anti-circumvention. It is an internal analysis, not a judicial holding.

In *Aleti v. Metropolitan Baltimore*, the Maryland court declined to infer a private right to recover paid rent from Baltimore's licensing prohibition. Its analysis concerns that ordinance, legislative intent, and the claimed remedy. It does **not** automatically establish either that every expressly drafted local private action is forbidden or that adding express language guarantees validity.[^aleti]

For Real Rent, the legal decision points are municipal authority, state-law conflict or preemption, permissible penalties and restitution, the proposed private remedy, and procedural protections. Section 5 sets out peer enforcement models that do not depend on a local private right of action.

### Licensing enforcement and displacement

[[What Happens After License Revocation -- The Missing Piece in Frederick's Tenant Protection Stack]] identifies a useful policy problem: what happens to residents when enforcement makes continued operation impossible? Its proposed changes and the fee proposals in [[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-05-26]] should remain attributed proposals until adopted text establishes otherwise. Rockville's adopted relocation rules (Section 5) are the closest Maryland municipal model for answering it.

**Recommended design questions:** Who communicates with residents? Is the building immediately unsafe? Can repairs occur with residents in place? Who funds temporary accommodation, deposits, moving, accessible transport, and storage? Is there an appeal, a repair deadline, a return process, and a means to recover costs from the responsible party? Treat violation correction and resident stability as separate outcomes.

## 4. Fees, utilities, and algorithmic pricing

### All-in pricing and fee schedules

A useful fee policy distinguishes required recurring charges, genuinely optional purchases, deposits, actual usage charges, and contingent charges such as late fees. Each category may need different treatment. Two adopted peer laws show the main design choices.

**Rockville (Chapter 18, § 18-163 and § 18-180, effective January 1, 2027).**[^rockville]
- Banned: fees to pay rent by any method other than credit card processing and bounced checks; fees for services previously included in rent; fees for repairs the landlord already owes; any charge not billed within 120 days; utility administrative fees, including trash, not expressly allowed by Maryland law.
- Capped: lost key at cost plus $25; lockout at $25 or documented third-party cost; bicycle parking at a fraction of car parking.
- Opt-in only: parking, internet, and cable, with internet and cable billed at the provider's cost split among opting units. No fee for storage tied to the unit.
- Pets: one monthly fee per pet plus one escrowed pet deposit returned within 45 days with itemized proof.
- Any other fee only after written, informed opt-in following disclosure of the service, price, and how to cancel. Regulated and opt-in fees may rise once per 12 months with 90 days' notice. Overcharges must be refunded.
- Every advertised price must disclose all mandatory fees and whether utilities are included, except on sites the landlord does not control. Before signing and at renewal, the landlord discloses every fee and estimated utility costs, and **three years of rent history** for the unit and the building's average year-over-year increase. The lease states the total monthly charge.

Rockville staff modeled the list on Montgomery County's allowed-fee approach but declined the county's inflation-indexed fee caps, which exist to prevent circumvention of county rent stabilization. Staff found no study measuring the effects of fee caps and expected some landlords to fold banned fee revenue into base rent.[^rvstaff]

**Seattle (Ordinance 127497, effective July 1, 2027).**[^seattlefees]
- A closed list of permitted mandatory fees; any other mandatory or optional fee, however named, is "unfair or excessive," and any undisclosed fee is prohibited.
- A disclosure of no more than two pages, placed before the first page of every lease and in every listing and application, showing rent, concessions, utilities, every fee, and the total monthly cost including estimated utilities. Updated every 12 months and on any fee change. A City model form is translated into the five most common non-English languages.
- Optional fees require written opt-in, free opt-out at any time, third-party services at cost, and **unbundling** of bundled services.
- Banned even as optional: appliance use, common-area access, check or ACH payment, mail and packages, landlord duties, roommate changes, and any pet fee other than a pet damage deposit.
- Late fees remain under Seattle's separate $10 monthly cap, which also bans any fee for issuing a notice to a tenant.[^seattlelate]

Maryland already caps late fees at 5% of the unpaid amount;[^billrights] Rockville staff did not recommend a local late-fee rule for that reason.[^rvstaff] Fees for preparing or serving notices are not addressed in the state summary, which makes a notice-fee ban a narrower local question than a lower late-fee cap.

**Evaluation recommendation:** compare advertised rent with the complete required monthly payment and move-in cost. Measure whether an ordinance improves comparability and eliminates prohibited charges. Do not claim that consolidating fees necessarily reduces the total amount charged.

### Ratio utility billing systems

RUBS allocates master-meter utility charges without measuring each tenant's actual consumption. It should not be treated as synonymous with submetering or with a markup.

Maryland already regulates RUBS in §8-212.4. Covered landlords must disclose the method and utilities, recent bills, prior average monthly charges, record-inspection rights, and service or administrative charges. Failure to provide required information makes the lease's RUBS payment provision unenforceable. The section has coverage limits and exceptions, and expressly addresses stronger local disclosure and record rules.[^rubs]

That existing law is the starting point for Real Rent. Broader fee prohibitions need separate legal analysis. For policy design, examine common-area charges, vacancies, leaks, allocation assumptions, access to records, and responsibility for repairs.

### Algorithmic pricing

Rockville's § 18-148 bars landlords from using, subscribing to, or paying for an "algorithmic device," from engaging in price coordination, and from agreeing not to compete on rents, fees, or other rental terms. An algorithmic device is one that uses **non-public competitor data** less than 90 days old from two or more landlords to recommend rents, fees, terms, or occupancy. Exempt are aggregated reports published no more than monthly that do not recommend prices, tools used to set affordable-program rent or income limits, and landlords with only one rental unit in the City. Violations are municipal infractions.[^rockville]

Rockville staff identified eight local properties, nearly 2,000 market-rate apartments, managed by companies named in the U.S. Department of Justice suit against RealPage. They limited the ban to non-public competitor data after reviewing Berkeley's broader ban, which drew a RealPage First Amendment suit, and added the non-public data definition in response to RealPage's written testimony. Montgomery County Bill 8-26 is described by Rockville staff as largely similar.[^rvstaff]

This is a future-effective peer policy, not evidence of measured results. Before borrowing clauses, compare jurisdictional authority, exemptions, proof requirements, and enforcement capacity.

### Federal disclosure proposal: a complement, not a substitute, for a local ban

Rep. Kathy Castor's Housing Price Transparency Act would require firms that create or influence rental pricing to disclose their use of algorithmic pricing tools, enforced through the FTC's unfair-and-deceptive-practices authority, with a parallel track for state civil actions on tenants' behalf. As introduced, this is a transparency mandate, not a ban on algorithmic pricing itself, so it is weaker than Rockville's prohibition.[^algodisclosure] It has not been enacted. If it moves, it would establish a federal disclosure baseline rather than replace the stronger local-ban approach Real Rent would need its own provision to adopt; it is tracked here as a bill to watch, not current law.

## 5. Enforcement and remedies: peer models

Protections matter only if a tenant can get a remedy. After *Aleti*, the most relevant peer designs are ones that do not depend on a locally created private lawsuit.

**Rockville (Maryland municipality).**[^rockville]
- **Commission on Landlord-Tenant Affairs:** a long-standing body of two landlords, two tenants, and three public members, expanded by Chapter 18. After City Manager investigation and attempted conciliation, it holds hearings, may summon witnesses and documents, and may order lease termination with return of deposit and rent, damages up to **$5,000 per affected unit**, or rent reductions for lost services. Unpaid awards are enforceable in court by the party awarded; appeals go to Circuit Court. Tenant organizations have standing to file complaints for tenants who authorize them.
- **City-authorized repair and deduct:** after a failed correction deadline, the City Manager may authorize a tenant to have a licensed contractor fix the violation and deduct up to two months' rent, or may revoke the license.
- **Licensing:** every rental is licensed; mandatory denial for three violations in two years not cured within 30 days or one serious health and safety citation; an enhanced inspection sample for buildings with prior failures.
- **Relocation (§ 18-178):** temporary relocation (hotel, per diem, or comparable housing, plus moving and storage) when a unit is unfit; permanent relocation (deposit with interest, prorated rent, three months of HUD small area fair market rent, moving costs, and an added amount for households with someone over 62, a person with disabilities, or a child) on license revocation, long displacement, or redevelopment that displaces a household under 50% AMI; payable regardless of arrears; a right to return on substantially similar terms. City relocation costs are billed to the landlord and become a **property lien** if unpaid.
- **Penalties:** violations are municipal infractions, with a fine schedule up to $5,000 set by resolution.

Rockville staff had recommended tenant recovery of up to three times any unlawful fee through private claims; the adopted ordinance relies instead on the Commission and municipal infractions.[^rvstaff] Staff also reported no recent license suspensions or revocations and months-long court delays on contested citations, and cited Minneapolis, which recovered about a quarter of relocation payments it advanced to tenants.[^rvstaff]

**Seattle (Washington; not directly transferable).**[^seattlefees] Seattle has a private right of action, but its 2026 law centers on administrative enforcement: building-wide and company-wide investigations; subpoenas issued by a hearing examiner; three-year compliance records with a **rebuttable presumption of violation** when records are missing; orders of full restitution plus interest plus double the prohibited fees; penalties of $750 and $1,500 per violation, cut 75% or 50% for restitution paid within 10 or 15 days; a 30-day cure-and-repay defense to tenant suits; and conversion of unpaid amounts into a debt owed to the City, with tenant remedies held in trust and paid out. It is funded by a rental regulatory fee of about $11 per unit supporting three positions.

**Design questions for Frederick:** which body investigates and orders relief; whether the Charter supports an administrative award or hearing officer; whether license conditions, municipal infractions, and liens can carry most enforcement; how restitution reaches tenants; and how staff positions are funded. These are open authority and capacity questions, not settled conclusions.

## 6. Rent stabilization: evidence and design

Rent stabilization constrains increases in covered rents. Policies differ in inflation indexing, absolute caps, vacancy treatment, exemptions, hardship adjustments, capital-improvement rules, and enforcement.

Two primary research sources illustrate why evidence needs context:

| Evidence | Finding or approach | Limit for Frederick |
|---|---|---|
| Diamond, McQuade, and Qian, San Francisco, 2019 | A quasi-experimental study of the 1994 expansion found less tenant mobility and displacement, while affected landlords reduced rental supply by 15%, including through sales to owner-occupants and redevelopment. | These estimates concern that policy and market; a 15% loss of rental supply is not a 15% destruction of buildings or a universal forecast. |
| Joseph Rowntree Foundation, 2026 | Models combinations of rent controls and tax reform using English landlord data and household projections. | A modeled scenario is not an observed policy outcome. British tax arrangements and national forecasts do not establish Frederick landlord returns or local effects. |

Sources: [^sf] [^jrf]

The JRF analysis supports studying how design and tax treatment affect feasibility. It does not eliminate the possibility of owner exits or reduced investment; the report itself discusses the risk of rapid rental-market contraction.

Rockville pairs mandatory unit-level rent reporting at licensing with **voluntary** rent guidelines that landlords must include in every rent increase notice, an approach short of stabilization that builds the data record. Rockville staff recommended displaying building rent increases as five-year tiers rather than raw percentages, partly so published data would not help landlords coordinate prices.[^rockville][^rvstaff]

**Recommended assessment:** examine incumbent tenants and future renters separately; model maintenance and financing costs; distinguish new construction from conversion of existing rentals; identify enforcement funding; and monitor rental-stock changes. A proposal should state whether its primary objective is stability, lower rent growth, affordability for very low-income households, or some combination. These objectives may require complementary subsidies and preservation.

## 7. Eviction prevention and targeted support

A practical local system can connect early notice, legal screening, financial assistance, mediation, and follow-up. Maryland District Court offers free mediation or settlement conferences in many locations for civil and landlord-tenant cases; availability should be checked for the relevant court.[^adr]

These tools address different barriers:

- **Counsel** can identify defenses and negotiate terms but does not itself pay arrears.
- **Emergency assistance** can resolve a temporary shortfall but needs a sustainable plan for later rent.
- **Payment agreements** should reflect actual income and specify fees, missed-payment consequences, and legal rights.
- **Mediation** needs informed participation and access to advice, particularly where bargaining power differs.

Rockville staff flagged a design problem in assistance programs that require an active court case: some landlords may delay filing until arrears exceed what public and nonprofit funds can cover, at times over $10,000. They also proposed local funding to extend counsel to households above the state threshold.[^rvstaff]

These are program-design considerations, not claimed causal results from the original Alexandria clipping. A city should not promise an automatic 10% down-payment option based on an out-of-state example.

For older renters and people with disabilities, assess accessibility, fixed-income constraints, caregiver connections, and the cost of moving. Avoid treating age alone as a complete measure of need.

**Early lease termination.** State law covers abuse, active-duty reassignment, and certain medical conditions. Rockville adds 30-day termination, with any charge capped at one month's rent, for involuntary job relocation, a unit unfit for 60 days, license suspension or revocation, unrepaired health and safety violations, death of a primary wage earner, change in disability status, inpatient treatment, and other cause beyond the tenant's control.[^rockville][^rvstaff]

**Comparative example: NYC SCRIE.** NYC's published eligibility page lists age 62+, qualifying regulated housing, household income of **$50,000 or less**, and rent exceeding one-third of household income. Landlords receive a property-tax credit for the covered difference. The earlier $75,000 figure is not the limit on that program page; a July 2026 Council announcement described proposed expansion to $75,000.[^scrie] [^scrieproposal] Adapting such a mechanism in Frederick would require its own authority, funding, coverage, and administration.

**Near-neighbor eviction data: Baltimore County.** Baltimore County recorded more than 4,300 evictions in fiscal year 2025, the most of any Maryland jurisdiction, even as the statewide total fell, according to DHCD and District Court data reported by The Baltimore Banner.[^baltco] Zafar Shah of Maryland Legal Aid points to Montgomery County as the counterexample: by population and rent levels it should have the highest eviction rate in the state, and instead has the lowest, which he attributes to local legal-aid and assistance capacity rather than rent levels alone. That is a county-level comparison, not a Frederick measurement, but it supports pairing any tenant-protection package with funded eviction-prevention capacity (Section 7) rather than relying on substantive rules alone.

News coverage describes the Baltimore County Council as having passed a bill extending pre-eviction notice from six to fourteen days and repealing the county's "abandonment" ordinance, which had treated anything a tenant left behind after an eviction as abandoned.[^baltco] A separate, same-period report describes a similarly aimed bill, sponsored by a county councilmember, as still under council consideration rather than enacted.[^baltcobrew] **The current status of this notice/abandonment measure should be verified against the county's legislative record before it is cited as enacted law.** Maryland's state notice floor is six days, with local jurisdictions authorized to set a local notice period between four and fourteen days (Section 2 above); a Frederick ordinance choosing a point in that range should state its own reasoning rather than default to Baltimore County's choice, whatever that choice turns out to be.

The legal exposure behind the change is real regardless of this bill's status. A materially similar Baltimore City ordinance was struck down as unconstitutional by a federal court in 2022, a ruling the Fourth Circuit affirmed in 2024 for providing no reclamation period at all, and Maryland Legal Aid has separately sued Baltimore County on the same theory.[^baltcolawsuit] Frederick should confirm whether the City or Frederick County has any ordinance or practice treating a tenant's possessions as abandoned after a writ of restitution is executed, and if so, review it against this precedent rather than wait for a comparable complaint. Baltimore County has left open, with stakeholder recommendations due December 31, 2026, who stores a displaced tenant's belongings, who pays, and for how long; Frederick can watch that design process rather than need to resolve the storage question without a reference model.[^baltco]

## 8. Tenant agency and access to enforcement

Tenant organizations can surface recurring building-level problems and help residents document complaints. Maryland's anti-retaliation law specifically includes participation in a tenants' organization, subject to statutory conditions.[^retaliation]

Rockville's Chapter 18 states tenants' rights to self-organize, act collectively, and distribute information; gives tenant organizations free use of meeting rooms and common areas for their meetings; bars landlords from prohibiting tenant organization activity on the premises; and gives tenant organizations standing to file complaints on members' behalf.[^rockville] The National Tenants Bill of Rights, an advocacy agenda from NHLP, NLIHC, and the Tenant Union Federation, goes further: access for outside organizers, a rebuttable presumption that adverse action against a tenant organization member is retaliation, and remedies including license suspension.[^ntbor] Treat the latter as a policy menu, not established practice.

**Recommended implementation:** provide multilingual and accessible notices, multiple ways to submit complaints, clear referral routes, and updates on case progress. Ownership-search and guided legal tools can support this process, but ownership information should be checked and automated forms should not substitute for case-specific advice.

A complaint count alone is ambiguous: increases may reflect worsening conditions or improved access. Pair it with response time, substantiated findings, repeat violations, repairs completed, and resident outcomes.

## 9. A Council decision framework

For each proposed protection, request a short staff analysis covering:

1. **Problem and baseline:** how many affected households or units, where, over what period, and with what evidence?
2. **Coverage and authority:** current law, proposed change, exemptions, responsible government, and legal basis.
3. **State overlap:** whether Maryland already provides the protection (Section 2), so local law adds coverage or enforcement rather than duplicating it.
4. **Delivery and remedy:** intake, investigation, documentation, hearing or appeal, staffing, funding, and what the tenant actually receives.
5. **Tradeoffs:** compliance costs, rent or fee changes, owner exits, effects on small landlords, accessibility, and displacement during enforcement.
6. **Evaluation:** outcomes at stated intervals, an accountable department, and revision triggers.

The National Tenants Bill of Rights organizes protections around a tenant's path: fair application, fair lease, freedom from discrimination and harassment, habitable home, reasonable rent and costs, the right to organize, and safeguards against eviction.[^ntbor] That sequence is a useful checklist for whether a package leaves a stage uncovered.

Suggested measures should distinguish the following:

| Objective | Useful measures |
|---|---|
| Transparent prices | Difference between advertised and mandatory price; verified prohibited charges; restitution |
| Safe homes | Time to correct serious defects; repeat violations; households displaced by enforcement |
| Eviction prevention | Filings, judgments, executed evictions, assistance timing, and housing stability after 3–12 months |
| Fair access | Substantiated discrimination; voucher lease-up barriers; accommodation resolution |
| Sustainable rental stock | Licensed units, conversions, repairs, preservation, and rents for incoming tenants |
| Accessible enforcement | Referral completion, response time, language access, and who remains unserved |

Measure unique households as well as cases; repeated filings can distort counts. Report service outputs separately from outcomes and avoid attributing every change to one policy when market conditions and other programs also changed.

For Frederick, an appropriate next policy step is to connect **Real Rent enforcement, rental-condition enforcement, state legal-aid referrals, and a displacement response** into a defined administrative process. This is a recommendation from the analysis, not an adopted City plan.
