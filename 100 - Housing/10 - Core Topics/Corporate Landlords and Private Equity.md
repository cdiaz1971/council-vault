# Corporate Landlords and Private Equity

## Purpose

This page is a reference for decisions about concentrated or financialized rental ownership. It separates ownership form from landlord conduct, states what current evidence can and cannot establish, and provides a method for building a Frederick-specific record.

The core rule is: **define the actor, identify the conduct, measure the local outcome, and then choose a remedy within the relevant government's authority.** “Corporate landlord” is too broad to do that work by itself.

## The categories are different

| Term | What it identifies | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| **Record owner** | The person or entity named in land or tax records | The ultimate controlling party, property manager, or source of capital |
| **Property manager** | The firm handling leasing, maintenance, collections, or tenant relations | Ownership of the property |
| **Corporate landlord** | A landlord using a legal entity such as an LLC or corporation | Size, investment strategy, quality, or private-equity control |
| **Institutional investor** | An owner meeting a stated size or operational threshold | A universal legal category; studies and agencies use different definitions |
| **Private-equity real-estate fund** | A pooled, usually illiquid private fund that may buy controlling interests and use leverage | Every large landlord or every LLC |
| **REIT** | A company or trust using a federal real-estate investment trust tax structure; it may be publicly traded, public non-listed, or private | Private equity as such, or a particular management practice |
| **Lender or mortgage holder** | A creditor with a secured interest | Day-to-day control unless loan terms, distress, or foreclosure change that role |

The SEC describes private funds as pooled investment vehicles that are generally excluded from investment-company registration. Private-equity funds commonly invest in illiquid assets, may take controlling interests, and may use leverage. Fee arrangements and holding periods vary, so “2 and 20,” a 1–2 percent equity contribution, or a fixed three-to-seven-year hold should not be assumed without fund-specific evidence. Many private-fund advisers are subject to SEC registration and reporting rules, although Form PF is non-public and public visibility remains incomplete. [SEC: Starting a Private Fund](https://www.sec.gov/about/starting-private-fund) · [SEC: Private Fund Adviser Overview](https://www.sec.gov/about/divisions-offices/division-investment-management/private-fund-adviser-overview)

## What the evidence supports

The strongest national conclusion is narrower than the former version of this page suggested.

- A 2024 GAO review found that studies of institutional investment in single-family rentals generally associated it with higher home prices and rents, but effects on homeownership opportunities and tenants were less clear. GAO also found major data limitations and no consistent definition of “institutional investor.” [GAO-24-106643](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-24-106643)
- In a 2026 study of six selected metropolitan areas, GAO defined institutional investors as firms owning at least 5,000 single-family rental homes nationwide across at least five metropolitan areas. They owned 1–3 percent of all single-family homes and 4–22 percent of single-family rental homes in those markets. Those results are not a national estimate and do not measure Frederick's multifamily market. [GAO-26-108675](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-26-108675)
- A peer-reviewed Boston study found that large landlords filed eviction cases more often than small landlords and did so over lower amounts owed, after accounting for several property and neighborhood factors. It is evidence about Boston, not a universal estimate for every market or ownership type. [Gomory, *Social Forces*](https://academic.oup.com/sf/article-abstract/100/4/1774/6301048)
- A peer-reviewed study of institutional single-family rental mergers found rent increases in neighborhoods where merging firms' portfolios overlapped; it also found reduced crime near the properties. This shows why an analysis should test specific outcomes and tradeoffs rather than infer them from an ownership label. [Gurun, Wu, Xiao, and Xiao, *Review of Financial Studies*](https://academic.oup.com/rfs/article/36/1/70/6550515)

These sources justify scrutiny of scale, leverage, fees, maintenance, eviction practices, and acquisition patterns. They do **not** support a claim that private equity owns a fixed share of all U.S. apartments, that all corporate owners follow one business model, or that national findings describe Frederick without a local ownership analysis.

Institutional landlord conduct can also become a national political event. In Madrid, the eviction of an 87-year-old tenant after her institutional landlord raised her rent from 500 to 2,650 euros set off national protests and a government housing bill (bans on speculative purchases, rent freezes, indefinite leases) that then failed in Parliament ([[Spain Eviction Protests and the Failed Housing Bill]]). The source is commentary from a housing researcher; it is context for how quickly a single case can reframe the issue, not evidence about U.S. owners.

## Mechanisms to test

Ownership matters when it changes incentives, capacity, or market power. A local analysis should test these mechanisms rather than presume the result.

| Mechanism | Evidence to seek | Possible tenant or market outcome |
|---|---|---|
| Scale and standardized management | Portfolio size, shared management, centralized policies | Faster professional service, or less discretion and more automated enforcement |
| Debt and leverage | Mortgages, refinancing, debt-service pressure, maturity dates | Capital improvements, or pressure to raise revenue and reduce costs |
| Fees and ancillary revenue | Mandatory fees, utility billing, late charges, insurance products | Higher all-in housing cost and harder price comparison |
| Acquisition concentration | Purchases by related entities, overlapping portfolios, neighborhood share | Greater operating efficiency, or less competition and bargaining power |
| Investment horizon | Fund documents, offering materials, sale history | Planned improvements, refinancing, recapitalization, or sale pressure |
| Layered control | Record owner, parent entities, manager, local agent, lender | Specialized expertise, or obscured responsibility and slower accountability |

## Enforcement examples: conduct, not labels

Recent federal actions show the value of defining the practice at issue.

- In 2026, the FTC distributed more than $47 million to consumers after resolving allegations that Invitation Homes used undisclosed fees, failed to inspect some homes before move-in, and unfairly withheld deposits. [FTC: Invitation Homes refunds](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2026/03/ftc-sends-checks-totaling-more-472-million-consumers-deceived-invitation-homes-undisclosed-fees)
- In 2025, Greystar agreed to pay $24 million and change advertising practices in a federal and Colorado case concerning mandatory fees omitted from advertised rents. [FTC: Greystar settlement](https://www.ftc.gov/news-events/news/press-releases/2025/12/greystar-agrees-pay-24-million-stop-deceptive-advertising-practices-result-ftc-colorado-lawsuit)
- In 2025, the Justice Department proposed a settlement requiring RealPage to stop practices involving the use of landlords' competitively sensitive information in rental-pricing software. [DOJ: RealPage proposed settlement](https://www.justice.gov/opa/pr/justice-department-requires-realpage-end-sharing-competitively-sensitive-information-and)

These cases document specific allegations and remedies. They do not establish that every large, corporate, REIT, or private-equity landlord uses the same practices.

### Pending federal disclosure legislation

Rep. Kathy Castor (D-Fla.) introduced the Housing Price Transparency Act, which would require landlords and pricing firms to disclose when algorithmic tools are used to set rental prices, enforced through the FTC's unfair-and-deceptive-practices authority, with a parallel track letting states bring civil actions on tenants' behalf. It is a disclosure mandate, not a ban on algorithmic pricing, and it has been introduced but not enacted. [Nextgov: tech bills of the week](https://www.nextgov.com/policy/2026/08/tech-bills-week-overseeing-algorithm-set-rental-prices-and-halting-federal-overreach-data-center-construction/415569/) A federal disclosure requirement would sit alongside, not replace, the RealPage settlement and any state or local algorithmic-pricing rule Frederick or Maryland might adopt; it is a lead for the pricing-vendor column in the policy-levers table above, not yet a tool Council can rely on.

## Frederick: what is known and what remains to be built

The City of Frederick's rental-licensing page states that the city has more than 10,500 rental dwelling units. The city adopted the licensing program after identifying a lack of comprehensive information about rental location, ownership, and condition. The program provides address-based public license search and periodic inspection. [City of Frederick: Rental Licensing Ordinance](https://www.cityoffrederickmd.gov/1588/Rental-Licensing-Ordinance)

No verified local estimate has yet been added to this page for:

- the share of rental units controlled by the largest owners;

- the relationship between ownership type and rents, fees, code outcomes, maintenance, or eviction filings; or
- recent portfolio acquisitions and sales.

Until that work is complete, national percentages should not be presented as Frederick facts.

## Frederick ownership audit

### 1. Define the question before collecting names

Choose a unit of analysis: licensed rental unit, parcel, building, or portfolio. For most policy questions, count **units under common control**, not the number of LLCs. Set the geography and observation date. Define “large” or “institutional” explicitly and test more than one threshold.

### 2. Build a property-level base table

Request or export, where legally available:

- rental-license number and status;
- street address and parcel or property-account identifier;
- dwelling-unit count and property type;
- record owner and owner mailing address;
- property manager and local responsible agent;
- license issue, renewal, and inspection dates; and
- violation, reinspection, and resolution fields that can lawfully be released.

Join the licensing records to the Maryland State Department of Assessments and Taxation property database and business-entity search. These sources can identify record owners, mailing addresses, and registered entities, but they do not always reveal the ultimate beneficial owner. [Maryland SDAT: Real Property Search](https://sdat.dat.maryland.gov/RealProperty/Pages/default.aspx) · [Maryland Business Entity Search](https://egov.maryland.gov/BusinessExpress/EntitySearch)

### 3. Resolve common control

Group entities only when there is evidence, such as a shared parent disclosed in filings, a common address combined with other corroboration, a company portfolio list, an SEC filing, or a deed and financing pattern. Keep the record owner, manager, and controlling owner in separate fields.

Assign each match a confidence level:

- **Verified:** primary filing or company disclosure establishes control.
- **Probable:** multiple independent records align, but the parent is not directly disclosed.
- **Unresolved:** name or address similarity alone.

Manually review the largest portfolios. Do not publish personal home addresses or unsupported claims about control.

### 4. Measure outcomes

At minimum, calculate:

- units held by the largest 5 and 10 controlling owners;
- portfolio share by neighborhood and property type;
- acquisitions, transfers, and refinancing over time;
- advertised rent plus mandatory fees, when reliably available;
- inspection failures, repeat violations, time to correction, and unresolved cases;
- tenant complaints using a stable classification system; and
- eviction filings, judgments, and executed evictions as separate measures, if court data can be matched lawfully and accurately.

Compare like properties and time periods. Building age, subsidy status, unit count, neighborhood, and tenant population can affect outcomes independently of ownership.

### 5. Publish a reproducible result

Release the definition, date, source fields, matching rules, unresolved cases, and correction process with the findings. A dashboard should show trends and portfolio-level patterns without implying that correlation proves causation.

## Policy levers by level of government

| Level | Plausible tools | Main question before action |
|---|---|---|
| **City / county** | Rental licensing; ownership and responsible-agent fields; inspection and repeat-violation systems; transparent all-in price requirements where authorized; preservation acquisition partnerships; conditions on local subsidies or property dispositions | Does state law authorize the tool, and can staff administer and enforce it consistently? |
| **Maryland** | Entity and beneficial-ownership disclosure; landlord and fee rules; eviction-data access; tenant purchase or notice rights; attorney-general enforcement; enabling authority for local regulation | Is statewide uniformity needed, or should local governments receive tailored authority? |
| **Federal** | Antitrust enforcement; private-fund and securities disclosure; tax policy; fair-housing and consumer-protection enforcement; mortgage and GSE rules | Which agency controls the conduct, transaction, or source of capital? |

The most defensible local rules target measurable conduct or outcomes: hidden mandatory fees, failure to name a responsible party, repeated code violations, unlawful screening or eviction practices, and portfolio-level noncompliance. Ownership data remains essential because it reveals patterns that property-by-property enforcement can miss.

## REITs, merger review, and Vivmark

A REIT is a tax and ownership structure, not a synonym for private equity. The IRS administers the REIT tax rules through Form 1120-REIT. [IRS: About Form 1120-REIT](https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-1120-reit)

The claim that “REIT mergers do not face FTC pre-merger review” is too broad. Federal Hart-Scott-Rodino rules exempt certain acquisitions of office or residential real-property assets, while separately acquired non-real-estate assets can remain subject to review. Whether a transaction is reportable depends on its structure, thresholds, and applicable exemptions; REIT status alone does not create a blanket exemption. [16 C.F.R. § 802.2](https://www.ecfr.gov/current/title-16/chapter-I/subchapter-H/part-802/section-802.2)

Equity Residential and AvalonBay completed their merger on August 17, 2026, creating Vivmark, Inc. The companies reported approximately $53 billion in pro forma equity value, $71 billion in enterprise value, more than 180,000 apartment homes, and more than 10,000 homes under development. Those national figures establish scale. They do not establish ownership concentration or effects in Frederick; that requires checking the company's property list against the local ownership audit. [SEC Form 8-K](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/906107/000119312526354068/d372623d8k.htm)

## Decision checklist

Before recommending a policy, answer:

1. **Problem:** What measurable harm or market failure is occurring?
2. **Actor:** Is the responsible party the record owner, controlling owner, manager, lender, pricing vendor, or another intermediary?
3. **Evidence:** What is known locally, and what is inferred from another market?
4. **Authority:** Which level of government can act?
5. **Remedy:** Does the proposal address the identified mechanism?
6. **Tradeoffs:** Could the policy reduce rental supply, deter maintenance, shift costs, or accelerate sale or displacement?
7. **Administration:** What data, staffing, appeals, and enforcement are required?
8. **Evaluation:** What baseline and outcome measures will show whether it worked?
