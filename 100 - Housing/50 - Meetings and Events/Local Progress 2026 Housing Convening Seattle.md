# Local Progress 2026 Housing Convening Seattle

## Summary

Local Progress convened 70+ local elected officials in Seattle to share anti-displacement and affordable housing strategies at a moment when federal funding is disappearing and state and local governments must fill the gap.

## Key Points

The convening -- the first Local Progress housing convening in four years -- brought together members from across the country to pool knowledge on affordable housing and anti-displacement strategy. The timing was deliberate: federal housing funding is being gutted under the current administration, and state and local leaders are being asked to carry more of the weight.

Day one sessions covered community stabilization amid development, governing in collaboration with tenant unions, progressive revenue tactics to stretch public dollars, and a communications workshop on how to talk about housing strategy with colleagues and communities. Site visits gave members firsthand exposure to El Centro de la Raza and the Seattle Chinatown International District Preservation and Development Authority -- both examples of community-rooted organizations developing mixed-use affordable housing while preserving neighborhood identity.

Day two focused on what it means to govern under federal austerity: how to provide local housing support under authoritarianism, how to leverage public and underutilized land for housing, and how to preserve existing housing stock without federal dollars. Participants left with a shared framework: housing is a human right, and leading with values is the prerequisite to building any durable coalition around it.

## Relevance

The convening's themes map directly onto Frederick's active work. The federal funding gap is already showing up in the FY2027 budget discussions and the [[FY2027 Budget -- Work Session 2 April 9 Notes]]. The session on governing with tenant unions is relevant to Libby Taylor's push for stronger renter protections in the [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] process. The public land session connects to the broader question of whether Frederick has underutilized city-owned parcels that could support affordable housing without new infrastructure cost.

The framing -- local elected officials sharing what's actually working, at peer scale -- is the same framing César has used to justify the Housing Production Target Resolution. This convening is evidence that peer cities are moving.
