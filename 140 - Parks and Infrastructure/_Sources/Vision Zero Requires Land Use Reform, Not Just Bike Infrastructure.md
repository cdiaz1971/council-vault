# Vision Zero Requires Land Use Reform, Not Just Bike Infrastructure

## Summary

At a Durham, NC Bicycle and Pedestrian Advisory Commission meeting, city council members Matt Kopac and Carl Rist made a pointed argument to bicycle advocates: you cannot achieve Vision Zero traffic safety targets without changing land use policy. Reducing vehicle miles traveled (VMT) is the most effective lever for reducing traffic deaths, and reducing VMT requires enabling density -- which requires zoning reform. Bike lanes alone are insufficient.

## Key Points

The causal chain: sprawl increases VMT, VMT increases traffic deaths, bike infrastructure does not address VMT if the built environment still requires car trips for basic needs. Vision Zero, properly understood, is a land use policy as much as a transportation policy.

Durham's BPAC had focused its Land Development Code task force on the bicycle and pedestrian design elements of the code -- infrastructure standards, parking requirements -- but had not engaged with the broader question of where growth should be permitted and at what density. Council pushed the commission to recognize that those are the same question.

Councilmember Rist's framing was direct: the LDC vote is going to be a battle, and BPAC members should show up individually to support it because "it's not just about how the LDC can support bike and pedestrian infrastructure, but also how the built environment does support a much more safe and accessible way to get around the city."

Durham is not on track to meet its Vision Zero targets. Traffic deaths have not declined at the rate the 2017 resolution required.

The broader principle: advocates who focus only on the design elements of their issue (bike lanes, crosswalks, signal timing) while leaving land use to others are ceding the most important lever. The same logic applies to housing advocates, transit advocates, and anyone working on sustainable transportation.

## Relevance

Directly applicable to Frederick's LMC review. The argument that land use reform is transportation policy -- and therefore traffic safety policy -- is useful framing for connecting the LMC conversation to broader quality-of-life and safety outcomes. If Frederick is serious about walkability and reduced car dependence, the LMC has to enable the density that makes those things possible. Bike lanes in a sprawl pattern are amelioration, not transformation.
