## Core argument

San Francisco's proposed convenience store curfew reveals the defining tendency of contemporary urban governance: managing where suffering is visible rather than addressing why it exists. The city confronts concentrated poverty, addiction, and homelessness -- all predictable outcomes of housing-as-speculative-asset -- and responds by regulating store hours.

The author identifies this as "governance through management of visibility": the objective isn't eliminating root causes but controlling where problems can be seen and by whom.

## The structural critique

The essay names the causal chain clearly: housing transformed into a speculative asset → displacement of working class residents → public infrastructure sold off → mental health systems underfunded → stable employment scarce. These are the predictable outcomes of an economic order that treats human need as the least urgent claim on resources.

The policy response (curfew) bypasses this entirely and instead externalizes costs downward onto:
- **Immigrant-owned small businesses** operating on thin margins in disinvested corridors
- **Night-economy workers** (nurses, cooks, warehouse workers, delivery drivers) who depend on late-night services
- **Residents in crisis** who are treated as the crisis itself

## Collective punishment framing

The author frames the curfew as "collective punishment disguised as public administration" -- restrictions applied to entire communities because the city lacks will to address underlying conditions. This framing is directly applicable to Frederick: any time a zoning, land use, or operational rule targets a geography rather than a behavior, ask whose visibility is being managed.

## Relevance to Frederick

This is the diagnostic lens for evaluating policy proposals that use technical or public safety language to displace structurally vulnerable populations or restrict immigrant-owned businesses. The "management of visibility" framing catches what "disparate impact" analysis sometimes misses -- even formally neutral policies can function as governance-by-aesthetics when the target is the visibility of poverty, not poverty itself.
