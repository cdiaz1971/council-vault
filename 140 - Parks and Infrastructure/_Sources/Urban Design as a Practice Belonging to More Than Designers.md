# Urban Design as a Practice Belonging to More Than Designers

## Summary

An argument from a University of Utah urban design professor that the future of city-shaping depends on expanding who gets to claim the identity of "urban designer" -- not just credentialed professionals, but community members, health workers, educators, and advocates who shape urban outcomes through organizing, stewardship, and coalition-building.

## Key Points

The piece opens with Denver's decision to assume city responsibility for sidewalk maintenance after decades of leaving it to adjacent private property owners. The change was driven by a coalition of neighborhood organizations, public health professionals, and equity and disability advocates -- not professional urban designers. Robinson's argument: those coalition members were practicing urban design whether credentialed for it or not.

The central claim is that urban design has always been inherently cross-disciplinary -- architecture, landscape architecture, planning, engineering, public policy, social sciences -- and that the next evolution is expanding who gets to be a designer at all. The challenge facing cities (housing crisis, climate, transportation equity, public space) cannot be solved within any single professional boundary.

Three specific moves she advocates:

1. **Expand the role of trained designers.** Urban designers should become conveners, translators, and coalition builders -- not just makers of places. Success should be measured not only by the physical outcomes of design projects but by the capacity built in communities to shape their own environments.

2. **Make design methods accessible.** Walking tours, sketching exercises, counting benches, games that build vocabulary for talking about design -- these build urban design literacy in people who will never have a planning degree. The goal is not to make everyone an expert, but to give more people the confidence and tools to participate in decisions about the places they know best.

3. **Widen the identity.** The biggest shift is not methodological but identity-based: calling more people urban designers doesn't dilute professional expertise, it raises the design literacy floor across everyone shaping cities. "The future of urban design won't be determined solely by how many architects, planners, or landscape architects we educate. It will depend on how many people -- inside and outside those professions -- learn to think, collaborate, and act as urban designers."

The Denver sidewalk example is instructive: the question "who is responsible for the sidewalk?" turned out to be answered by community organizing, not by engineering expertise.

## Relevance

This maps directly onto how César operates in council work already -- not as a credentialed planner but as someone who shapes how the city builds, maintains, and governs its physical environment through policy and coalition-building. The Robinson framework provides language for that role.

It also connects to the community engagement dimension of the housing and LMC work. The challenge with the LMC Review Commission, the housing production resolution, and the vertical mixed-use text amendment is not primarily technical -- it's about whether the communities most affected by those decisions have the literacy and access to participate meaningfully. Walking tours, community sketch sessions, and design vocabulary games are not frivolous -- they are what shifts a public hearing from a formal compliance exercise to a genuinely participatory process.

The Better Block work ([[Better Block Foundation -- Tactical Urbanism and Show Don't Tell]] and [[Better Block Bulletin - July 2026]]) is an operational version of this same idea: use low-cost demonstrations to give community members a visceral experience of what different design choices feel like, rather than asking them to evaluate a technical drawing.
