# Show-Don't-Tell Urbanism -- Parking Space Demonstration Projects as Political Communication

## Summary

Fayetteville Strong built a one-bedroom and studio apartment on five parking spaces to let skeptics experience small housing before imagining it. The approach moves people beyond imagination limits by making the abstract concrete -- a technique directly applicable to Frederick's parking reform and missing middle conversations.

## Key Points

Reported from Strong Towns' National Gathering in Fayetteville, Arkansas (2026). The newsletter covers three storytelling strategies discussed at the gathering: short-form social video (advice: be authentic, rough around the edges often reaches more people than polished content), walking tours using show-and-tell street-level storytelling, and physical demonstration projects.

The standout example: Fayetteville Strong took over five parking spaces in a public lot and built two temporary structures -- a one-bedroom apartment (three parking spaces) and a studio apartment (two parking spaces). The goal was to let residents step inside small homes and experience them as reasonable spaces rather than imagining sardine cans. The project sparked new conversations among local government members before the National Gathering even ended.

The technique works because it breaks through the imagination barrier. People opposed to small housing, duplexes, or parking reform often cannot picture what they are opposing at human scale. A physical demonstration -- even temporary -- makes the abstract concrete and shifts the deliberation from speculation to experience.

Social video advice from the panel: authentic content that feels real and human often reaches more people than perfectly produced content. For a Council President with a Bluesky presence, short video from a site visit or community event often performs better than polished graphics.

## Relevance

Frederick's missing middle and parking reform conversations are exactly where this technique applies. A temporary demonstration of what an ADU or duplex looks like at human scale -- even as a pop-up at a community event -- would do more to shift the imagination of skeptical residents than any presentation slide.

The three-parking-space apartment is particularly useful framing for Frederick's West Patrick Street and downtown corridor discussions, where parking is politically charged. Rather than debating abstract parking minimums, the question becomes: would you trade three of these spaces for one of these homes?

The social video advice connects to César's Bluesky communications strategy. Authentic, slightly rough content from the field -- a site visit, a community meeting moment, a constituent story -- is the register that works on that platform.
