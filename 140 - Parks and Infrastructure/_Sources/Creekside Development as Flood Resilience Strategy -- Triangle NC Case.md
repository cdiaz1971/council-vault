# Creekside Development as Flood Resilience Strategy -- Triangle NC Case

## Summary

The Research Triangle's greenway-lined creek corridors could be transformed from flood-risk liabilities into connected, walkable development opportunities by reforming state riparian buffer rules that currently prohibit nearly all construction within 50 feet of waterways.

## Key Points

The Triangle region (Raleigh, Durham, Chapel Hill) was historically founded away from water -- ridge-line siting for railroad access, not waterfront development. The result is a region with extensive greenway trails along creek corridors but almost no waterfront activation: no restaurants, no buildings opening onto streamside paths, no civic life at the water's edge.

The barrier is North Carolina's riparian buffer requirements for the Neuse and Jordan Lake basins, which make it nearly impossible to build anything except a trail within 50 feet of streams and waterways. These rules protect water quality and aquatic habitat effectively -- but they also preclude the kind of creekside development that makes cities feel alive at the water.

The argument in the piece: these corridors already exist as public greenway infrastructure. The question is whether adjacent development can be shaped to engage with them rather than turn away from them. Flood risk is real but manageable through design -- elevation, setbacks calibrated to actual flood modeling, and building types that can tolerate occasional inundation.

## Relevance

Carroll Creek in Frederick is a direct parallel. The creek corridor through downtown is one of the city's most underutilized civic assets -- the flood control infrastructure is already in place, but the development along it is inconsistent in quality and activation. The Triangle case provides a peer-city frame for arguing that creekside land use reform is an economic development and livability strategy, not just a flooding or parks question. Worth pulling into any Carroll Creek or downtown planning conversation.
