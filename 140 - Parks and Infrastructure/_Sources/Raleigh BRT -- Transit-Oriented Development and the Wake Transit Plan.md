## What Raleigh's BRT Actually Does

BRT takes high-demand, high-transit-propensity corridors and upgrades them with:

- **Level boarding:** Speeds boarding, improves accessibility
- **Transit Signal Priority (TSP):** Buses get green light extensions or shorter reds
- **Queue jumps:** Buses skip through intersections ahead of traffic
- **Dedicated bus lanes** along key corridor segments
- **10-minute frequency** and expanded span of service
- **Sheltered stations** with real-time arrival displays, ticket vending, public art, and improved pedestrian/bike infrastructure

The program connects Cary, Garner, North Raleigh, and East Raleigh through four routes. Phase one is under construction along the New Bern Avenue corridor.

## The Wake Transit Plan

Originally adopted in 2016 (amended 2021 and 2025), the Wake Transit Plan introduced a dedicated 1/2-cent sales tax for transit. Before the plan, Raleigh had three high-frequency bus routes (15 minutes or less). Now it has 11. The plan was built on four principles: connect the region, connect all Wake County communities, create frequent/reliable urban mobility, and enhance access to transit.

Rausch recommends the National Transit Database for understanding transit funding structures.

## Land Use Integration

Raleigh's Comprehensive Plan explicitly identifies Frequent Transit Corridors and encourages density along those routes. This is the planning policy mechanism that links BRT to land use transformation -- not just running buses faster, but zoning the land around stations for the development that makes transit viable.

Rausch cites Jarrett Walker's *Human Transit* (second edition) as the key reference for understanding how transit and land use policy decisions interact. Walker's point: the way location affects costs can cause important destinations to spread out in ways that undermine transit -- not intentionally, but as a side effect of development practices.

## The Induced Demand Argument

Rausch makes the induced demand case explicitly: adding road lanes doesn't reduce traffic because more people start driving to fill the new capacity. The only way to actually reduce traffic is to make non-car alternatives genuinely faster, safer, and more convenient. BRT is the tool for doing that without light rail costs.

## Peer City Case Studies

- **Richmond, VA (PULSE BRT):** The Sauer Center -- a 37-acre mixed-use residential development -- is being built along the BRT line.
- **Atlanta, GA (MARTA BRT):** A city-issued RFP for an 11-acre residential development along the BRT corridor (September 2025).

Both show BRT as a development catalyst, not just a transit project.

## Why This Matters for Cesar

Frederick doesn't have BRT, but the underlying planning logic is directly relevant to the LMC conversation and corridor strategy. The argument that transit investment must be matched with transit-supportive land use policy -- density allowances, by-right approvals, reduced parking -- is the same argument for the Golden Mile and other Frederick corridors. The Wake Transit Plan's dedicated sales tax model is also worth noting as a peer-city funding mechanism.

Rausch's quote on perception change is quotable in community engagement contexts: investment in transit infrastructure shifts how people think about their transportation options.
