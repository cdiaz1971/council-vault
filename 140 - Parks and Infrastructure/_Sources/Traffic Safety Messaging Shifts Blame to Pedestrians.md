# Traffic Safety Messaging Shifts Blame to Pedestrians

## Summary

A pedestrian is killed by a vehicle every 72 minutes in the United States. Strong Towns founder Charles Marohn argues that standard public safety messaging around pedestrian deaths -- "look both ways," "wear bright clothing" -- shifts responsibility away from the dangerous systems that produce these outcomes and onto the individuals being harmed by them.

## Key Points

The problem is not inattentive pedestrians; it is road systems engineered for vehicle speed that predictably produce pedestrian deaths. When the response to that system is a PSA telling people to be more careful, it obscures the design failure and forecloses political pressure to fix the infrastructure.

This is a specific instance of a broader pattern: when systems cause harm, the path of least resistance for institutions is to educate the harmed rather than redesign the system. The messaging individualizes a structural problem.

The implication for local governments is that endorsing or amplifying these PSA narratives -- even with good intentions -- participates in protecting the status quo. Advocacy for safe street design requires explicitly naming the system, not the behavior.

## Relevance

Directly relevant to any Frederick conversation about pedestrian safety, street redesign, or the LMC. The framing Marohn offers is useful for council communications that want to shift the conversation from individual behavior to infrastructure accountability.
