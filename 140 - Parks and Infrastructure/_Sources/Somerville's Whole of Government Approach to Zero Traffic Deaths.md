# Somerville's Whole of Government Approach to Zero Traffic Deaths

## Summary
Somerville, Massachusetts has gone four years without a single traffic fatality, a result Vision Zero cities like Boston have not matched despite adopting the same policy framework. The city credits a whole of government approach layering dozens of small, cheap interventions rather than betting on any one big fix.

## Key Points
Somerville installed 139 striped speed humps on busy corridors since 2019, added flex posts and stop signs to chaotic traffic circles, and used flowerbeds and landscape planters to physically separate bike paths from car traffic. Somerville's director of mobility Brad Rawson frames it as making it near impossible to drive fast through street design rather than relying on signage or enforcement alone. The injury risk to pedestrians and cyclists spikes sharply once vehicle speed passes 35 mph, so the interventions target speed directly rather than just visibility or awareness.

The results are stark relative to peer cities. Serious crashes dropped 50 percent between 2024 and 2025. Boston and Cambridge, both larger cities that also subscribe to Vision Zero, recorded 560 and 112 serious crashes last year respectively, while Somerville has had just 46 serious injury crashes total since 2021. At least 48 people have died in Boston traffic incidents since Somerville's last fatality. Somerville also benefited from a major transit expansion, the Green Line extension, that reduced car dependency alongside the street redesigns.

## Relevance
This is a concrete example that a layered, cheap intervention approach to traffic safety outperforms symbolic policy adoption. Somerville and Boston both formally committed to Vision Zero, but only Somerville backed it with sustained physical infrastructure investment and dedicated staff. Directly useful for the E-Bike (EPMD) Ordinance thread, where [[Golden Mile Small Area Profile]] documents 576 car accidents and 25 severe or fatal injuries on that corridor from 2020 to 2024.
