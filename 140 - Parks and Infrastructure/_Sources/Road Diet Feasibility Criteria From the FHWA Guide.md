# Road Diet Feasibility Criteria From the FHWA Guide

## Summary
The FHWA Road Diet Informational Guide sets the screening numbers for converting four-lane undivided roads to three lanes: 20,000 vehicles per day or less as a good candidate, peak-hour directional volume under 750 as probably feasible, and an expected crash reduction of 19 to 47 percent.

## Key Points
A classic road diet converts a four-lane undivided road to two through lanes and a center two-way left-turn lane, freeing width for bike lanes, parking, transit stops, or pedestrian refuge islands. The safety benefit comes from removing left-turning vehicles from through lanes, shortening pedestrian crossings, and lowering speeds.

Crash reduction depends on context. Iowa small-town sites on US and state routes showed 47 percent; suburban corridors near larger cities in California and Washington showed 19 percent; the combined estimate is 29 percent. The guide says to pick the figure that matches the site type, so a suburban arterial should be argued at the lower end.

Traffic screening: FHWA advises that roads at or below 20,000 vehicles per day are good candidates and should be evaluated. Documented projects range from 8,500 to 24,000, and a Kentucky study found them workable up to 23,000. The real test is peak-hour volume in the peak direction, typically 8 to 12 percent of daily traffic. Iowa guidance: probably feasible at or below 750 vehicles per hour per direction, consider cautiously from 750 to 875, and less likely above 875. One sensitivity study found arterial level of service began declining at 1,750 vehicles per hour two-way, which signal timing optimization can partly offset. Closely spaced signals make congestion effects worse.

The guide also recommends reviewing resurfacing schedules to find candidate streets, since restriping during an overlay is the cheapest moment to reconfigure.

## Relevance
This gives the numbers needed to evaluate any Golden Mile or West Patrick Street reconfiguration proposal and to test staff or SHA claims about capacity. It also supports the E-Bike Ordinance and multimodal path work: a road diet is often how bike lanes get room. The resurfacing-schedule tactic is a low-cost ask for Public Works.
