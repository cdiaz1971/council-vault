# Deferred Road Maintenance Is a Structural Fiscal Problem, Not an Engineering One

## Summary
The pothole problem is not primarily technical -- it is fiscal. State and local governments have been in a cycle of deferred maintenance for decades, driven by stagnant gas tax revenue, rising construction costs, and federal infrastructure funding that outpaces neither inflation nor need. The Pew Charitable Trusts estimates a combined $86.3 billion state roadway maintenance shortfall over the next decade.

## Key Points
The federal gas tax is 18.4 cents per gallon and has not changed since 1993. It does not adjust for inflation or the growing fuel efficiency of modern vehicles. Fuel-efficient and electric vehicles pay proportionally less per mile driven, eroding the revenue base as the vehicle fleet improves.

Roughly 1 in 5 US roadways is currently in poor condition. The share of acceptable roads peaked at 91% in 1999 and has averaged around 80% in recent years. The worst performers: DC, Rhode Island, Hawaii, California, New Mexico (34-89% in poor condition). Best: Kansas, Tennessee, Indiana, Wyoming, Vermont (94-97% acceptable).

The maintenance funding gap creates a feedback loop: deferred repairs allow water infiltration, which causes more extensive deterioration, which eventually requires full reconstruction at 5-10x the cost of timely resurfacing.

AI and sensor-based road monitoring is being piloted in several cities. San Jose found 97% accuracy on pothole detection and 88% on roadway debris in a 2023-2025 pilot using camera-equipped city vehicles. Chicago launched a Samsara partnership for AI road operations monitoring. Massachusetts communities are running pothole detection software pilots. Hawaii uses volunteer dashcams.

Equity caveat: a 2024 Policy Studies Journal study found that in Houston, lower-income neighborhoods and communities with larger Black and Hispanic populations submitted fewer 311 pothole complaints despite often having worse road conditions. Complaint-based systems systematically underserve the communities that need the most repair.

## Relevance
Frederick City manages its own road infrastructure. The same gas tax stagnation and federal funding dynamics apply at the local level. The equity finding on 311 complaints is directly relevant to César's citizen participation and community engagement priorities -- a data-driven argument for proactive inspection over complaint-driven response.
