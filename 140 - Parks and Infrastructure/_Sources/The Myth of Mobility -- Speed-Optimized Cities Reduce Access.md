# The Myth of Mobility -- Speed-Optimized Cities Reduce Access

## Summary

Cities that measure mobility by vehicle speed often end up reducing genuine accessibility, particularly for people who cannot drive. Separating residential zones from commercial uses and optimizing roads for throughput produces cities where nearly every basic activity requires a car -- making life harder, not easier, for a significant share of residents.

## Key Points

Late-twentieth-century American urbanism separated residential and commercial uses by default, placing homes in residential zones and shops, services, and workplaces far away along commercial corridors. This produced cities where nearly every basic daily activity requires a car trip. On paper, vehicle throughput is high. In practice, accessibility is low.

The populations most harmed are those who cannot drive easily: older adults, children, people with disabilities, and those who cannot afford a vehicle. For them, the distance between home and daily needs becomes a structural barrier. Car-centric environments -- wide arterials, vast parking lots, spread-out retail -- are also physically exhausting and psychologically uncomfortable to navigate even for those who can drive.

True mobility should not be measured by how fast people travel but by how easily they can reach what they need. A city where everything requires a fast car trip may still be a city where nothing is reachable without one.

## Why This Matters for César

This connects directly to Frederick's LMC review and transportation equity discussions. Frederick has a car-dependent street network and commercial pattern across much of the city. The LMC determines whether future development can shift that pattern through mixed use, reduced parking requirements, and design standards that support pedestrian access. This framing -- that speed optimization paradoxically reduces accessibility -- is useful in public conversations about zoning reform for residents who don't connect those policy levers to lived experience.
