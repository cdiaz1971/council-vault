# Interstates and the Road Not Taken -- New Hampshire's Alternative Growth Pattern

## Summary

In much of America, the interstate highway system reshaped communities around vehicle speed and large-scale infrastructure. New Hampshire's less complete highway buildout created conditions where some communities developed along different lines. The results offer evidence that the dominant growth pattern was a choice, not an inevitability.

## Key Points

The interstate system did not just move people faster -- it actively restructured land use, favoring dispersal over density and speed over access. Communities that didn't fully adopt this model were sometimes left with more walkable, human-scale urban fabric as a byproduct.

The New Hampshire case is useful precisely because it is not a planned alternative -- it is an accident of incomplete infrastructure investment that inadvertently preserved a different kind of place. This makes it a stronger argument than designed experiments, because it happened under real conditions.

The implication is that the form of infrastructure a community builds or tolerates shapes what kind of community it becomes over decades. Local land use decisions -- including Frederick's LMC work -- are infrastructure decisions with long time horizons.

## Relevance

Useful background for any conversation about transportation infrastructure and land use in Frederick. The LMC is fundamentally a question about what kind of community Frederick is building -- the New Hampshire framing puts that in long-run perspective.
