# Frederick Water, Sewer, and Stormwater

## How the system is run and paid for

The City of Frederick operates its own drinking water, wastewater, and stormwater systems. Council records reference the City wastewater treatment plant, the Linganore Water Treatment Plant, and at least 13 water and sewer facilities ([[Hearing Minutes 2026-08-06]]; [[Hearing Brief 2026-08-20]]).

- **Enterprise funds.** Water and Sewer and Stormwater are separate enterprise funds, paid for mainly through rates and fees rather than taxes. The proposed FY2027 budget expects every enterprise fund to be self-supporting, with no General Fund subsidy. That gives the Council less discretionary leverage than it has over governmental funds ([[FY 2027 Budget -- City Fund Types Reference]]).
- **Debt.** Water and sewer debt is exempt from the Charter's 8%-of-assessed-value cap on general obligation (GO) debt. The late-2023 ~$47.76M GO issue funded stormwater projects among others, with bonds rated AA+ stable by Fitch ([[Frederick Bond Information]]).
- **Development fees.** Water/sewer impact fees are one of six fee programs covered by G-26-11, the Housing Certainty Act implementation ordinance. Under G-26-11, the City may collect them only as a precondition of final inspection and no more than 30 days before it. ([[Hearing Brief 2026-09-03]]). A separate housing recommendation would defer, or waive at ≤60% AMI, water and sewer connection fees for deed-restricted affordable units ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]).
- **Recent capital and procurement:**
  - $1.45M contract plus a $1.55M purchase order for OSHA fall-protection upgrades at 13 water and sewer facilities (CIP 130003) ([[Hearing Brief 2026-08-20]])
  - $75,549 for nitrate instruments at the wastewater plant ([[Hearing Minutes 2026-08-06]])
  - $86,375 for a vertical turbine pump at Linganore WTP ([[Hearing Minutes 2026-08-06]])
- **Supply.** At the June 11 data center work session, the City's Economic Development Director cautioned that large projects are unlikely in Frederick "given limited land and water supply" ([[Behind the Spires City Update 07-07-2026]]). The Potomac basin supplies about 75% of the region's drinking water. The Interstate Commission on the Potomac River Basin (ICPRB) projects that data-center consumptive use could stress supply reliability in drought by 2050, and it names Frederick County as a site of rapid expansion ([[Data Centers -- Potomac River Basin Water Impact Projections]]).

## Wastewater: the phosphorus problem

The City's wastewater plant exceeded its annual phosphorus discharge limit for the **fourth time in five years**. In 2025 it discharged 10,827 lb against a Maryland Department of the Environment (MDE) limit of 5,720 lb, about 1.9 times the limit. MDE called the violations "significant in nature" and was evaluating penalties of up to $10,000 per day. The City attributed the exceedances to higher influent phosphorus that the plant's bioaugmentation reactors struggle to process, and it is working with Moonshot Missions consultants on plant operations. Phosphorus drives the algae blooms and oxygen loss that harm the Chesapeake Bay ([[Frederick Wastewater Plant -- Chronic Phosphorus Discharge Violations]]).

The plant also reuses treated effluent: Clustered Spires golf course irrigation took 16–31 million gallons a year from 2020 to 2025 ([[Frederick Golf Course Effluent Irrigation Data -- Water Use Comparison for Data Center Debate]]).

## Stormwater and storm drains

- **Mason's Law (SB 189, signed May 13, 2026).** The law is named for Mason Kearns, 13, of Mount Airy, who died after being swept into an open storm drain in July 2025. It was sponsored by Frederick's Sen. Karen Lewis Young. As reported, it requires municipalities to:
  - inventory open storm drains by **July 1, 2027**
  - adopt a risk-based inlet prioritization plan by **April 1, 2028**
  - finish repairs to public and private inlets by **April 1, 2031**, with private owners maintaining approved systems afterward

  ([[Masons Law -- Maryland Storm Drain Inspection Requirements]]). *confidence: medium. The deadlines come from a news roundup, not the bill text.*
- **Permeable surfaces.** Asphalt parking can run up to 20°F hotter and carries oil and metals into waterways. Porous concrete, pavers, bioswales, and shading requirements are the main alternatives. New Orleans requires permeable paving in public lots where practical, and Sacramento requires 50% lot shading within 15 years. These alternatives cost more upfront but last longer, and heavy-vehicle durability is a real engineering concern ([[Permeable Parking as Stormwater and Heat Island Mitigation]]).
- **Stormwater in capital projects.** The Monocacy Blvd improvements design (CIP 320037) added stormwater management scope in September 2026 ([[Hearing Brief 2026-09-17]]).
- **Construction sediment enforcement.** In Harford County, Maryland secured $4.1M from D.R. Horton, Forestar, and Kinsley over repeated sediment discharges from Ridgely's Reserve into the Gunpowder River: a $2M civil penalty plus $2.1M in remediation administered by the Chesapeake Bay Trust. The Gunpowder Riverkeeper joined as intervenor. No company admitted liability ([[Developer Sediment Enforcement -- Maryland $4.1M Gunpowder River Settlement]]).

## Flooding and floodplain development

- **Carroll Creek levee.** On June 4, 2026 the Council approved 6-0 a $395,000 Bayland Consultants contract (CIP 350024) to analyze three Carroll Creek levee segments for FEMA/Army Corps certification (44 CFR §65.10). The goal is FEMA accreditation and an updated Letter of Map Revision (LOMR). Staff identified 22 properties in NAC 12 (East Church Street and Highland Avenue) that face mandatory flood-insurance reclassification without accreditation. The packet's equity statement read "N/A" ([[Hearing Brief 2026-06-04]]; [[Hearing Notes 2026-06-04]]).
- **Gateway Property (special events center).** Much of the proposed field sits in FEMA 100- and 500-year flood hazard areas and requires fill. The rezoning (G-26-7) passed 5-1 on August 6, 2026. Its conditions bar public use until MDE issues a Nontidal Wetlands and Waterways Permit for floodplain grading; if MDE denies it, the facility must relocate or the project doesn't proceed ([[Hearing Brief - Gateway Property 2026-08-06]]; [[Hearing Minutes 2026-08-06]]). A public commenter raised the risk of downstream flooding outside City limits ([[Workshop Brief 2026-06-25]]).
- **Husky Park.** Per the City's floodplain layer, about 1.04 acres (4.6%) of the parcel lies in the FEMA AE zone. MDE and FEMA conditional map revision (CLOMR) review are conditions of construction, not of bid authorization ([[Workshop Brief - Husky Park Rebuttal 2026-08-27]]).

## Affordability: shutoffs and utility assistance

- **Existing practice.**
  - The City doesn't start a residential shutoff unless the balance exceeds $350.
  - Payment plans are available on request.
  - A one-time leak-forgiveness program credited 55 accounts $68,742 in FY2025, and 26 accounts $48,038 in FY2026 through Feb 17.
  - Customers can manage accounts online through InvoiceCloud.
  
  ([[Water Shutoff Prevention -- Frederick Existing Programs and Proposed Legislative Actions]])
- **In development.** Finance and HHS are developing an engagement-based residential assistance program. Nash identified three legislative asks: (1) authority to waive interest for customers actively engaged in repayment, (2) authority to modify or waive turn-on/turn-off fees, and (3) codification of the assistance program. César's April 9 reply favored moving (1) and (2) first ([[Water Shutoff Prevention -- Frederick Existing Programs and Proposed Legislative Actions]]).
- **Council positions.** Finance first floated shutoff prevention at the April 8 budget session. Shackelford opposed it, citing non-payment abuse; César supported it ([[Budget Notes 2026-04-08]]).
- **Roadmap (Notion, checked 2026-09-23).**
  - Water Utility Assistance Fund: Pre-Legislative, target July 1, 2028
  - Water Utility Bill Voluntary Check-Off: Committee Review
  - Turn-On/Turn-Off Fee authority: Pre-Legislative, not yet drafted
  - Program codification: Pre-Legislative, not yet drafted
  
  ([[Legislative Roadmap FY2027]])

## Regional lesson: the 2026 Potomac Interceptor collapse

On January 19, 2026, DC Water's Potomac Interceptor collapsed and released an estimated **300 million gallons** of raw sewage into the Potomac. The spill was contained to the C&O Canal by early March, with a temporary fix on March 14 and a permanent repair planned by year's end.

- **Lingering effects.** E. coli spiked above recreation thresholds after rain for months, because pathogens persist in sediment. The spill deposited nitrogen and phosphorus at levels comparable to the worst recent years, and PFAS effects won't be clear until 2027. Drinking-water intakes were upstream and unaffected.
- **Accountability.** DOJ and the Maryland Attorney General sued DC Water on April 20, and American Rivers named the Potomac the nation's most endangered river.
- **Takeaways the researchers stressed:** there was no mechanism to flag the pipe as a failure risk beforehand; EPA monitoring ended by May 1 despite open questions; and independent monitoring depended on time-limited university funding.
