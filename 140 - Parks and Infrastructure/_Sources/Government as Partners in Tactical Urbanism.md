# Government as Partners in Tactical Urbanism

## Summary
Better Block's seventh impact pillar argues that temporary demonstrations become permanent investment when government acts as a partner instead of a gatekeeper. Its lead example is Prince George's County, Maryland, where pop-up projects turned shelved town center plans into a permanent civic plaza and programmed public space.

## Key Points
In Prince George's County during 2022 and 2023, master plans for walkable town centers had been sitting unused while residents lived with disconnected streets and empty lots. Rather than commissioning another report, the county brought a traveling Better Block in a Box to Suitland, Largo, and Riverdale with temporary plazas, pop-up storefronts, and gathering spaces. Seeing residents use the spaces moved county leaders from hesitation to commitment. Largo now has a permanent civic plaza under construction on the demonstration site, Suitland kept its landmark letters and turned an empty lot into a programmed venue, and a Riverdale mural from the project remains on a local business.

In West Allis, Wisconsin, a 2019 weekend activation of the car-dominated Burnham Pointe corridor brought pop-up businesses, music, a natural playground, and traffic calming. The pandemic slowed follow-through, but the project led to Burnham Pointe Park and pushed the city toward permanent traffic calming.

In South Dallas in 2021, city food codes effectively blocked food truck vendors from preparing meat and poultry on site. Better Block and TREC Community Investors ran a temporary MLK Food Park that drew more than 5,500 people, and that demonstrated demand helped persuade the Dallas City Council to change the rule.

The common thread is that a municipality willing to suspend its usual rules for a test gets real-world evidence that can justify permanent investment or a code change.

## Relevance
The Prince George's County case is the closest Maryland precedent for using tactical demonstrations to unstick plans that already exist on paper, which is directly useful for [[Frederick Urbanism and Placemaking]] and for framing the [[Tactical Traffic Calming Program -- Frederick]] as a city partnership rather than a pilot the city merely tolerates. The Dallas food truck example also shows the model working as a path to regulatory reform, not only physical change, which extends the argument in [[Better Block Proving Market Demand Through Tactical Urbanism]]. It pairs naturally with [[Better Block -- Community as Co-Creator Framework]] as the government side of the same relationship.
