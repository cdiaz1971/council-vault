# Frederick Parks and Recreation

The City of Frederick's Department of Parks and Recreation, directed by Bob Smith, runs a system of 73 developed parks and 12 undeveloped properties. Actual spending was about $12.2M in FY2025 ([[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]], [[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]). Three things drive the policy picture. The 2023 Master Plan puts renovation of existing parks ahead of expansion, and the CIP has largely followed it. The plan's own numbers show a parkland shortfall as the city grows. The department's main tool for low-income access delivers a thin benefit per household.

## The 2023 Master Plan

The Parks and Recreation Master Plan sets a 5–10 year roadmap for parks, facilities, programs, trails, and operations. It's meant to plug into the Comprehensive Plan and the CIP ([[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]]). The plan year, 2023, comes from the Husky Park research, which read the plan directly ([[Workshop Brief - Husky Park Rebuttal 2026-08-27]]). The vault's summary note doesn't date it.

**Five core goals:** improve organizational efficiency; increase financial opportunities and sustainability; strengthen recreation programs; invest in existing and new facilities system-wide; and expand trails and greenways ([[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]]).

**What residents said** in a statistically valid survey, forums, focus groups, and interviews: maintain existing parks better, build more trails and greenways, communicate and sign parks more clearly, and focus on youth engagement and equitable access ([[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]]).

**Problems the plan identifies:** inconsistent signage, confusion over park ownership and responsibility, staffing shortages and pay inequities, aging facilities and deferred maintenance, and little ability to track usage ([[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]]).

**Capital priorities** drawn from the plan, in order: renovate existing parks (playgrounds, courts, restrooms, ADA, lighting); expand trails and greenways; target improvements in equity emphasis areas; upgrade the whole system (signage, design standards, usage and asset tracking); build new facilities only where growth and documented gaps call for them; add sustainability measures (stormwater, canopy, efficient lighting); and build space that supports programming (indoor and multi-use) ([[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan  Priorities]]). This list is César's January 2026 reading of what the plan implies, not a table taken from the plan.

## Inventory and parkland supply

The plan's GIS table (p. 167, "2020 GIS Acres") counts **928 acres**, a ratio of **12.3 acres per 1,000 residents** (2020 population 75,281). Holding that ratio at the 2025 projected population of 79,344 takes **978 acres, or 50 more**. The table excludes about 70 acres of undeveloped parkland ([[Husky Park Master Plan Figures To Verify 2026-08-27]]). The plan's prose uses a rounded ~950 acres ([[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]]). The 928 figure is the one the plan uses in its own calculation. The Comprehensive Plan has a separate 114-acre, 25-year need figure built on a different method (NRPA standard, longer horizon). It's a different measure, not a conflicting one ([[Husky Park Master Plan Figures To Verify 2026-08-27]]).

The **12 undeveloped properties** total about 69 acres. In order of size: Husky Park (23), Parkland 2 (17), Parkland 10 (8), Parkland 12 (6), Hillcrest Orchard Park (4), Airport Parkland (4), Parkland 8 (3), Parkland 6 (2), Parkland 11 (1), Parkland 5 (1), Parkland 7 (0.2), and Parkland 3 (0.1) ([[Husky Park Master Plan Figures To Verify 2026-08-27]]).

On national benchmarks, the April 2026 budget materials put the department above NRPA averages: 11.5 acres per 1,000 residents (national 10.2), 1,139 residents per park (national 2,411), and 336 programs a year (national 250), with every resident within 0.5–1 mile of a park ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]).

> **confidence: low.** The plan's 12.3 acres per 1,000 and the budget materials' 11.5 don't match, and neither source explains why. The acreage and population denominators behind each haven't been confirmed. A newer population figure would account for the gap, but that's an inference. The two framings also point different ways: above the national average, and short of the plan's own growth target. Both can be true at once.

Parkland supply also grows through development review. Under LMC Section 608, a project that can't dedicate parkland on site pays a fee-in-lieu. The Worman's Mill Court Apartments item on the September 17, 2026 agenda set that fee at $240,000 for 2.93 acres, based on a fair-market appraisal. The developer is counting 0.58 acres of private amenities (tot lot, dog park, community gardens) toward the requirement ([[Hearing Brief 2026-09-17]]).

## Husky Park and CIP 320044

Husky Park, at 530 Highland Street, is a 22.58-acre parcel zoned PRK. It's currently a DPW storage yard with no built park amenities. It's also the largest of the City's undeveloped park properties, close to a third of that land bank. The Master Plan rates it low priority for development: "Area is well served and limited residential near this property lowers the priority for this property" ([[Workshop Brief - Husky Park Rebuttal 2026-08-27]], [[Husky Park Master Plan Figures To Verify 2026-08-27]]). CIP 320044 would extend Bond Street through the parcel. A separate unfunded line, CIP 120009 ("Relocation of Husky Park," $1.25M), would move the DPW yard off the site ([[Workshop Brief - Husky Park Rebuttal 2026-08-27]]).

Resident Kevin Hluch has challenged the project on ten stated prerequisites. The strongest are a 1995 forest conservation easement whose 1994 boundary plan (FCP 94-8) can't be located, and the lack of any Phase I or II environmental assessment on a former industrial site. At the September 10, 2026 work session, staff corrected the record: the Renn Final Forest Conservation Plan had *not* extinguished the 1995 easement, so a formal release will be needed ([[Workshop Brief 2026-09-10]]). How much land the road permanently takes isn't established. The only cited figure, 3.96 acres, is a construction-disturbance footprint from a 2021 engineering drawing that isn't in the record ([[Workshop Brief - Husky Park Rebuttal 2026-08-27]]). Status is tracked in the Notion Legislative Roadmap.

## The CIP park portfolio

The current CIP park projects, as summarized in January 2026 ([[CIP Park Projects]]):

- **Playground Replacement Program (CIP #410034):** multi-year and citywide. Recent and planned sites include South End and Cobblestone.
- **Hill Street Park Renovation (CIP #410039):** restrooms, skate park, playground, fields, and paths. The skate park design/build contract was awarded in March 2026, with completion expected in late winter 2027 ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]).
- **Sports Court Replacement and Renovation (CIP #410032):** College Estates, Lucas Village, Overlook, Clerestory, and Lake Coventry.
- **Park Improvements, Equity Emphasis Areas (CIP #410038):** targeted upgrades in underserved neighborhoods.
- **Individual projects:** Riverside Center, Avalon, Clover Ridge, Renn, Kellerton, Mullinix, McCurdy Field, Baker Park Covered Bridge, fountain renovations, Senior Park, Millie's Delight, and the Community Bridge restoration (CIP 410037, see below).
- **Related trail and connectivity work** under transportation programs: the Shared Use Path Plan, Rails-to-Trails, North Branch Trail, and Golden Mile multimodal access.

> **confidence: medium.** The CIP cycle behind this list isn't recorded in the source note. It's likely the FY2026–2031 book. Hill Street is spelled "Hillstreet" in the CIP note and "Hill Street" in the budget brief. It's treated here as one park and one project.

The completed and near-completed renovation list (Hill Street, Lucas Village, College Estates, Overlook, Linden Hills) is weighted away from downtown. That contrasts with the downtown-weighted pattern flagged in the broader citywide capital list at the April 8 budget session ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]).

**Westside Regional Park Community Center** was at 60% design in April 2026, with design completion expected in FY2027 and construction procurement after that. Total cost and procurement timing were open questions at the hearing ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]).

**Community Bridge restoration (CIP 410037)** on Carroll Creek is a $1.355M mural restoration contract in active dispute with the artist. It's covered in its own brief and not repeated here ([[FY2027 -- Community Bridge Restoration Project -- Overview]]).

## How well the CIP tracks the plan

César's January 2026 comparison graded the CIP against each plan priority ([[Parks Plan vs Current CIP]]):

| Plan priority | Assessment |
|---|---|
| Renovate existing parks | Strong alignment |
| Trails and greenways | Strong alignment |
| Equity-focused improvements | Partial. Projects exist, but equity framing isn't consistent across investments |
| System-wide improvements (signage, data, standards) | Weak. The CIP is site-by-site |
| Strategic new facilities | Limited. No acquisitions or new builds visible |
| Sustainability | Indirect. Embedded, but not a capital category |
| Programming-supportive space | Partial. Outdoor is well served, indoor and multi-use is not |

Gaps it names: a system-wide signage and wayfinding project, usage-data and asset-management technology, long-range land acquisition, visible sustainability capital projects, and indoor or flexible recreation space. Risks: continued reactive investment, equity goals getting diluted without tracking, population growth outpacing capacity, and operating and staffing costs lagging behind capital. Options it lists for Council: require CIP projects to cite plan priorities, add a system-wide wayfinding project, create a visible equity parks category, score park capital on sustainability, and start planning for acquisition or an indoor facility ([[Parks Plan vs Current CIP]]). The land-acquisition gap connects directly to the 50-acre shortfall above.

## Operating budget, FY2027

The department's full actual spending was $9.2M in FY2023, $10.3M in FY2024, and $12.2M in FY2025. The memo's summary totals ($1.44M expenditures, $1.23M revenues) cover only part of the department and shouldn't be used for comparison ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]). Cost pressures in the FY2027 proposal:

- Parks Division salaries up 7% ($2.09M to $2.24M).
- Electricity up 25% ($150K to $202.5K), with no narrative explanation.
- Workers' compensation at $123K, the highest of any department reviewed at that hearing.
- Overtime budgeted at $85K against volatile actuals ($113K in FY2024, $79K in FY2025).
- Capital outlay at $235K, up from $60K, for vehicles, mowers, Talley Recreation Center HVAC and flooring, and Diggs Pool furniture.

One new position was requested: a **Recreation Coordinator for Youth Programming**. Program registration is trending toward 30% growth. Afterschool programs now run at four FCPS elementary schools (Butterfly Ridge, Parkway, Monocacy, and Waverley), and early childhood programming launched this year ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]).

> **confidence: low.** The division structure differs between sources. The Master Plan summary lists Golf Course, Special Events, Parks, and Recreation ([[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]]). The FY2027 budget materials list Parks, Recreation Centers, Golf, and Urban Forestry ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]). This may reflect the FY2026 reorganization the budget brief mentions, but that isn't confirmed.

## Low-income access: the Reduced Registration Program

The department's main low-income access tool gives reduced program fees to households that qualify for free or reduced school meals. FY2026 enrollment was up 102% at the time of the April 2026 hearing. But 399 qualifying households had received $8,767 in total savings, about **$22 per household**. Whether that reflects a low per-program subsidy, low uptake, or a funding ceiling was an open question at the hearing ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]).

## Urban forestry

Maryland's Tree Solutions Now Act (2021) commits the state to 5 million native trees by 2031. At least 10% must go to underserved urban areas, defined by income, unemployment, and redlining history. That share is administered through Chesapeake Bay Trust grants. About 1.5 million trees had been planted by the FY2025 report. The main weakness is survival: roughly 30% of new trees die within a few years from deer, invasive species, and lack of follow-up care ([[Maryland 5 Million Trees Initiative -- Urban Forestry and Equity]]).

Frederick has used this channel. A **$494,000 Chesapeake Bay Trust Urban Trees Grant** funds 1,190 trees in underserved areas identified by the 2021 DNR Urban Tree Canopy Assessment. Planting began in March 2026 ([[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]). The state's survival-rate problem means maintenance after planting matters as much as the grant does.

## State park access and fee models

Maryland DNR piloted mandatory advance registration at five high-demand state parks in summer 2025 after parks were filling before mid-morning. The pilot handled 67,000 reservations ([[Maryland State Parks -- Reservation System Expansion and QR Entry Gates]]). Revenue rose 352% over honor-box collection with no fee change, a sign of how much informal collection misses ([[Pay-Per-Use Park Registration as a Municipal Revenue and Access Tool]]). The 2026 expansion includes **Gambrill State Park in Frederick**. QR payment gates replace unmanned honor boxes at lower-demand sites and log a timestamped entry for each visitor ([[Maryland State Parks -- Reservation System Expansion and QR Entry Gates]]).

The vendor, Kaizen Labs, takes 23% of each entry fee, adding about $0.70–$1.15 per visitor on a $3–$5 entry. The state estimates Kaizen earns about $4M through 2031 against about $18M to the state, or about $725K in net new revenue. The Board of Public Works approved it 2–1. Comptroller Lierman dissented over the lack of competitive bidding while saying she supported the goal ([[Pay-Per-Use Park Registration as a Municipal Revenue and Access Tool]]). DNR had pulled an earlier version of the item after board pushback ([[Maryland State Parks -- Reservation System Expansion and QR Entry Gates]]).

For a city system, the transferable points are the revenue lesson about informal collection, the lighter-weight QR option for facilities that don't need capacity management, and the procurement lesson. A per-transaction vendor fee is ultimately paid by visitors, which matters for access at an equity-focused system. The two sources describe the same program 10 days apart and agree on the facts.

## Council items in the pipeline

As of the June 2026 local roadmap, all parks items were Pre-Legislative. Current status lives in the Notion Legislative Roadmap ([[Legislative Roadmap FY2027]]):

- **Park in Lieu Programming** (Whole Council): absorb derelict HOA common space as parkland, activate parks with public art in Equity Emphasis neighborhoods, and improve accessibility and connectivity.
- **Waverley Elementary Parkland Activation** (President Letter): planned for FY2028.
- **Westside Regional Park, County Assistance** (President Letter): ask the County to fund it as a regional park, and look at directing interest earnings to community assets there.
- **Baker Park Baseball Infrastructure** (Public Works): remove the decrepit baseball infrastructure.
- **Trees as Heat Shields for Playgrounds** (Public Works): shade trees at playgrounds where possible.
- **Husky Park, CIP 320044** documentation dispute: Study Underway, High priority, per the 2026-09-10 workshop brief ([[Workshop Brief 2026-09-10]]).
