# Why Commercial Storefronts Stay Vacant -- Eight Mechanisms

From Strong Towns, May 2025. Vacant ground-floor retail is a chronic complaint in new mixed-use development and older commercial corridors alike. The causes are structural, not accidental.

## The Eight Reasons

**1. Developer indifference.** Ground-floor commercial is often included in infill projects because planning requirements or community expectations demand it, not because the developer has genuine commercial expertise or interest. When the developer does not care whether the space leases, it often does not.

**2. Poor architectural design.** Retail spaces added as an afterthought tend to be poorly proportioned -- wrong ceiling heights, awkward column placement, insufficient storefront glazing, or loading access that doesn't work. A space that looks like retail on the exterior may be unusable as retail for most tenants.

**3. Mismatched marketing.** Large developers without in-house commercial leasing expertise default to national brokerage firms that specialize in "credit tenants" -- chains and franchises with strong balance sheets. These brokers have no incentive or capacity to find the local operators, pop-ups, or emerging businesses that would actually activate the space. The result is that spaces sit waiting for a national tenant that never comes.

**4. Tenant improvement costs.** Developers deliver commercial space in varying states of completion. A "cold dark shell" is essentially just walls and structure -- no HVAC, no electrical distribution, no floor finish. A "warm shell" adds HVAC and basic electrical. A "white box" or "vanilla box" is finished to a paintable state with functional systems and basic bathrooms. The less finished the delivery condition, the more the tenant must invest before opening -- which screens out exactly the small, undercapitalized operators most likely to activate a space.

**5. Rent expectations set too high.** Developers often pro forma commercial rents based on comparable spaces in established markets, not the actual market where the building sits. Holding out for those rents produces vacancy rather than a lower-rent tenant who would activate the space and generate pedestrian activity.

**6. Inflexible lease terms.** Short-term leases, high security deposits, and personal guarantee requirements are standard in commercial leasing but act as barriers for small and emerging businesses. Developers rarely offer structures that accommodate the risk profile of a new local operator.

**7. Regulatory barriers.** Health permits, liquor licenses, signage restrictions, and use limitations can make a technically available space legally unusable for the tenant most interested in it.

**8. Legacy vacancy as self-reinforcing signal.** An empty storefront signals to prospective tenants that the location doesn't work. Vacancy breeds more vacancy, particularly in corridors where foot traffic is already low.

## Why This Matters for César

This taxonomy is directly useful for any Frederick economic development or downtown corridor discussion. The policy levers available at the local level include: adjusting use requirements in the LMC to allow more flexibility in what commercial spaces can contain; creating a small business tenant improvement grant program; and working with the planning department on delivery condition standards for required ground-floor commercial in mixed-use projects. The vacancy problem is often diagnosed as a market failure when it's actually a policy and design failure.
