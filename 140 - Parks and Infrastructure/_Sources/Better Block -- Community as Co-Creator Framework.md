# Better Block -- Community as Co-Creator Framework

## Summary

Better Block Foundation's April 2026 bulletin focuses on their "Community as Co-Creator" principle, drawn from their 10-year impact report. The core argument is that residents are not subjects of planning processes but partners and authors. Two case studies -- East Point, Georgia and Mesa, Arizona -- illustrate how temporary tactical urbanism activations unlock lasting institutional change.

## Key Arguments

Better Block defines co-creation as residents being partners at every stage, from first sketch to final build. Their role is convener and catalyst, not commander. The power to transform a place already exists in the community -- the job is to unlock it.

The East Point case: a city struggling with disconnection between plans and community participation used a hands-on activation with Microsoft support. Community members and city staff painted sidewalks and built parklets side by side. The result was proof of concept that temporary projects reveal strong community support and give city leaders confidence to invest in ongoing programming.

The Mesa case is more instructive politically. Residents in the Southside neighborhood used a temporary activation -- bike lane, painted crosswalk, artisan mercado -- to make the city's broken permitting process visible. By confronting the system directly, they forced the city to acknowledge barriers and become a partner rather than a gatekeeper. The permitting process was reformed. A recurring Mercado Mesa launched. A permanent crosswalk is in development.

Current projects include a 39-day Main Street activation in downtown Dallas during a major football event, and a community park build at West Trinity Heights.

The urban highway cost question: Better Block points to a Bloomberg analysis by Dallas planner Patrick Kennedy on the economic toll of downtown freeways, and to Paris as a model for highway-to-people-space conversion.

## Relevance

The co-creator framework maps directly onto Frederick's community engagement challenges. The pattern of temporary activations creating proof points for permanent investment is useful for parks and placemaking arguments -- particularly around [[Parks and Infrastructure CIP]] and any future tactical urbanism pilots. The Mesa permitting reform story is directly relevant: Frederick's own permitting and development review processes are a recurring friction point. The data collection piece -- collecting traffic, safety, and public life data during temporary activations to justify permanent changes -- is a methodology worth adopting.
