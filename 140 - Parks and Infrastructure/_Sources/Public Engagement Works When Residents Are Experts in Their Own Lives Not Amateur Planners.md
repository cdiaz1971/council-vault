# Public Engagement Works When Residents Are Experts in Their Own Lives Not Amateur Planners

## Summary

Strong Towns argues that conventional public engagement processes fail because they ask residents to act as amateur urban planners rather than experts in their own experience. The alternative starts with two questions: where are people struggling, and what is the next smallest thing we can do about it. Curiosity, not consensus-building, is the missing element.

## Key Points

The typical planning process begins with a hidden assumption: the solution already exists and public engagement exists to generate buy-in. The questions are designed to refine a predetermined answer, not to learn. Innovations like restaurant-menu prioritization exercises or gamified engagement improve how answers are collected without questioning whether the right questions are being asked.

The Strong Towns approach: start with struggle, not vision. "Where are people struggling?" replaces "What is your vision for the neighborhood?" Once a struggle is identified, the next question is "What is the next smallest thing we can do to address it?" Then act, observe, learn, repeat. This is the incremental urbanism model applied to participation itself.

The Bruce Lee parallel (from the Pragmatic Dharma note elsewhere in this session) is implicit here: research your own experience, absorb what is useful, reject what is useless. The resident is doing that research every day by living in the place. The planner arrives once every few years for a two-hour meeting.

The key failure mode: curiosity is the critical missing component. Most processes substitute documentation of public opinion for genuine learning about what a place needs.

## Relevance

This connects directly to Frederick's LMC Review Commission work and to any community engagement around the Real Rent Ordinance, the Housing Production Target Resolution, or the data center ZTA. The anti-data center public comment at the Frederick worksession was powerful specifically because it came from residents describing their actual concerns about water, power bills, and air quality, not from residents being asked to weigh in on a planning framework.

The "next smallest thing" model is also useful for the Affordability Beyond Housing thread. Rather than trying to produce a comprehensive food access plan, the question is: where are District 2 residents struggling with food access right now, and what is the smallest intervention the city has authority to make. That reframe helps identify actionable steps rather than comprehensive strategies that stall at the planning stage.

The strongest quote from the piece: "We spend a lot of time teaching residents how to think like planners. Maybe we should spend more time teaching planners how to think like residents."
