# Leon Krier -- Public-Private Space Symbiosis in Urban Design

## Summary

Leon Krier's Sunday sketch argues that public and private spaces in cities are not in tension but mutually constitutive -- each type of space depends on and reinforces the other, and urban design that treats them as separate categories produces worse outcomes for both.

## Key Points

Krier's argument, rendered through a Sunday sketch format in the City Build newsletter, is that the traditional opposition of public versus private space in urban planning is a false dichotomy. A well-functioning street needs both the private frontage of buildings that activate it and the public right-of-way that allows movement and gathering. A plaza without private uses surrounding it becomes dead space; private buildings without public interface become fortresses.

The practical implication is that zoning and development decisions cannot treat public and private space as independently optimized systems. Maximizing private square footage without considering what happens at the boundary with public space produces hostile streetscapes. Over-investing in public amenities in areas without active private frontage produces underused amenities.

This is a design principle with direct application to mixed-use zoning, ground-floor activation requirements, and walkability standards.

## Relevance

Directly applicable to Frederick's housing and land use conversations, particularly around missing middle housing, mixed-use zoning, and the Lucas Village redevelopment. The framing gives language for why ground-floor activation matters in dense housing -- not just as an amenity but as a structural condition for making public space work. Worth referencing in conversations with planning staff or in public remarks about development standards.
