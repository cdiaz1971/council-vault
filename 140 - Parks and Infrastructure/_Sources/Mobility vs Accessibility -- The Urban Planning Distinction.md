# Mobility vs Accessibility -- The Urban Planning Distinction

## Summary

A NextCity essay by Dr. Gabrielle Bendiner-Viani argues that cities have confused speed of movement with access to what people actually need. "Placework" -- her term for the civic and social function of everyday places -- depends on urban environments that put daily needs within reach. Car-centric design strips that texture away, leaving people fast but cut off.

## Key Points

Bendiner-Viani introduces "placework" as both a concept and an argument: everyday places (street corners, shops, parks, common spaces) do necessary work for people, allowing them to negotiate individual life alongside shared life and to become part of a community. Car-centric development destroys the density and texture required for placework to happen.

Drawing on urban justice scholar Lorena Zarate's framework, the essay argues that in an urban century, justice necessarily includes spatial dimensions alongside economic and political ones. The goal of a city is collective wellbeing, not the city as an investment vehicle or a system optimized for vehicle throughput.

The practical planning implication: when cities measure mobility purely by speed, they optimize for car drivers at the expense of everyone else -- the elderly, children, people with disabilities, people without cars. The alternative measure is accessibility: can people reach what they need, without a car, in a reasonable amount of time? That question should drive land use and street design decisions.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick's LMC review is where these design choices get codified. How the code treats mixed use, parking minimums, lot coverage, and street standards determines whether Frederick neighborhoods enable or obstruct everyday accessibility. The "placework" concept is also useful framing for public arguments about downtown vitality, pedestrian investment, and the relationship between land use and community wellbeing -- grounding technical zoning arguments in something people can feel.
