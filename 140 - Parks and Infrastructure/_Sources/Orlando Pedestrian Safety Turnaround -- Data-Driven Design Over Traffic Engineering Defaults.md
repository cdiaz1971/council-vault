# Orlando Pedestrian Safety Turnaround -- Data-Driven Design Over Traffic Engineering Defaults

## Summary

Smart Growth America's 2026 Dangerous by Design report documents that U.S. pedestrian fatalities remain 72% above 2009 levels -- with 7,080 deaths in 2024 -- despite a modest recent decline attributable to post-COVID traffic congestion returning rather than any design intervention. Orlando is identified as the most significant success story: it dropped from consistent top-20 deadliest status to 25th by making deliberate design investments. Hoboken, NJ has sustained nine consecutive years of zero pedestrian fatalities.

## Key Points

The national picture: pedestrian deaths are up 72% since 2009, outpacing both population growth and vehicle miles traveled. At the current rate of decline, the U.S. won't return to 2009 fatality levels until 2042 -- with 96,615 more deaths in the interim. Pedestrians now account for nearly one in five roadway fatalities, a new high. More than 80% of states and metro areas have gotten more dangerous for pedestrians over time.

The structural diagnosis from SGA: transportation agencies apply interstate road design standards to city streets with conflict points (driveways, crosswalks, cross streets, parking), prioritizing congestion management over safety. The recent fatality dip came from congestion returning after COVID, not from design changes. "Celebrating coming down slightly off of record highs is part of the problem."

What Orlando did:
- Increased the number of crossings
- Ensured all crosswalks have markers and signals
- Improved visibility and lighting
- Drew driver attention to activity outside their own lanes
- Prioritized projects near schools and known speed-concern areas
- Used data strategically to identify where to deploy resources first

Hoboken's zero-fatality model (nine straight years since January 2017): redesigned its most dangerous intersections with curb extensions and bollards to improve sight lines, reduced speed limits to 20 mph, and installed leading pedestrian intervals (LPIs) that give walkers a head start before cars can turn.

Racial and income disparities: Native and Black Americans make up 16% of the population but 22% of pedestrian deaths 2020-2024. People in lower-income areas are disproportionately more likely to be hit and killed. SGA frames this as the product of decades of transportation design decisions that prioritize moving cars fast through communities.

The design-behavior relationship: "We know on a very visceral level that the design of the roadway can change our behavior, but our agencies will not admit it." Speed traps prove roadway design changes driver behavior -- the same principle applies to safety design.

## Relevance

The Orlando model is directly applicable to Frederick in two ways. First, the EPMD ordinance debate has surfaced the question of whether Class 1 and 2 e-bikes should have path access -- and the underlying tension is the same one Orlando resolved: do streets serve the people who live on them or moving cars efficiently? Second, the data on racial and income disparities in pedestrian deaths maps onto Frederick's geography: the Golden Mile corridor (ZIP 21702, 40% below ALICE threshold) has the heaviest pedestrian conflict environment and the most ALICE households.

The Hoboken LPI model (pedestrians get a head start before cars can turn) is a simple, low-cost intervention that Frederick could pilot at high-pedestrian intersections near schools or the Golden Mile. It requires no major infrastructure investment.

The SGA framing that agencies won't admit road design changes behavior is a useful counterargument when city engineers resist pedestrian safety investments on congestion grounds.
