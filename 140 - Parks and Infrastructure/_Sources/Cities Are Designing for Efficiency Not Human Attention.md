# Cities Are Designing for Efficiency Not Human Attention

## Summary

Urban environments are engineered to compete for attention (ads, traffic, noise) even though attention is a limited cognitive resource, and a few cities have started treating it as a public good worth protecting through advertising bans, dark-sky lighting codes, right-on-red bans, and car-free superblocks.

## Key Points

The core argument, drawing on neuroscientist Grace Lindsay's framing of attention as "the flexible control of limited computational resources," is that city infrastructure is usually optimized for throughput and commerce, not for how much cognitive load it places on the people moving through it, and that mental fatigue from a normal commute gets treated as a personal failing rather than a design outcome.

Concrete interventions the piece cites: São Paulo's 2007 Clean City Law banned outdoor advertising and removed over 15,000 billboards. Pittsburgh's Dark Sky Ordinance uses shielding, dimmers, and warmer lighting on city lighting to cut glare and light pollution. Cambridge banned right turns on red as part of its Vision Zero plan, removing one decision point from an already complex intersection. Barcelona's Superblocks reclaim car-dominated space for pedestrians, though researchers describe that transformation as an "unfinished policy paradigm shift" due to ongoing political resistance.

The piece is careful to note the goal isn't a stimulation-free city (urban stimulation is part of why people want to live in cities, and city life carries real social, educational, and career benefits) but rather treating attention as a resource cities should budget for, the same way they already budget for traffic congestion, air quality, and pedestrian flow. A cited 2008 study found participants who walked through an arboretum performed better on directed-attention tests afterward than those who walked through an urban environment, supporting the idea that cities also need to provide places to recover attention, not just places that spend it.
