# Fare-Free Transit Prioritizes the Wrong Problem -- The Case for Frequency First

## Summary

Denis Agar argues that transit frequency is a more powerful equity lever than fare elimination -- the Brampton vs York Region case study shows 430% vs 60% ridership growth from frequency investment alone, and the funding trade-off ($700M for free transit vs $70M for progressive fares) makes frequency the higher-value ask.

## Key Points

**The core argument:** If a terrible bus becomes free, it does not become useful. The one-third of Americans and Canadians without driver's licenses are not primarily blocked by cost -- they are blocked by infrequent, unreliable, coverage-limited service. A Washington State survey of nondrivers found that lack of transit service was a barrier for 65% of them; cost was a barrier for only 3%.

**Brampton vs York Region (the key data point):** Two adjacent Toronto suburbs with similar demographics, income levels, immigration rates, and urban form. Brampton invested in frequency for 20 years; York kept frequencies low but built new infrastructure. Result: Brampton ridership up 430%, York ridership up 60%. Frequency, not fare price, drove the difference.

**The funding math (Metro Vancouver):** Free transit for all would cost $700M/year. Progressive fare discounts for low-income riders would cost $70M/year -- a $630M difference. That $630M, applied to bus service, would boost the fleet by 50%, adding ~850 buses. That is enough to eliminate overcrowding, extend frequent service to almost everyone, and add regional express routes. Paratransit could become on-demand, region-wide, 24 hours.

**On progressive fares vs universal free:** Agar supports progressive discounts -- income-tested free or reduced fares (LA offers 20 free trips/month to anyone under ~$58K/year). The difference is that $630M in operating funding is real and hard to get, and targeting it at frequency rather than universality does more for the people who most need transit.

**The equity argument for frequency:** Without frequent transit, nondrivers are systematically excluded from employment, education, and democratic participation. Some nondrivers reported that friends stopped inviting them to events because everyone assumed they couldn't get there. Hourly bus service is not transit equity -- it is managed exclusion.

**Counterpoint acknowledged:** Agar concedes that "free transit" is a simpler political ask that can attract riders who only use transit occasionally (airports, sports games). He does not dismiss fare-free transit -- he argues frequency should be on the marquee, not fares.

Second, the framing connects to the Infrastructure of Democratic Survival synthesis -- Agar's point that nondrivers are excluded from "employment, education, and democratic processes" maps directly onto Layer 3 civic infrastructure.
