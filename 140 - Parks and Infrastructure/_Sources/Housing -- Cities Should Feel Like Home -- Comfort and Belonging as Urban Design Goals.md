# Housing -- Cities Should Feel Like Home -- Comfort and Belonging as Urban Design Goals

## Summary

A CITYBUILDER essay on Witold Rybczynski's 1986 book "Home: A Short History of an Idea" reframes the goal of urban policy: cities, not just houses, should be built to feel like home. Comfort and privacy are not natural features of domestic life -- they are cultural innovations that emerged from specific material conditions. The same intentionality that produced the modern home needs to be applied at the scale of the city.

## Key Points

**Comfort is a cultural invention, not a given**

Medieval homes -- even noble ones -- were transient, public, and entirely without privacy. The bourgeois family home as a place of comfort and refuge emerged gradually through Dutch, French, and English innovations in domesticity, driven by economic conditions and evolving social norms. Rybczynski's key finding: he set out to write about technological innovation in homes and discovered instead that comfort and privacy are cultural achievements, not eternal features of human habitation.

**The modernist rupture**

World War I produced a sea change that rejected comfort and privacy as bourgeois indulgences. The international style and its descendants -- glass walls, open plans, iron cafe chairs in living spaces -- abandoned the ergonomic and psychological gains of several centuries. Comfort only slowly reasserted itself.

**Why this matters at the urban scale**

Rybczynski notes that furniture designed for human comfort four hundred years ago is still comfortable today because human bodies change on evolutionary timescales, not fashion timescales. The implication: city planning shaped by human-scale comfort -- walkability, density, third spaces, financial affordability -- is not a trend but a durable investment in conditions that human beings will always need.

**The CITYBUILDER frame**

Zoning reform, transit investment, housing affordability, and the creation of genuine third spaces are all means to the same end: making cities feel like home in the same way a well-designed house does. Political democracy without economic democracy is unstable; cities without comfort and belonging produce the same result at a smaller scale.

## Relevance

Useful framing for housing production arguments in Frederick. The "home as comfort and belonging" lens reframes housing policy away from units-and-density technicalities and toward what residents actually need: places where they can be known, comfortable, and safe. Connects to [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] and to the broader question of what Frederick's Housing and Human Services budget is actually trying to build. The third-space angle connects to parks and infrastructure work.
