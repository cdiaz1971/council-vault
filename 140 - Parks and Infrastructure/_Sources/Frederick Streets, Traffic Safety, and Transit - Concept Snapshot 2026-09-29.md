# Frederick Streets, Traffic Safety, and Transit

## Who controls what

Authority over Frederick's streets and transit is split three ways, and most policy questions turn on which layer owns the lever.

- **City of Frederick.** Owns and maintains 360+ centerline miles (1,300+ lane miles) of streets, up 41 centerline miles since 2020, plus 343 miles of sidewalk and the curb ramps on them ([[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-09-08]]; [[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-08-25]]). Runs the DPW traffic-calming program ([[Hearing Brief 2026-03-19]]). Regulates use of the public right-of-way, including micromobility ([[EPMD Ordinance Brief -- 2026-05-26]]), and sets automated speed-enforcement penalties within state limits ([[Hearing Brief 2026-08-20]]). Controls the land use and parking rules that shape travel demand ([[Affordability Agenda -- Council Authority Tiering by Domain]]).
- **Frederick County.** Operates and funds TransIT bus service. The City's role is advocacy and coordination on routes, stops, and frequency. The Affordability Agenda puts transportation at **Tier 2** (indirect leverage) ([[Affordability Agenda -- Council Authority Tiering by Domain]]).
- **State of Maryland.** State highways run through the City. On Route 40, SHA has designated the Old Camp Road-Route 15 segment one of the state's first six Vulnerable Road User Safety Assessment corridors, with reduced speed limits already in effect ([[Golden Mile Small Area Profile]]). The state also sets the e-bike baseline in Transportation Article §21-1205.2 ([[EPMD Ordinance Brief -- 2026-05-26]]) and runs MARC.

## Frederick status snapshot (as of 2026-09-23)

**Traffic calming.** DPW's traffic-calming program presented to Council on March 19, 2026. Completed projects: Rosemont, Hayward, Tuscanney, Walter Martz, S. Jefferson, Villa Estates, Baughmans. Active or upcoming: Motter/13th St, Willowdale Dr, E. 16th St, Himes Ave, Hillcrest Dr, 7th St ([[Hearing Brief 2026-03-19]]). The FY2027 council priorities deck (Nash) requested a **$150k Tactical Traffic Calming and Pedestrian Safety Program** and a separate **$150k solar-powered crosswalk signaling** line, alongside $10M for roads, sidewalks, and shared-use paths ([[Workshop Brief 2026-04-01]]; [[Tactical Traffic Calming Program -- Frederick]]). *confidence: low. The vault doesn't record whether either $150k line survived budget adoption.* A related roadmap item, LED crosswalk signage funded through the Traffic Calming CIP with priority for Equity Emphasis Areas, is in Public Works Committee review, targeted for Oct-Dec ([[Legislative Roadmap FY2027]]; Notion roadmap, checked 2026-09-23).

**Golden Mile / High Injury Network.** The Golden Mile plan area recorded 576 vehicle crashes, 42 non-motorist crashes, and 25 severe or fatal injuries in 2020-2024. Pedestrian crashes concentrate on Hillcrest Drive and Key Parkway, both designated **High Injury Network** corridors, and each has an active project: the Key Parkway High Injury Corridor Study and the Hillcrest Drive traffic calming project (CIP 340009). The **Golden Mile Multimodal Access Plan** (CIP 380009, in design) adds a 10-foot bi-directional shared-use path and a 12-foot bus-only/right-turn lane on westbound Route 40 ([[Golden Mile Small Area Profile]]).

**Active mobility planning.** Planning is updating **Let's Move Frederick**, the active mobility plan, with measurable goals and a framework for bike and pedestrian investment, for adoption in FY2027. A **MARC station TOD designation** application has been filed with MDOT ([[Budget Brief - Planning 2026-04-15]]). The working assumption is that no Frederick MARC station meets the frequency threshold for parking relief under the Transit and Housing Opportunity Act (HB 894) ([[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]]).

**Sidewalks and curb ramps.** The 2025-26 O'Connell & Lawrence survey put the citywide Sidewalk Condition Index (SCI) at 78. It found 14% of mileage (46 miles, 740 segments) Poor or Very Poor, concentrated in historic downtown, and 62% of inspected ADA ramps failing (3,841), 85% of them for a missing tactile warning pad, the cheapest fix. Condition is flat across Equity Emphasis Areas (79 inside vs. 77 outside) but varies by district (District 3 lowest at 71, District 5 highest at 84). Planning-level costs run $5.0-5.8M for priority segments, $11.2-53.4M for grade differentials, and $19.5-20.5M for ramp compliance, against $3.5M committed ([[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-08-25]]).

**Pavement.** FY2027 Street Maintenance spending is $3.3M against $4.7M in Highway User Revenue, plus $3.5M in CIP 320029. The pavement condition survey dates from 2021-22, and a five-year paving plan is in place ([[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-09-08]]).

**Automated enforcement.** G-26-4 replaced the flat $40 school-zone speed-camera fine with the tiered $40-$425 structure required by state HB 182. It passed 5-0-1 on August 20, 2026, with Nash abstaining because her outside employer sells speed cameras ([[Hearing Brief 2026-08-20]]; [[Hearing Minutes 2026-08-20]]).

**E-bike (EPMD) ordinance.** See the micromobility section below. As of the September 14, 2026 draft, still awaiting introduction.

**State funding context.** Maryland's FY2027 six-year Consolidated Transportation Plan is $21.9B, about $200M below last year, and funds **no new capital projects**. The Transportation Trust Fund faces a ~$1.2B, 10-year road-maintenance gap, and titling taxes are projected to overtake gas taxes as the largest state revenue source. Expectations for new state money for local projects such as the Multimodal Access Plan should be set accordingly ([[Maryland's FY2027 Six-Year Transportation Plan Funds No New Capital Projects]]).

## Traffic safety: design over behavior

The research in this cluster converges on one position: street design, especially design that controls speed, drives safety outcomes more than education or individual behavior does.

- **Behavior framing.** Safety PSAs ("look both ways," "wear bright clothing") shift responsibility onto the people harmed and away from roads engineered for speed ([[Traffic Safety Messaging Shifts Blame to Pedestrians]]). The same critique applies to cycling campaigns like the "Dutch reach": Dutch safety comes from separated infrastructure, calming, and lower speeds, not from rider technique. Protected lanes work as "affordances" that make safe behavior the obvious behavior ([[Bike Safety Requires Infrastructure Not Behavior Change]]).
- **National trend, read two ways.** GHSA counted 3,024 pedestrian deaths in H1 2025, down 11%, the largest drop in 15 years, though still above 2019. New Mexico halved its early-2025 deaths after piloting crosswalk warning lights ([[Pedestrian Safety -- 2025 Deaths Show Largest Drop in 15 Years but Remain Above Pre-Pandemic Levels]]). Smart Growth America's 2026 *Dangerous by Design* counts 7,080 deaths in 2024, 72% above 2009. It attributes the recent dip to post-COVID congestion, not design, and projects no return to 2009 levels until 2042 ([[Orlando Pedestrian Safety Turnaround -- Data-Driven Design Over Traffic Engineering Defaults]]). *confidence: medium on interpretation. Different datasets and periods.*
- **Distribution.** Native and Black Americans are 16% of the population but 22% of pedestrian deaths (2020-2024), and lower-income areas are over-represented ([[Orlando Pedestrian Safety Turnaround -- Data-Driven Design Over Traffic Engineering Defaults]]).
- **Peer models.**
  - *Orlando* moved from the top-20 deadliest metros to 25th by adding crossings, marking and signalizing every crosswalk, improving lighting, and using data to prioritize school and speed-concern areas.
  - *Hoboken* has gone nine years without a pedestrian death, using curb extensions, bollards, a 20 mph limit, and leading pedestrian intervals (LPIs) ([[Orlando Pedestrian Safety Turnaround -- Data-Driven Design Over Traffic Engineering Defaults]]).
  - *Somerville* has gone at least three years without a traffic death (the Globe reported four) by layering cheap measures: 139 striped speed humps since 2019, flex posts and stop signs at traffic circles, and planter-separated bike paths. Serious crashes fell 50% from 2024 to 2025, while Boston, under the same Vision Zero framework, recorded 560 serious crashes. Somerville also benefited from the Green Line extension ([[Somerville's Whole of Government Approach to Zero Traffic Deaths]]). *confidence: low on the exact fatality-free duration.*
- **Road diets.** Converting a four-lane undivided road to two through lanes plus a center turn lane cuts crashes 19-47% per FHWA, and costs little more than restriping when timed with resurfacing ([[Road Diets (Roadway Reconfiguration)  FHWA]]; [[Road Diet]]). The rough guidance is roads under ~20,000 vehicles per day. Proposals should document ADT, crash history, sidewalk gaps, and proximity to schools and transit ([[Road Diet]]). FHWA's *Road Diet Information Guide* PDF is archived with the sources but wasn't reviewed for this page.
- **Data-led prioritization.** MoDOT ranks intersections using Cambridge Mobile Telematics data on hard braking, phone use, and speeding, which finds risk before crashes occur. Intersections are roughly 25% of U.S. traffic fatalities and half of injury crashes. This kind of analysis fits the Safe Streets and Roads for All (SS4A) federal grant program ([[Telematics Data for Proactive Traffic Safety Prioritization]]).
- **Land use.** In Durham, council members told the bike/ped advisory commission that Vision Zero can't be met through bike infrastructure alone: cutting vehicle miles traveled requires the density that zoning controls. Durham is off track on its 2017 targets ([[Vision Zero Requires Land Use Reform, Not Just Bike Infrastructure]]).

## Mobility vs. accessibility

Two essays argue that measuring mobility by vehicle speed, together with separating homes from daily needs, produces cities that are fast but hard to reach, especially for people who can't drive: older adults, children, disabled people, and people without cars. The proposed measure is **accessibility**: can a person reach what they need, without a car, in reasonable time? The essays tie this to land use and street standards in the LMC ([[Mobility vs Accessibility -- The Urban Planning Distinction]]; [[The Myth of Mobility -- Speed-Optimized Cities Reduce Access]]). *confidence: low that these are independent sources; they may be two captures of one Next City essay.*

New Hampshire's incomplete interstate buildout offers a natural experiment on the same point. Some communities there kept more walkable, human-scale fabric as a byproduct of highways that were never built, which suggests the dispersed, speed-first pattern elsewhere was a choice rather than an inevitability. The form of infrastructure a place accepts shapes what kind of community it becomes over decades ([[Interstates and the Road Not Taken New Hampshire Alternative Growth]]).

## Transit

- **Frequency vs. fares.** Denis Agar argues frequency is the stronger equity lever:
  - In a Washington State survey of nondrivers, lack of service was a barrier for 65% and cost for 3%.
  - Brampton, which invested in frequency, grew ridership 430%; neighboring York Region, which didn't, grew 60%.
  - In Metro Vancouver, universal fare-free transit would cost ~$700M/yr versus ~$70M/yr for income-tested fares, and the difference could expand the bus fleet ~50%.
  
  He concedes fare-free is a simpler political ask ([[Fare-Free Transit Prioritizes the Wrong Problem -- The Case for Frequency First]]).
- **Disability access beyond fares.** Disability Culture Lab lists six asks: reliable 5-15-minute headways, protected multi-use mobility lanes (delineator posts can cut crashes up to 53%), accessible parking parity, public charging for mobility devices, working elevators, and paratransit reform ([[Free Transit Is Not Enough The Disability Access Gap]]).
- **BRT and land use.** Raleigh's BRT delivers near-rail service (level boarding, signal priority, queue jumps, dedicated lanes, 10-minute frequency) funded by a ½-cent Wake County transit sales tax. Frequent routes grew from 3 to 11. The comprehensive plan zones for density along Frequent Transit Corridors, and Richmond and Atlanta show BRT spurring development ([[Raleigh BRT -- Transit-Oriented Development and the Wake Transit Plan]]). The state is weighing BRT as an alternative to Baltimore's stalled Red Line, with a decision expected in early 2027 ([[Maryland's FY2027 Six-Year Transportation Plan Funds No New Capital Projects]]).
- **Frederick scale.** César estimated TransIT operating cost at roughly $95 per revenue hour (NTD 2024). On that basis, four extra peak hours a day, five days a week, is 1,040 hours a year, or about $98,800 ([[Bus Service]]). *confidence: medium. Mode and agency record unspecified. TransIT is county-funded, so this is a figure for an advocacy ask, not a City appropriation.*
- **Fare-system procurement.** Tap-to-pay vendor Cubic's partnership with Palantir raises the risk that rider payment data could reach immigration enforcement through corporate channels. That argues for data-use restrictions in any fare-technology procurement. Frederick doesn't currently use Cubic ([[Transit Payment Systems as ICE Data Collection Infrastructure via Cubic-Palantir Partnership]]).
- **Synthesis.** César's synthesis note treats transit frequency, housing, and micromobility as three layers of the same access infrastructure ([[Access to Opportunity Is Civic Infrastructure -- Transit, Housing, and Micromobility as Democratic Preconditions]]).

## Micromobility: the EPMD ordinance

Council Member Peter Brehm is the named sponsor of a draft ordinance, not yet formally introduced, regulating electrically powered micromobility devices (EPMDs), a category covering e-bikes, e-scooters, e-cargo bikes, EPAMDs, low-speed electric vehicles, OPDMDs, mopeds, and similar devices. It repeals Chapter 22, Article VI of the City Code and adds Chapter 33, Article II (EPMDs generally) and Article III (shareable, dockless electric bicycles and scooters) ([[E-Bike Ordinance-092326]]).

- **Banned outright.** E-motos (motor-vehicle-class electric two-wheelers exceeding e-bike limits) may not operate on any travel way or non-motorized trail. Commercial shareable, dockless rental of electric bicycles and scooters is prohibited citywide.
- **Where EPMDs may operate.** Allowed by default on City shared-use paths, alleys, bike lanes, roadways, and streets, unless the City posts signs restricting a specific location. Two named exceptions run the other way: the Baker Park Shared Use Path between Baughman's Lane and Bentz Street bars EPMD use outright, and Carroll Creek Linear Park bars it unless the City posts signs permitting it. Sidewalk use is banned in the downtown business district; outside downtown, EPMDs may use sidewalks but must yield to pedestrians and sound an audible signal before passing.
- **Operating rules.** Riders must travel at a reasonable and prudent speed, yield right-of-way to pedestrians and bicycles, keep at least 3 feet of clearance when passing, dismount where posted, and may not operate while impaired. Passengers are allowed only up to what the manufacturer designed the device to carry; aftermarket modifications that increase a device's speed, power, or passenger capacity are barred.
- **Equipment.** Riders under 16 must wear a CPSC-compliant helmet, though a first helmet violation draws a warning rather than a fine. Devices need a front white light and rear red light from a half hour after sunset to a half hour before sunrise, brakes able to stop from 10 mph within 15 feet, and a bell or audible signal (sirens and whistles are barred).
- **Penalties.** A general EPMD violation is a $100 municipal infraction; a collision between an EPMD operator and a pedestrian is $1,000. A parent who allows a minor's violation is also subject to a $100 infraction. For shareable dockless devices, offering one for rent is a $400 infraction (each day a separate offense) and operating one on a travel way is $60; an impounded device can be reclaimed for a $40 initial fee plus $5 per day storage, with anything unclaimed after 45 days treated as abandoned.
- **Equity record.** Staff assessed "no discernible equity impact." The counter-record cites District 2's income and foreign-born profile, e-bikes as primary transportation for lower-income workers (NREL's Denver data), and regional default-permit precedent, including Montgomery County Parks (2021), the C&O Canal, and Virginia law ([[EPMD Ordinance -- Equity Argument, Class 1 and 2 Path Access]]; [[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-05-26]]).
- **Peer state models.** Massachusetts' S.3077 would tier devices by speed and reserve license, registration, and insurance for the 41+ mph tier ([[Massachusetts Speed-Based Tiered E-Bike Regulation -- Peer State Model]]). Washington draws the e-bike/motorcycle line at 20 mph unassisted and 750W ([[Washington State E-Bike Classification Law -- Speed and Power as Legal Dividing Lines]]).

## Streets as an asset: maintenance and repair

- **Fiscal structure.** Deferred maintenance is a funding problem more than an engineering one. The federal gas tax (18.4¢) hasn't changed since 1993 and erodes as vehicles grow more efficient. Timely resurfacing costs a fraction of reconstruction (the source puts reconstruction at 5-10x). Pew estimates an $86.3B state maintenance gap over 10 years. AI and camera pothole detection is being piloted: San Jose reported 97% accuracy, and Chicago has a similar pilot ([[Deferred Road Maintenance Is a Structural Fiscal Problem, Not an Engineering One]]). Maryland's own Trust Fund shows the same squeeze ([[Maryland's FY2027 Six-Year Transportation Plan Funds No New Capital Projects]]).
- **Complaint-driven repair.** A 2024 Houston study found lower-income and majority Black and Hispanic neighborhoods filed fewer 311 pothole complaints despite worse roads. Proactive inspection avoids that bias ([[311 Complaint Systems Underserve the Neighborhoods That Need Repairs Most]]). *(The two notes above clip the same Stateline article.)* Frederick's sidewalk prioritization formula weights condition at 50 points and school proximity, Equity Emphasis Areas, downtown, and transit at 50, which is a proactive, scored approach ([[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-08-25]]).

## Emerging technology in the right-of-way

- **Autonomous vehicles.** Whether AVs arrive as shared fleets or privately owned cars changes parking demand and sprawl pressure. Zoning written now shouldn't over-specify car storage ([[Autonomous Vehicles and City Planning -- Fleet vs Ownership Models]]).
- **Sidewalk delivery robots.** West Hollywood requires operators to share $4 per device per day in ad revenue and pay ADA and geofencing penalties into a sidewalk accessibility fund, an estimated $40k-80k a year ([[Delivery Robots as Sidewalk Infrastructure Revenue -- West Hollywood Fee Model]]). Operators also collect detailed ADA hazard data (35,000 points in DC in 2024), which cities could require through Mobility Data Specification (MDS) sharing as a license condition, though few use it yet ([[Sidewalk Robot Data Sharing as ROW Governance -- Accessibility and Curb Management]]). *(Both notes clip one Next City article.)* Frederick has no delivery robots today.
- **Private parking data.** An op-ed proposes bringing private garage inventory into city mobility data to cut cruising for parking, which cited studies put at 18-68% of downtown traffic. *The author sells this product* ([[Private Parking Garages as an Untapped Mobility Data Layer]]).
