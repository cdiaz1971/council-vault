# Delivery Robots as Sidewalk Infrastructure Revenue -- West Hollywood Fee Model

## Summary

West Hollywood has implemented what may be the first program requiring sidewalk delivery robot operators to share advertising revenue and pay ADA violation penalties into a dedicated fund for sidewalk accessibility improvements. The model treats the public right-of-way as a revenue-generating asset when commercial operators use it, converting bot traffic into infrastructure investment. Estimated annual yield is $40,000-$80,000 -- modest but precedent-setting.

## Key Points

West Hollywood's revised license agreement with operators Serve and Coco requires $4 per device per day in shared advertising revenue, plus annual program fees and ADA and geofencing violation penalties. All revenue flows into a special accessibility improvement fund. The program has been running for about three months as of the article's publication.

The prior 2020 pilot agreement had no financial accountability mechanisms -- no fees, no penalties, nothing that created consequences for sidewalk obstruction or ADA failures. The new contract represents a structural shift from permissive pilot to accountable license.

Industry reaction is cautiously supportive. Coco's head of government relations said revenue sharing "right-sized" to link back to infrastructure improvements is something they would welcome. The underlying dynamic is that companies need cities to keep the regulatory environment stable; conceding modest fees buys political durability.

Several cities -- DC, Santa Monica, West Hollywood -- are also requiring operators to share Mobility Data Specification (MDS) data on robot location and sidewalk conditions. Robot.com captured 35,000 ADA hazard data points in DC in 2024 alone. So far, cities are not systematically using that data to prioritize accessibility repairs, but the infrastructure is there.

A Coco-BlindSquare partnership provides real-time navigation hazard data for blind and low-vision users as bots traverse sidewalks -- reframing robots from accessibility threat to accessibility asset when appropriately governed.

## Relevance

Frederick doesn't have delivery robots yet, but this is the right framework to have in place before operators arrive rather than after. The right-of-way fee model is directly applicable: if the city permits commercial use of public sidewalks, it should extract value for sidewalk maintenance and accessibility improvements. The West Hollywood precedent makes that argument much easier.

More immediately, the ROW governance framework here connects to franchise agreement negotiations and the ADA web accessibility deadline already documented in the vault. The principle -- commercial operators using public infrastructure owe a contribution to that infrastructure's maintenance -- applies across cable franchise agreements, delivery robots, and any future mobility technology.

Worth flagging to Planning and Public Works when the city next revisits sidewalk maintenance funding or right-of-way policy.
