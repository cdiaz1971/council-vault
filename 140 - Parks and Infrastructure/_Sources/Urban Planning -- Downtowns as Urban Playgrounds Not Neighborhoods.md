# Urban Planning -- Downtowns as Urban Playgrounds Not Neighborhoods

## Summary

The Happy Urbanist argues that American downtowns have become destinations rather than neighborhoods -- places people visit with intent and leave without consequence. The essay diagnoses this as a structural failure rooted in low downtown residential population, car-oriented design, and the tendency to address urban vitality through expensive destination amenities rather than through the more fundamental fix of getting people to actually live there.

## Key Arguments

Most people who say they are "from" a city are actually from its annexed edges. Downtown cores generate a disproportionate share of economic output but contain a tiny fraction of the population -- one analysis of the 45 largest U.S. downtowns found they held roughly 11-12% of regional jobs but only about 1.8% of residents. The result is a pulse-driven environment: morning rush, lunch spike, evening exit, then silence. This is a business park, not a neighborhood.

Cities have responded by programming downtown for play -- signature parks, stadiums, aquariums, museums. This can work as a defibrillation (Chattanooga is cited as a success case), but if life only happens during festivals and events, the underlying problem remains. Large single-destination amenities also concentrate access: a kid with a car gets a park, a kid without one gets nothing.

The essay argues that culture only happens where people live. Jane Jacobs' "eyes on the street" require actual residents -- not visitors who expect the city to manage problems the way a tourist expects Disney employees to pick up litter. When downtown is pure destination, ownership disappears and management falls entirely to city government.

The fix is not another big investment -- it is political will to allow more housing, more small-scale commercial space, and more everyday life in downtown cores. Cities that feel lived in (Paris is the example) attract visitors precisely because they are not designed for visitors. The first move is getting people living downtown.

## Relevance

Useful framing for Frederick's downtown housing conversation. The distinction between "destination downtown" and "lived-in downtown" maps directly onto the debate around residential density near the city core and the role of the LMC review. The observation that large consolidated amenities (one big park instead of many small ones) create access inequality is relevant to Frederick's parks and CIP discussions. The essay also reinforces the supply-side argument in the [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] -- the question isn't just whether housing is affordable, but whether it exists at all in places where people want to live.
