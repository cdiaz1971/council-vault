# How Postwar Zoning Dismantled Walkable Local Economies

## Summary

Neighborhood-scale businesses don't disappear by accident. The history of Las Tunas Drive in San Gabriel, California illustrates how zoning codes and postwar planning ideology systematically dismantled once-walkable local commercial corridors over decades. The loss is not just aesthetic -- it is economic and civic.

## Key Points

Postwar planning treated the separation of uses as progress: residential here, commercial there, industrial elsewhere. Mixed-use streets that had organically developed to serve neighborhoods were rezoned, deprived of density, and subjected to parking requirements that made small-scale retail unviable. The result was corridors optimized for cars passing through rather than people stopping to live and shop.

The process was not a single decision but an accumulation of zoning choices, each individually defensible, that collectively destroyed something that would have been extremely difficult to consciously design. This is a common pattern: the existing urbanism that we now want to recreate was built before the rules that now prohibit it.

The relevance to contemporary zoning reform is direct: if you want neighborhood-scale mixed-use corridors, you have to legalize them and remove the rules that currently prevent them.

## Relevance

Relevant to Frederick's LMC review and any conversation about mixed-use zoning or commercial corridor planning. The Las Tunas Drive story is a concrete illustration of what the rules actually produce over time -- useful for making the abstract stakes of zoning decisions tangible to constituents and colleagues.
