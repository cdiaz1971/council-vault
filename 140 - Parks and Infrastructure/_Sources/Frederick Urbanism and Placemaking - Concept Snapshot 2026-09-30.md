# Frederick Urbanism and Placemaking

## Frederick: what's on the table

- **Placemaking definition and wayfinding.** César is leading an effort, via President Letter and Public Works, to define placemaking for the City after public engagement. It includes updating the wayfinding study scope to cover placemaking and issuing a neighborhood placemaking survey with NAC participation. The Notion roadmap lists it as In Progress, targeted for Oct–Dec 2026 ([[Legislative Roadmap FY2027]]).
- **Placemaking capital request.** The FY2027 council priorities deck (Nash) requested **$5M for placemaking**, including city gateways and per-district yearly allocations ([[Workshop Brief 2026-04-01]]).
- **Public art.** On September 3, 2026 the Council approved 6-0 a **$250,000 Frederick Arts Council grant agreement**. It reallocates a 2022 Sky Stage capital award to:
  - a permanent public artwork at Westside Regional Park and Community Center ($154,000)
  - a Utility Box Wrap and Artist Professional Development pilot in public-art-underserved parts of the city ($58,500)
  
  FAC overhead is capped at 15% ([[Hearing Brief 2026-09-03]]; [[Hearing Minutes 2026-09-03]]; [[Culture and Community Committee Brief 2026-09-03]]). The Park in Lieu Programming roadmap item also names public art and park activation in Equity Emphasis neighborhoods ([[Legislative Roadmap FY2027]]). The Community Bridge restoration project is a separate public-art infrastructure matter ([[FY2027 -- Community Bridge Restoration Project -- Overview]]).

## Demonstration: why experience moves opinion

The method at the center of this cluster is **tactical urbanism**: temporary, low-cost changes such as painted crossings, parklets, pop-up shops, and temporary lane reallocations that let people experience a changed place before anyone commits to permanent investment.

- **The mechanism.** Resistance to change is often a limit of imagination, not bad faith. People can't picture what they haven't experienced, and arguments don't bridge that gap; shared experience does. Neighborhood-scale examples include play streets, a kids' maker fair, and a crowdfunded ~$3,000 set of barricades rented out for recurring street events ([[Imagination Barriers -- Why Show Don't Tell Works in Community Change]]). Suburbia can feel "frozen" because it was built all at once and codified, so most residents have no reference point for the alternative ([[Suburbia as Liminal Space -- The Backrooms and the Built Environment]]).
- **Sequence.** Aaron Lubeck argues great places require a sequence: cultural discontent, then political will, then legal reform, then design and construction skill. Code change is a middle step, not the first ([[Lubecks Hierarchy of Needs -- Preconditions for Great Urban Development]]). César's syntheses read the demonstration as the tool that moves the early steps ([[The Imagination Problem in Housing Reform -- Why Code Change Is Step 3 Not Step 1]]; [[Demonstration as Political Strategy -- Creating Facts Before the Regulatory Fight]]).
- **Better Block's record.** The Better Block Foundation reports that temporary activations led to permanent investment in many places:
  - **Toronto, Danforth Ave (2019):** a two-day pop-up became a permanent cycle track, and its café seating became the model for the citywide CaféTO program.
  - **Akron, Kenmore Blvd (2017):** a temporary road diet became permanent and a pop-up became a real business.
  - **Tallahassee, Frenchtown (2016):** a $6.5M neighborhood grant followed.
  - **Dallas, Forest-Audelia (2021):** $7.5M in city investment followed.
  - **Charlotte, Norview Five Points:** a $3.5M permanent project.
  - **Pittsburgh, the Backyard:** a $31M permanent riverfront park.
  - **Detroit (2024):** no vehicle exceeded 40 mph during the demonstration, and the data supported a $24M state safety grant.
  - **Richardson, TX:** a six-to-four-lane test led to permanent protected bike lanes and a $2.6M federal grant.

  ([[Better Block Foundation -- Tactical Urbanism and Show Don't Tell]]; [[Better Block Proving Market Demand Through Tactical Urbanism]]; [[Better Block Bulletin - July 2026]]; [[Better Block Bulletin August 2026]]). *These outcomes are self-reported by Better Block, not independently evaluated.*
- **Current programs.** *Better Intersections* lets neighborhoods pilot and measure safety treatments at specific intersections before capital projects. Dallas's first cohort covers three council districts: senior bus and park access, a school/library/preserve node, and elementary-school access. *Activate Main Street* in downtown Dallas turned on-street parking into curb extensions and parklets for 39 days to collect baseline data ([[Better Block Bulletin -- June 2026]]; [[Better Block Bulletin - July 2026]]).
- **Residents as co-creators.** In East Point, GA, residents and staff built the downtown changes together. In Mesa, AZ, a temporary bike lane, crosswalk, and mercado exposed a broken permitting process; the city reformed it, and a recurring market and permanent crosswalk followed ([[Better Block -- Community as Co-Creator Framework]]; [[Better Block Bulletin -- June 2026]]).
- **Economics.** A Japanese study cited by Strong Towns found that pedestrianization turns streets from places people pass through into destinations, generating foot traffic and commercial activity ([[Tactical Urbanism Produces Measurable Economic Benefits]]). *confidence: low. The study isn't named in the source.*
- **Demonstrating housing.** Fayetteville Strong built a one-bedroom apartment on three parking spaces and a studio on two, so residents could step inside small homes. The gathering also stressed walking tours and informal short video as ways to tell these stories ([[Show-Dont-Tell Urbanism -- Parking Space Demonstration Projects as Political Communication]]).

## Designing public space

- **Public and private space depend on each other.** A plaza without active private frontage goes dead, and buildings without a public interface become fortresses. Ground-floor activation is structural, not an amenity ([[Leon Krier -- Public-Private Space Symbiosis in Urban Design]]).
- **Small places for pausing.** Narrow, shaded, socially permissive spaces like the Greek alley often get more use than large formal plazas ([[City Builder -- Greek Alleys and Urban Pause Spaces]]).
- **Attention as a public good.** Cities load pedestrians with advertising, noise, and complex decisions. Responses include São Paulo's 2007 billboard ban (15,000+ removed), Pittsburgh's dark-sky lighting ordinance, Cambridge's right-on-red ban, and Barcelona's superblocks. Green space restores directed attention ([[Cities Are Designing for Efficiency Not Human Attention]]).
- **Comfort and belonging.** Like the comfortable home, the comfortable city is a cultural achievement that has to be designed on purpose: walkability, third places, affordability ([[Housing -- Cities Should Feel Like Home -- Comfort and Belonging as Urban Design Goals]]).
- **Safety through design.** As reported, Philadelphia's LED streetlights cut outdoor nighttime street crime 15%, vacant-lot greening cut gun violence 29%, and fixing derelict buildings cut gun violence 39%. Tree planting was associated with a 9% drop in gun violence across six cities including Baltimore and DC ([[Lighting and Greening as Low-Cost Public Safety Infrastructure]]).
- **Public art in transportation spaces.** The National League of Cities frames art in and near transportation rights-of-way as infrastructure for street safety, corridor economies, and participation in planning. It notes the layered federal, state, and local rules that govern it ([[Public Art in Transportation Spaces as Safety and Participation Infrastructure]]).
- **Gender-responsive design.** Bogotá treats working women's equity as a design problem: transit aligned with care-work trips, safe public space, and economic inclusion ([[Bogota -- Equity for Working Women as Urban Innovation]]). *The source note captured only part of the article.*
- **Creek corridors.** Research Triangle writers argue that riparian buffer rules keep greenway creeks from gaining any waterfront life, and that flood-aware design could allow creekside activation ([[Creekside Development as Flood Resilience Strategy -- Triangle NC Case]]).

## Walkable commercial fabric and downtown life

- **How it was lost.** Using Las Tunas Drive in San Gabriel, CA as a case, a Strong Towns piece shows how postwar separation of uses, lost density, and parking requirements took apart walkable neighborhood commerce one defensible decision at a time ([[How Postwar Zoning Dismantled Walkable Local Economies]]).
- **Why storefronts stay empty.** Strong Towns identifies eight structural causes:
  - developer indifference
  - afterthought design
  - brokers chasing national "credit" tenants
  - unfinished "cold dark shell" delivery that screens out small operators
  - pro-forma rents above the local market
  - rigid lease terms
  - regulatory barriers
  - vacancy that signals more vacancy
  
  ([[Why Commercial Storefronts Stay Vacant -- Eight Mechanisms]])
- **Destination downtowns vs. lived-in downtowns.** Downtowns programmed around destinations (stadiums, signature parks) empty out between events. As reported, the 45 largest U.S. downtowns hold about 11–12% of regional jobs but only ~1.8% of residents. The essay's remedy is more people living downtown, and it notes that one large amenity serves people with cars better than several small ones ([[Urban Planning -- Downtowns as Urban Playgrounds Not Neighborhoods]]).

- **Night economy and "managing visibility."** An essay on San Francisco's proposed convenience-store curfew argues the policy controls where poverty, addiction, and homelessness are visible rather than addressing why they exist. It pushes costs onto immigrant-owned corner stores on thin margins, night-shift workers (nurses, cooks, warehouse and delivery workers) who rely on late-night services, and people in crisis. It calls this "collective punishment disguised as public administration" ([[The City of Curfews -- SF Convenience Store Policy as Visibility Management]]).

## How residents shape places

- **Everyone is an urban designer.** Denver's move to City responsibility for sidewalk maintenance was won by neighborhood, public-health, and disability coalitions, not design professionals. Walking tours, sketching, and counting benches build design literacy ([[Urban Design as a Practice Belonging to More Than Designers]]).
- **Walk first.** Charlotte Urbanists walk neighborhoods with residents, planners, and elected officials together. The method is low-barrier, observational, and hard for organized voices to dominate ([[Walk-First Community Engagement]]).
- **Residents as experts in their own lives.** Engagement should start with "where are people struggling?" and "what's the next smallest thing we can do?", not with asking residents to act as amateur planners ([[Public Engagement Works When Residents Are Experts in Their Own Lives Not Amateur Planners]]).
- **Someone has to organize.** Walkable form is necessary but not sufficient for community. Someone has to pick the date, host, and follow up ([[Community Building -- The Work of Community by Ryan Puzycki]]).
- **Learning the craft.** Seth Zeren's *New School for City Builders* aims to teach the practical work of developing, permitting, and financing places ([[Why the New School for City Builders -- Seth Zeren Interview]]; [[Why the New School for City Builders -- Seth Zeren on Urbanism Education]]; *two captures of one interview*). For seeing complete New Urbanist neighborhoods in person, Tucson, Orlando (Baldwin Park), St. Louis, and Norfolk are proposed as the best examples ([[New Urbanism Conference Venue Criteria -- Two Tests for Ideal Host Cities]]).
