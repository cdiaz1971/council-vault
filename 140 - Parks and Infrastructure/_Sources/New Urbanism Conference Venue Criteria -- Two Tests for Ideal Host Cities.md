# New Urbanism Conference Venue Criteria -- Two Tests for Ideal Host Cities

## Summary

Lewyn proposes two criteria for ideal CNU conference venues: a city that has never hosted before, plus a walkable New Urbanist development large enough to tour as a complete neighborhood. Tucson, Norfolk, Orlando, and St. Louis are the top candidates meeting both tests.

## Key Points

The article uses the 2026 CNU conference in Fayetteville/Bentonville, Arkansas as a jumping-off point. Northwest Arkansas was an unusual venue -- split between two smaller cities, with New Urbanist developments that are either purely residential or still under construction. That prompted Lewyn to ask what an ideal venue would actually look like.

His two tests:

First, the city should never have hosted CNU before. Most large, walkable cities have already hosted, which eliminates many obvious choices.

Second, the city should have a New Urbanist development large enough to make a neighborhood tour worthwhile -- not just a few improved blocks, but something approaching a complete mini-town. This distinguishes genuinely new walkable urbanism from cities that have old walkable neighborhoods (which older, denser metros like New York and Boston have in abundance).

Cities meeting both tests:
- **Tucson** -- Civano New Town (one of the oldest New Urbanist developments) and the newer Mercado District near downtown
- **Orlando** -- Celebration (famous) and Baldwin Park (over 7,000 residents, one of the largest New Urbanist neighborhoods in the country)
- **St. Louis** -- New Town at St. Charles, an exurban development about 20 years old
- **Norfolk** -- East Beach at the city's northern edge

Near-misses: Phoenix/Tempe (Cul-de-Sac is parking-free but too small), Memphis (Harbor Town is walkable to downtown, reducing the learning value; Crosstown Concourse is a single building), Nashville (both CNU-listed neighborhoods are near downtown).

## Relevance

Thin direct relevance to Frederick council work, but useful as planning reference. The two-test framework -- has it been done before, and is there something worth learning from on the ground -- is a generalizable heuristic for peer city selection. The emphasis on completeness (a full neighborhood, not just a few improved blocks) echoes the same logic behind [[Better Block Foundation -- Tactical Urbanism and Show Don't Tell]] and the distinction between pilot-scale and replicable-scale interventions. Worth holding as a reference when evaluating peer city urbanism examples for housing or LMC work.
