# Suburbia as Liminal Space -- The Backrooms and the Built Environment

## Summary

Jon Jon Wesolowski uses the cultural phenomenon of the Backrooms to explain why suburbia feels uncanny and liminal -- familiar pieces assembled without internal logic, cosplaying rural while demanding urban resources -- and why changing it requires helping people imagine what they've never seen.

## Key Points

- Theoretical lineage: Van Gennep (liminality as in-between states in rites of passage), Freud (the uncanny as familiar-but-wrong), Victor Turner, Bachelard, and Heidegger all feed into how architecture came to carry liminal feeling
- The Backrooms resonates because everything in them is normal -- walls, floors, lights -- but assembled without internal logic. McMansion Hell's entire project was naming exactly this: not that the houses are ugly but that they have no internal logic, assembling architectural signals of luxury, tradition, and wealth with no real commitment to any of them
- Suburbia is "cosplaying rural while demanding the resources of the urban" -- wanting rural privacy and tranquility while expecting urban amenities and convenience. The disdain for traffic is the most emblematic symptom: we reject the efficient systems that would make our preferred arrangement viable
- The "Growth Ponzi Scheme" (Marohn): development infrastructure is built upfront with one-time cash, handed to the city, and then the tax base never generates enough to replace it 40 years later -- so cities approve more growth to pay for the obligations of past growth
- New Urbanism (Seaside/DPZ) was urbanism built in a suburban way: all at once, fully completed, with no room to evolve. The result was still uncanny -- which is why it worked as The Truman Show's set
- The deepest problem: suburbs cannot meaningfully change. "Urbanism is not just a form, it's a process." The Backrooms feel frozen because they are
- The challenge for reform: convincing people who only know the echo that the original sound is preferable. Most suburban residents have no reference point outside the built environment they've always known

## Relevance

This is one of the most useful theoretical framings for the LMC Reform and urban density work. The community opposition to density in Frederick often reads as NIMBYism but is better understood as the Backrooms problem: people can't imagine what they haven't seen, and what they have always known feels normal by default. The Truman Show analogy also maps onto communities designed for a single moment -- Frederick's older suburban neighborhoods are in exactly this position. The Growth Ponzi Scheme section is directly applicable to the fiscal arguments for infill and densification.
