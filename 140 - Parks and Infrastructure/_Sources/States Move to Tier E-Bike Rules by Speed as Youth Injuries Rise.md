# States Move to Tier E-Bike Rules by Speed as Youth Injuries Rise

## Summary

E-bikes and e-scooters now reach motorcycle speeds while most state and local rules were written for slow rideshare scooters. Illinois, New Jersey, California, Pennsylvania, Michigan, Ohio and Massachusetts are adding age floors, licensing and speed-tiered restrictions as injuries among children 11 to 14 climb.

## Key Points

The regulatory gap is a technology gap. Cheaper hardware and aftermarket upgrades have produced personal devices that blur the line between bicycle, scooter and motor vehicle. A D.C. mayoral staffer killed in an October scooter collision was riding a device capable of 55 mph. Most existing rules date to the 2017-2018 rideshare scooter wave and assumed low speeds.

Injury data is rising and skewing young. A U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission report (2017-2024 data) found micromobility fatalities steadily increasing. Johns Hopkins Children's Center researchers found nearly half of the pediatric e-scooter injuries they studied for 2020-2024 occurred in 2024 alone. Children 11 to 14 account for nearly one in four e-scooter injuries, and about one in ten injured children required hospitalization. The researchers attribute the overrepresentation to growing access combined with immature motor skills and no driving experience. New York City recorded at least 28 rider deaths by September 2026, its highest total to that point in any year. Consumer Reports tested 13 e-scooters in August and recommended none.

State actions in 2026:
- **Illinois** (effective January): no one under 15 may ride any e-bike; devices capable of more than 28 mph require a driver's license, registration and insurance and are barred from sidewalks and standard bike lanes. Passed with bipartisan support.
- **New Jersey**: e-bike licensing and registration now in effect.
- **California**: Gov. Newsom signed three e-bike laws in October setting age limits and other restrictions.
- **Pennsylvania**: House passed a bill to legalize and regulate e-scooters (currently illegal on public streets), with a minimum age of 16, speed limits and helmets. Pending in the Senate.
- **Michigan**: proposed license requirement for riders under 18 on e-bikes faster than 20 mph, plus teen helmet rules and a companion fines bill, following local bans after teen crashes.
- **Ohio**: proposed ban on riders 13 and under and a helmet mandate.
- **Massachusetts**: Gov. Healey's statewide framework bill, filed in May, remains in committee.

More than two dozen states and D.C. have enacted or amended e-scooter laws with age minimums and speed limits, per NCSL.

The industry and advocate counterposition: rules should distinguish low-speed pedal-assist bicycles from throttle-driven devices that function as motorcycles, and should be tiered rather than outright bans on high-speed personal devices (as New York and some municipalities have considered).

## Relevance

This is the national backdrop for the [[E-Bike (EPMD) Ordinance]] now back in Public Works Committee. The trend line supports the tiered approach Brehm moved toward with his class 1 on footpaths compromise: Illinois draws its sidewalk and bike-lane ban at devices exceeding 28 mph, which leaves low-speed class 1 operation largely untouched and targets the e-moto category. That is a peer citation for keeping the line at speed and power rather than at the word "e-bike," consistent with [[Massachusetts Speed-Based Tiered E-Bike Regulation -- Peer State Model]] and [[Washington State E-Bike Classification Law -- Speed and Power as Legal Dividing Lines]].

The 11 to 14 injury finding comes from a Maryland institution and is worth having ready if the ordinance discussion turns to age floors. It also raises a preemption question worth checking with Rachel Gray before drafting anything on licensing, registration or age: these are being set at the state level elsewhere, and Frederick's room is more likely in where devices may operate than in who may ride them.
