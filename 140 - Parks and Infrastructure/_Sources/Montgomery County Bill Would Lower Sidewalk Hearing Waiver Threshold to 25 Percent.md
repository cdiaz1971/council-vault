# Montgomery County Bill Would Lower Sidewalk Hearing Waiver Threshold to 25 Percent

## Summary

A Montgomery County bill co-sponsored by all 11 council members would cut the property-owner petition needed to waive a sidewalk hearing from a majority to 25 percent, and skip the hearing entirely when no private property is affected. Hearing set for November 10.

## Key Points

Council member Evan Glass introduced the bill in October 2026 with every other member as co-sponsor. Under current county rules, a majority of property owners along a proposed route must sign a petition to waive the public hearing on a sidewalk project, which in practice gives half a block a veto. The bill makes two changes:

- The petition threshold to waive a hearing drops from a majority to 25 percent of property owners on the route.
- No hearing is required at all when the county determines the project would not impair access to, or require acquisition or use of, private property.

Glass framed the current rule as letting half a community decide the safety of the other half. Supporting data: pedestrian-involved crashes in the county are up 10 percent year over year; state crash data shows 14 pedestrian deaths in 2025 and 15 so far in 2026.

Opposition patterns are familiar. Residents object over street trees, a changed street character, or bringing more people into the neighborhood. A 2025 Kensington dispute near Parkwood Elementary saw "no sidewalks" yard signs and neighbors stop speaking after a father of a child with a disability applied for a sidewalk; he eventually withdrew. The county's Vision Zero coordinator says very few sidewalk projects are uncontentious, and notes sidewalks both separate people from cars and encourage more walking and biking trips. Greater Greater Washington's Maryland policy director called the bill overdue.

The county hearing is November 10, 2026.

## Relevance

This is a process reform, not a funding one, and that makes it portable. The question for Frederick is what procedural veto points exist in City sidewalk delivery today, whether property-owner petitions, hearing requirements, or assessment practices tied to the [[Sidewalk Cost Review]] item. Those rules are not documented in the vault yet, which is a gap worth filling before the Streets core page is next revised. The Montgomery design, a hearing only when private property is actually touched, is a clean model if Frederick has a similar majority-consent rule.

The Kensington episode is a disability-access story as much as a neighbor-conflict story, which matters for how Frederick frames gaps in the sidewalk network. Connects to the Golden Mile corridor crash data and Multimodal Access Plan and to [[Frederick Streets, Traffic Safety, and Transit]] on accessible sidewalks. Worth watching the November 10 testimony for the strongest opposition arguments.
