# Water Bills Rising Twice as Fast as Incomes in Maryland

## Summary
A Food and Water Watch analysis of the 500 largest U.S. water systems found bills rose 62 percent from 2015 to 2025, faster than inflation and incomes, and that Maryland is one of four states where water bills grew about twice as fast as household incomes.

## Key Points
Food and Water Watch analyzed the 500 largest community water providers, serving about 155 million people. Average bills rose 62 percent between 2015 and 2025, faster than overall inflation and more than twice the rate of grocery prices. Bills grew 19 percent faster than household incomes nationally, so households spend a growing share of income on water.

In Louisiana, Maryland, New Hampshire, and West Virginia, water bills grew about twice as fast as household incomes. Annual household cost ranged from $286 in Idaho to $1,383 in West Virginia. For-profit water companies charge more than publicly owned systems (FWW advocates against privatization, so read that finding with the source in mind).

Costs will keep rising. The American Water Works Association estimates drinking water systems need up to $2.4 trillion over 25 years, and if users alone bear it, bills could more than double by 2050. FWW recommends keeping systems public and locally controlled, holding polluters accountable for contamination, preventing tech, fossil fuel, and agriculture sectors from passing costs to residential users, and federal funding for compliance projects.

Context: NEADA projects heating oil about 50 percent higher this winter and average home heating costs up 8.7 percent, more than twice inflation.

## Relevance
Maryland being in the twice-as-fast group is a citable state-level data point for the WAVE Fines Act and the broader water shutoff prevention package, both of which assume water costs are outrunning what ALICE households can absorb. Frederick's system is public, which is the lower-cost model in this analysis, so the argument is about rate design and relief rather than ownership. The recommendation to stop large industrial users from shifting costs to residential ratepayers connects directly to the data center fight: any future data center water demand in the city should come with a rate structure that protects residential customers.
