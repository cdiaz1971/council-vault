# Free Transit Is Not Enough -- The Disability Access Gap

## Summary

Free transit fares are politically popular but address only one barrier to mobility for disabled people; the real access issues are reliability, elevator maintenance, paratransit reform, mobility device charging, accessible parking parity, and protected multi-use lanes.

## Key Points

Written by the founding executive director and managing director of the Disability Culture Lab, drawing on conversations with dozens of disabled activists, policy experts, and academics. The authors argue that free buses help but that the bigger accessibility needs are not getting the same headlines.

**The six asks:**

1. **Reliable, frequent service.** Strict schedules in 5-, 10-, or 15-minute intervals allow disabled riders to plan with confidence. Missing a bus when there's no backup option is not a minor inconvenience.

2. **Protected mobility lanes.** Bike lanes should be multi-use mobility lanes -- for power chairs, deaf cyclists, and others -- separated from both pedestrians and motor traffic. Low-cost delineator posts can reduce crashes by up to 53%.

3. **Accessible parking parity.** Current data from 20+ states shows only nine accessible parking spots per 100 vehicles registered with disability plates. Every school, business, playground, shopping center, and voting location should have accessible parking.

4. **Public charging for mobility devices.** Dead power chair batteries are dangerous and financially devastating. Bus stops, train stations, and subway stations should have universal charging stations for e-bikes, power chairs, and other mobility devices -- not just electric cars.

5. **Two working elevators at every underground station.** ADA requires it; implementation is not universal. NYC MTA data shows nearly 10% of elevators out of service at any given time. A broken elevator stranded one of the authors in San Francisco, adding 30 minutes and a $125 rideshare to a planned BART trip.

6. **Paratransit reform.** Many programs require in-person visits to apply -- a catch-22 when accessible transit is what you're applying for. Appointments must be made weeks in advance. Mobility aids are frequently damaged by poorly-trained privatized workers. The authors call for unionized, highly-trained paratransit professionals and simplified application processes.

The closing argument: when cities prioritize accessibility and communicate clearly about it, the result is better transit for everyone. Free buses may win elections. Accessible transportation is what makes cities livable.

## Relevance

Frederick's active transportation and parks planning -- [[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]], [[Bus Service]], [[Road Diet]] -- tends to treat accessibility as a compliance question rather than a design principle. The distinction the authors draw between mobility (speed, coverage) and access (can a specific person actually get where they need to go) maps onto the existing vault notes [[Mobility vs Accessibility -- The Urban Planning Distinction]] and [[The Myth of Mobility -- Speed-Optimized Cities Reduce Access]]. The elevator and paratransit points are less directly applicable to Frederick (no subway), but the mobility lane and accessible parking arguments are.
