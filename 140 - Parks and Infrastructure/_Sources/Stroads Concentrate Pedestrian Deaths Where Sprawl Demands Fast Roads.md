# Stroads Concentrate Pedestrian Deaths Where Sprawl Demands Fast Roads

## Summary
A teenager's death on Memphis's Union Avenue illustrates how stroads, wide multi-lane streets carrying fast traffic through places people live and shop, invite speeding, leave crossings poorly lit, and make the Sunbelt the most dangerous region for pedestrians. Sprawl is a root cause.

## Key Points
A 16-year-old was struck and killed on Union Avenue in Memphis, which the Wall Street Journal (Rebecca Cadenhead and Andrew Mollica) uses as its case for a fatal flaw in American road design. Union Avenue is a stroad, a hybrid that tries to be both a street serving destinations and a road moving traffic fast, and does neither safely.

Three design features drive the danger. Width and straightness encourage speeding and inattentive driving. Multiple lanes lengthen every crossing. And the sheer width usually means the roadway is poorly lit at night, so pedestrians crossing in the dark are hard to see.

The geography follows the design. The Sunbelt is now the most dangerous part of the country for pedestrians, and stroads are spreading there fastest.

Nicholas Ferenchak, who directs the Center for Pedestrian and Bicyclist Safety at the University of New Mexico, ties stroads to sprawl. When housing is built far from jobs, places have to build big, fast roads to connect them, so the road problem is downstream of a land use problem.

## Relevance
Route 40 through the Golden Mile is Frederick's clearest stroad, and it runs entirely through District 2. The [[Golden Mile Small Area Profile]] counts 576 car crashes, 42 non-motorist crashes, and 25 severe or fatal injuries on the corridor from 2020 to 2024, in the census tracts with the city's highest ALICE share. This article supplies a national frame for why those numbers are a design outcome rather than driver behavior, which matters as the Golden Mile Small Area Plan moves from Profile to recommendations and as the Multimodal Access Plan shared-use path is designed.

The lighting point is concrete and cheap to act on, and pairs with [[Lighting and Greening as Low-Cost Public Safety Infrastructure]]. The sprawl point reinforces [[Vision Zero Requires Land Use Reform, Not Just Bike Infrastructure]], since redeveloping the corridor's underutilized commercial parcels into mixed use is both a housing strategy and a traffic safety strategy. [[Road Diet Feasibility Criteria From the FHWA Guide]] is the practical next step for testing whether segments of Route 40 qualify for reconfiguration. The same crash data supports the E-Bike (EPMD) Ordinance when it returns to full Council.
