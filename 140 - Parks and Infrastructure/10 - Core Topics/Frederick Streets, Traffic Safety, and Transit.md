# Frederick Streets, Traffic Safety, and Transit

The purpose of transportation policy is safe, reliable access to daily needs. Council decisions should connect crash prevention, accessible walking routes, street maintenance, transit service and land use rather than evaluate vehicle speed alone.

**Reviewed September 29, 2026.** This page distinguishes verified primary material, dated local briefs, draft proposals and research examples. The live Legislative Roadmap remains the source for current legislative workflow; it was not refreshed during this review.

## Authority: identify the owner first

| Actor | Main responsibility | Practical City lever |
|---|---|---|
| City of Frederick | City streets, local capital work, land use and municipal operations | Budget, street standards, maintenance priorities, project approvals and coordination |
| Frederick County Transit | County bus operations and service planning | Partnership on routes, frequency, service span and accessible stops |
| Maryland SHA / MDOT / MTA | State highways, state transportation programs and MARC | State approval and funding requests; coordinate designs and station access |
| Private owners / developers | Facilities and obligations established by ownership and approvals | Enforce applicable standards and agreements; verify maintenance responsibility |

A street inside City limits is not necessarily City-owned. A bus stop needs both an operating service and a usable pedestrian route. School access may involve City, County, FCPS and state assets.

The September 8 brief reports 360+ centerline miles and 1,300+ lane miles of City streets. Those figures were not independently reconciled to an asset register in this review; retain them as **reported figures**, not validated denominators for cost-per-mile comparisons. Centerline miles, lane miles and sidewalk miles measure different things. [[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-09-08]].

## Local baseline: highest-value decision evidence

| Area | Dated evidence | Implication and limit |
|---|---|---|
| Golden Mile safety | 2020–2024 profile reports 576 vehicle crashes, 42 non-motorist crashes and 25 severe/fatal injuries | Crashes and injured people are different units; obtain underlying geocoded data and exposure |
| Sidewalk condition | August 25 brief: 343 miles surveyed; average SCI 78; 46 miles/740 segments Poor or Very Poor | A citywide mean can hide broken routes and district disparities |
| Curb ramps | 3,841 of 6,196 inspected ramps failed the survey's compliance review (62%) | Treat as an accessibility program need; fixing one defect does not certify the full route |
| Pavement | September 8 briefing relies on a 2021–2022 survey and a five-year plan | Refresh condition and costs before asserting current backlog or optimal sequencing |
| Traffic calming | March 19 briefing lists completed and upcoming sites | That list is a March status snapshot, not confirmation of September completion |

Sources: [[Golden Mile Small Area Profile]]; [[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-08-25]]; [[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-09-08]]; [[Hearing Brief 2026-03-19]].

For the Golden Mile, the retained profile identifies Hillcrest Drive and Key Parkway as High Injury Network corridors, Hillcrest traffic calming as CIP 340009, and Golden Mile multimodal access as CIP 380009. The proposed US 40 path and bus/right-turn lane describe a design, not an operating facility. Confirm the adopted scope, SHA coordination, funding and construction schedule before promising delivery.

## Sidewalks and ADA: prioritize complete usable routes

The August survey brief reports SCI 79 inside Equity Emphasis Areas versus 77 outside, but District 3 averaged 71 and District 5 averaged 84. Similar group averages do not establish equal access: missing links, crossings, destinations, shade and disability barriers may differ.

Of the 3,841 failing ramps, 3,282 reportedly lacked detectable warnings. That common defect suggests a possible early work package, but grade, cross-slope, landing space, connections and other requirements still matter. A detectable-warning retrofit alone is not proof of ADA compliance. [[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-08-25]].

Planning estimates in that brief are $5.0–5.8 million for priority sidewalk segments, $11.2–53.4 million for grade-differential treatments, and $19.5–20.5 million for ramps. These are FY2026 planning prices, not bids. Confirm overlap, alternative treatments, contingencies and phasing before adding them into a single backlog. The cited $3.5 million in committed funding also needs reconciliation between operating funds, CIP and any expected recoveries.

A defensible first phase links homes to schools, bus stops, parks and essential services; fixes serious hazards; and publishes the ranking and rationale for exceptions. The briefing's proposed scoring gives 50 points to condition and the other 50 to schools, Equity Emphasis Areas, downtown and transit. Test whether that weighting actually reaches residents with the greatest barriers.

## Safety tools: select treatments for the corridor

FHWA identifies road diets among proven countermeasures. The Road Diet Informational Guide's screening numbers treat average daily traffic at or below 20,000 vehicles as a good candidate for evaluation, with peak-hour directional volume as a secondary test: an Iowa DOT threshold cited in the guide treats 750 vehicles per hour in the peak direction as probably feasible, 750–875 as needing caution, and above 875 as less likely without added capacity analysis. [FHWA Road Diet Informational Guide, FHWA-SA-14-028](https://highways.dot.gov/sites/fhwa.dot.gov/files/2022-06/rdig.pdf). Its cited **19–47% overall crash reduction** concerns studied conversions of four-lane undivided roads and is context-dependent: the guide's own documented conversions show roughly 47% for small-town sites and roughly 19% for suburban corridors near larger metro areas, with 29% offered as a combined estimate. A Golden Mile-type suburban arterial should be argued toward the lower end of that range, not the headline 47%, and it is not a guaranteed Frederick outcome. [FHWA road-diet evidence](https://highways.dot.gov/safety/other/road-diets/road-diet-desk-reference/2-why-consider-road-diet). Traffic volume is a screening consideration, not a universal approval threshold. Assess turning movements, intersections, buses, emergency access, loading, pedestrian demand and diversion before design. Reviewing scheduled resurfacing is a low-cost way to identify candidate streets, since restriping during a planned overlay is the cheapest point to reconfigure a corridor.

Other options include shorter crossings, visibility improvements, speed management, protected connections, signal timing, refuge islands and lighting, chosen for the actual hazard. Education can support safe use, but it does not repair a design that predictably exposes people to high-speed traffic. [FHWA countermeasure library](https://highways.dot.gov/safety/proven-safety-countermeasures).

For a tactical project, define the safety problem, accessible temporary layout, materials, maintenance owner, before/after measurements, trial duration and criteria for permanent installation or revision. Do not count installed posts or paint as the final safety outcome.

Peer-city stories can illustrate a treatment or delivery method. Their fatality-free periods, national rankings and before/after changes do not prove that one intervention caused the result. Avoid transferring claimed percentage effects without comparable roads, populations, exposure and study design.

## Transit: build on the existing fare-free baseline

Frederick County's current FAQ says Transit services are free. The local question is therefore how to improve **frequency, service span, reliability, connections and disability access while sustaining funding**, rather than whether to introduce free fares. [County Transit FAQ](https://frederickcountymd.gov/m/FAQ).

Ask the County for route-level headways, early/late and weekend service, missed trips, crowding, on-time performance, transfer waits and rider priorities. City work can remove stop-access barriers, provide suitable waiting areas, improve crossings and coordinate housing and destinations around useful service. Measure door-to-door access, including the walk or roll to the stop.

The retained $95-per-revenue-hour example yields $98,800 for 1,040 additional annual hours (four hours × five days × 52 weeks). It is an **illustrative calculation**, not a County quote: the agency/mode record, wage year, vehicles, layover, deadhead, supervision and marginal cost were not established. Obtain an operating plan and recurring funding estimate before making a budget commitment. [[Bus Service]].

Foreign and peer-region frequency-versus-fares essays are hypotheses to test locally, not evidence that Frederick riders have the same priorities or costs. Accessible fixed routes and paratransit also serve different needs; an improvement to one does not automatically replace the other.

**FY2027 funding:** the City budget is adopted. The earlier $150,000 tactical-calming and $150,000 solar-crosswalk requests remain **unverified individual line items** in this review; do not advertise either as funded from the priorities deck alone. Reconcile them with adopted accounts and project scope. The September pavement briefing's $3.3 million operations, $4.7 million Highway User Revenue and $3.5 million CIP 320029 are different measures, not a single available balance. [Adopted budget resources](https://cityoffrederickmd.gov/1894/FY2027-Budget); [[Tactical Traffic Calming Program -- Frederick]].

**Let's Move Frederick:** the City webpage reviewed here still labels its download Public Draft 7. That supports treating it as a draft resource, not proof of adoption or a reliable adoption date. The April planning brief's FY2027 expectation and MARC TOD application need a subsequent resolution/designation check. [City plan page](https://www.cityoffrederickmd.gov/1496/Comprehensive-Bicycle-Pedestrian-Plan); [[Budget Brief - Planning 2026-04-15]].

**Speed monitoring:** August meeting records report G-26-4 passing 5–0–1. The primary PDF retrieved for this review is explicitly the **June 18 introduction**, with graduated $40–$425 penalties; it is not the signed final ordinance. For public fine guidance, verify the enacted text, effective date and current state requirements. Do not infer expanded camera-location authority from a penalty change. [Introduction text](https://cityoffrederick.granicus.com/MetaViewer.php?event_id=5654&meta_id=200181&view_id=12); [[Hearing Minutes 2026-08-20]].

**State capital funding:** the retained six-year-plan article is a dated funding snapshot. “No new projects” in a draft plan does not mean existing projects stop, nor that every future City request is legally barred. Verify the current MDOT program and project-specific commitments. [[Maryland's FY2027 Six-Year Transportation Plan Funds No New Capital Projects]].

## Micromobility: evaluate the latest draft, not an older summary

The September 23 EPMD text is a **draft**, covering device classifications, shared-path and sidewalk access, operation, rentals and penalties. Its general shared-path permission differs from the September 1 default discussed in the September 8 committee brief, while named-path restrictions remain. Treat those as successive versions, not simultaneous rules. [[E-Bike Ordinance-092326]]; [[Public Works Committee Brief 2026-09-08]].

Before introduction or a vote, resolve:

- Classification under current Maryland law, including the distinction between e-bikes and motor vehicles.
- Actual conflicts on particular paths, route alternatives and access for people using devices for transportation.
- Disability access: wheelchairs and other power-driven mobility devices require their applicable ADA analysis. General recreational-device restrictions cannot substitute for that analysis. [DOJ mobility-device guidance](https://www.ada.gov/resources/opdmds/).
- Passenger and modification rules for family/cargo use; enforceability; penalty proportionality; signage and staff costs.
- Codification: confirm chapter/article placement against other enacted ordinances.
- The evidence behind an equity conclusion, including disability, income, language and geography.

Draft fine amounts and bans are not current public instructions. Track the initiative in [[Parks and Infrastructure - In-Progress Plans]] and verify live status before describing it as introduced or adopted.

## Options and tradeoffs

| Option | Value | Main tradeoff or implementation test |
|---|---|---|
| Phase sidewalk/ramp repairs around complete routes | Makes daily trips possible | Cost, ownership, utility conflicts and route-level compliance |
| Pair resurfacing with safety redesign | Avoids repeated construction | Design lead time, emergency/transit coordination and community access |
| Pilot targeted calming | Tests a layout relatively quickly | Maintenance, accessible temporary materials and credible evaluation |
| Coordinate a County service improvement | Reduces waiting and expands useful access | Recurring operating support and County delivery capacity |
| Publish a condition-and-risk capital ranking | Makes priorities reviewable | Data quality, transparent weighting and justified exceptions |
| Set rules for mobility data and new devices | Protects access and accountability | Legal authority, vendor dependence, privacy and enforcement cost |

Complaint volume alone is a weak priority measure because reporting access differs. Combine complaints with proactive inspections and mapped risk. Commercial telematics, robot or parking data can add information, but sampling, accuracy, retention and sharing rights need review. Corporate partnerships do not establish that Frederick rider data reaches ICE. See [[ICE Surveillance and Data]].

## Council checklist and performance measures

For each proposal: identify owner and authority; specify the safety/access problem; show baseline and alternatives; provide lifecycle cost and funding; assess disability and neighborhood effects; coordinate utilities and maintenance; and set evaluation dates.

Track fatal/serious injuries, speeds and conflicts alongside exposure; route accessibility and ramp repair completion; pavement condition by district; access to frequent service and essential destinations; reliability and transfer waits; and the share of high-priority projects delivered. Report project stages and survey dates. Small annual crash counts fluctuate, so use multi-year trends and avoid declaring success from a short quiet period.
