# Frederick Parks and Recreation

Frederick's parks decisions concern the quality, reach and long-term affordability of a public service: usable places, accessible routes, recreation programs, shade and community space. Total acreage and capital spending are useful inputs, but neither establishes that residents can use the system.

**Reviewed September 29, 2026.** The adopted master plan, current City program pages and dated local briefs serve different purposes. Historical estimates and draft initiatives below are labeled accordingly.

## Authority and decision frame

| Actor | Role | Council question |
|---|---|---|
| City Council and administration | Municipal budgets, capital priorities, local policy and project oversight | What service gap is being addressed, with what recurring commitment? |
| Parks and Recreation / partner departments | Operations, programs, facilities, trees and project delivery | Are staffing, maintenance, accessibility and lifecycle costs funded? |
| Planning and development review | Applicable parkland dedication, credits, fees and approvals | What is publicly usable, when will it be delivered, and who maintains it? |
| County and FCPS | County parks, schools and potential shared-use partnerships | Is access guaranteed by an agreement, with responsibilities and hours? |
| Maryland DNR and grant agencies | State parks and relevant state funding | What restrictions, match and continuing obligations attach to funds? |

State parks such as Gambrill are not City facilities. School grounds, HOA common space and privately credited amenities are not automatically equivalent to unrestricted municipal parkland.

## Adopted plan and local baseline

The City confirms that the Parks and Recreation Master Plan was **adopted September 7, 2023**. Its adoption date should replace the earlier page's inference from Husky Park research. [City master-plan page](https://www.cityoffrederickmd.gov/1480/PR-Master-Plan).

The retained plan synthesis describes goals for organizational capacity, financial sustainability, recreation programs, facilities, and trails/greenways. César's January priority list—renovation, connectivity, equity, system improvements, targeted expansion and sustainability—is an analytical reading, **not an adopted ranked table**. Likewise, [[Parks Plan vs Current CIP]] is a useful internal assessment, not an independent performance audit.

| Measure in retained records | What it establishes | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| 73 developed parks and 12 undeveloped properties | Inventory reported in the plan/budget notes | A reconciled September 2026 asset register |
| 928 GIS acres and 75,281 residents | Plan's 2020 basis, about 12.3 acres per 1,000 | Today's service ratio |
| Approximately 69–70 undeveloped acres | Land-bank estimate in the research | Land ready for public use or free of constraints |
| 11.5 acres per 1,000 in April budget materials | A separately reported benchmark | A directly comparable ratio without the denominator |
| 336 programs and proximity of 0.5–1 mile | Reported activity and proximity measures | Unique users, affordability or safe walk/roll access |

Sources: [[Frederick Parks & Recreation Master Plan]]; [[Husky Park Master Plan Figures To Verify 2026-08-27]]; [[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]].

The plan's calculation applies its 2020 ratio to a **projected 2025 population of 79,344**, producing approximately 978 acres, or 50 more than its 928-acre baseline. That is a dated planning scenario, not a measured current deficit. The rounded 950-acre narrative, GIS acreage, undeveloped inventory and later Westside investments need a common date and definition before aggregation. A separate 25-year Comprehensive Plan estimate uses a different method.

**Next baseline request:** a GIS inventory showing ownership, developed versus undeveloped acreage, public access, easements, facility condition, accessibility, canopy, maintenance obligations and the population year used. Compare actual walking routes and crossings, not just circles around parks.

## Capital priorities and delivery

Use the master plan to evaluate each project against documented need, asset condition, access, growth, sustainability and operating capacity. A plan citation should explain the connection rather than act as a box checked.

The January source list identifies playground replacement (410034), Hill Street renovation (410039), sports courts (410032), Equity Emphasis Area improvements (410038) and Community Bridge restoration (410037). The source does not establish a common adopted CIP cycle or current completion status. Trail projects may sit in transportation accounts; avoid counting them twice. [[CIP Park Projects]].

The April brief reports a Hill Street skate-park contract and an anticipated late-winter 2027 completion. Treat the date as a forecast until a current construction report confirms it. Community Bridge's contract/dispute requires its own current file, not an assumption that the earlier disagreement has been resolved. [[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]; [[FY2027 -- Community Bridge Restoration Project -- Overview]].

### Westside: distinguish the park from the community center

The City's current project page describes **outdoor Phase I amenities under construction** and the community center **in design**. It lists a separate **$10 million community-center budget** (CIP 110011), comprising $750,000 design and $9.25 million construction, with construction anticipated in 2027–2028 after design and approvals. It reports approximately **$23.6 million invested to date in the park** (CIP 411202). These are different project/cost measures, not proof that the entire park is completed or fully funded. [City Westside project page](https://www.cityoffrederickmd.gov/1799/Westside-Regional-Park).

For Council review, request the approved phase scope, contingency, procurement milestones, escalation exposure, staffing, utilities, maintenance, program plan and any County cost-sharing agreement. The capital allocation alone does not fund a sustainable operating model.

## Parkland dedication and Husky Park

**Development contributions:** the September 17 Worman's Mill Court item proposed a $240,000 fee associated with 2.93 acres and described private amenity credits. An agenda listing does not independently establish final approval, collection or expenditure. Check the executed resolution, applicable LMC §608 provisions, valuation, credits, fund restrictions and intended service area. Private amenities should be described by their access conditions. [[Hearing Brief 2026-09-17]].

**Husky Park / Bond Street:** retained research identifies a 22.58-acre PRK-zoned parcel at 530 Highland Street, used as a DPW yard, with the proposed Bond Street extension under CIP 320044. Park designation, current use and readiness for recreation are separate facts. The older $1.25 million DPW-relocation entry (120009) is an unfunded planning reference, not a current funded relocation commitment. [[Workshop Brief - Husky Park Rebuttal 2026-08-27]].

The September 10 brief reports staff correcting the easement record: the 1995 forest-conservation easement had not been extinguished and would require a formal release. The environmental-assessment concern is a **record gap**; failure to locate a Phase I/II report does not prove none exists or establish contamination. The cited 3.96 acres is a construction-disturbance figure, **not established permanent parkland loss**. [[Workshop Brief 2026-09-10]].

Before a project decision, reconcile title/easement documents, environmental due diligence, permanent road footprint, temporary disturbance, floodplain approvals, yard relocation, public-access consequences, alternatives and costs. Keep current procedural status in the live Roadmap. See [[Frederick Water, Sewer, and Stormwater]] for floodplain context.

## Operations and budget: correct the comparison first

The April budget brief reports full-department actual spending of approximately **$9.225 million in FY2023, $10.307 million in FY2024 and $12.168 million in FY2025**. Its $1.44 million memo summary covers a smaller scope and is not comparable with full-department totals. FY2026 year-to-date spending is also not a completed-year result. [[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]].

The FY2027 budget is now adopted. The April hearing's salary, overtime, equipment and youth-coordinator requests remain proposal-stage figures until matched to adopted accounts. [City adopted-budget resources](https://cityoffrederickmd.gov/1894/FY2027-Budget).

One arithmetic correction is definite: **$150,000 to $202,500 is a $52,500 or 35% electricity increase**, not the 25% stated in the original page and brief. That calculation does not explain the cause; ask whether rates, facilities, usage or account changes drove it. Similarly, salary growth alone cannot distinguish added positions from compensation adjustments.

For each new facility, require a five-year operating estimate and funding source, including maintenance, utilities, staffing, accessibility, equipment replacement and insurance. Compare service levels and facility workload alongside spending; nominal growth alone does not establish waste or adequate resources.

## Reduced Registration: actual benefit and access barriers

The current City program page says **eligible youth under 18 receive 50% off youth programs**. It lists qualification routes through school-meal eligibility, Food Supplement Program/Temporary Cash Assistance households, or specified Housing Authority communities, with residency/documentation requirements. Once approved, households may register online, in person or by phone. This is broader than the earlier school-meal-only description. [City Reduced Registration Program](https://cityoffrederick.com/1615/Reduced-Registration-Program-RRP).

The April brief reports 399 qualifying households and $8,767 in savings during FY2026 to date: about **$21.97 per qualifying household**. That aggregate average is **not a household subsidy cap** and does not by itself prove the discount is inadequate. It can reflect nonparticipation, program mix, partial-year timing or how households were counted.

Request the number of enrolled households that actually registered, participating children, registrations and savings per active household, waitlists, uncovered costs, eligibility processing time and reasons for nonuse. Test documentation, language, transportation, scheduling and remaining fees. If affordability remains the barrier, compare a larger discount or scholarship with outreach, simplified enrollment and additional program capacity.

## Trees, shade and environmental performance

The April brief reports a **$494,000 Urban Trees grant for 1,190 planned trees**, with planting beginning in March. Confirm actual planting, locations, grant obligations and care arrangements rather than turning the target into a completed count. [[Budget Brief - Parks and Recreation 2026-04-15]]; [[Maryland 5 Million Trees Initiative -- Urban Forestry and Equity]].

Prioritize heat exposure and usable shade at playgrounds, routes and gathering places. Tree count is an output; establishment, survival and shade are outcomes. Budget watering, protection, pruning and replacements, and measure survival at one, three and five years. The research note's general 30% mortality claim is not a measured Frederick survival rate.

Coordinate planting with utilities, sightlines, accessibility and stormwater needs. Pair long-term canopy with suitable near-term shade where children or other users face immediate heat exposure.

## Policy options and tradeoffs

| Option | Best use | Main cost or constraint |
|---|---|---|
| Renew existing facilities using condition and access scores | Addresses deterioration and barriers | Construction disruption, lifecycle cost and transparent prioritization |
| Improve connected routes and neighborhood access | Makes existing parks usable to more residents | Coordination with streets, crossings and adjacent owners |
| Expand fee assistance and program capacity | Addresses documented exclusion | Recurring subsidy, staffing and evidence about actual barriers |
| Acquire or activate land where gaps are demonstrated | Serves growth or underserved areas | Purchase, constraints, development and ongoing maintenance |
| Add system-wide wayfinding and asset tracking | Improves usability and delivery oversight | Staff capacity, accessible design and ongoing data maintenance |
| Partner with County/FCPS or community organizations | Shares facilities and regional costs | Written access, scheduling, liability and maintenance commitments |
| Fund shade and tree establishment | Reduces heat exposure over time | Care costs, site suitability and delayed canopy benefits |

The state reservation/QR-payment examples are outside comparisons, not a proposed City-wide admission system. Any City fee or vendor proposal would need evidence of crowding or revenue need, alternatives for people without smartphones or bank cards, transparent total charges, procurement review and limits on visitor-data use. Higher collections alone are not proof of better access.

## Council checklist and measures

Before approval, require the service gap; ownership and restrictions; alternatives; capital and annual operating cost; funding conditions; accessibility and equity assessment; delivery schedule; maintenance owner; and performance measures.

Track condition backlog and completed repairs; safe walk/roll access by neighborhood; accessible facilities and programs; unique users and waitlists; assistance participation and benefit distribution; hours of service; cost per service unit; project milestones; and tree survival/canopy. Protect participant privacy and distinguish registrations from unique people.
