# Frederick Water, Sewer, and Stormwater

Frederick's central choices are how to maintain reliable service, meet water-quality and drainage-safety obligations, manage flood exposure, and keep essential service affordable. Capital spending, rates, operations and land-use decisions must be evaluated together.

**Reviewed September 29, 2026.** Meeting briefs below are dated evidence. An agenda item is not a vote, an appropriation is not a completed project, and a proposed program is not an existing entitlement.

## Systems and authority

| System | What it does | Council decision |
|---|---|---|
| Drinking water | Treats and distributes potable water | Reliability, capacity, rates, source protection and renewal |
| Sanitary sewer / wastewater | Collects sewage and treats effluent | Permit compliance, process capacity, operations and capital |
| Stormwater | Manages runoff and drainage infrastructure | Safety, flood reduction, water quality, maintenance and funding |
| Floodplain and levees | Manages development exposure and flood-protection infrastructure | Land use, project oversight and coordination with MDE/FEMA |

The City operates municipal systems; Frederick County facilities and regional utilities are separate operators. MDE regulates relevant water and discharge permits. FEMA mapping and accreditation processes do not replace City engineering or MDE approvals. Identify the asset owner and regulator before assigning responsibility.

Water and Sewer and Stormwater are separate enterprise funds. Their financing through rates and fees creates cost-recovery constraints, but does **not** remove Council's choices about rates, assistance, investment timing and service standards. The City now identifies FY2027 as an **adopted** budget; figures from spring hearings remain proposal-stage evidence unless reconciled to adopted accounts. [City FY2027 budget and capital resources](https://cityoffrederickmd.gov/1894/FY2027-Budget); [[FY 2027 Budget -- City Fund Types Reference]].

## Local baseline and evidence limits

- **Asset renewal:** August records identify fall-protection work at 13 water/sewer facilities, nitrate instruments at the wastewater plant and a Linganore treatment-plant pump. These are evidence of particular procurements, not a complete asset-condition inventory. The $1.45 million contract and $1.55 million purchase-order figure should not be added as separate projects without reconciling their relationship. [[Hearing Brief 2026-08-20]]; [[Hearing Minutes 2026-08-06]].
- **Financing:** the late-2023 bond issue and credit rating in [[Frederick Bond Information]] are historical. Before a new financing decision, obtain current outstanding debt, coverage, reserves, rate assumptions and applicable Charter treatment; a debt-cap exemption is not unlimited repayment capacity.
- **Development:** G-26-11 concerns collection timing for development fees; use the adopted ordinance and its applicability provisions for a specific project. A separate affordable-housing recommendation to defer or waive connection fees is a proposal, not a blanket existing waiver. [[Hearing Brief 2026-09-03]]; [[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]].
- **Supply:** regional Potomac projections and staff comments about limited land/water support further analysis, not a quantified City shortage. Require a City-specific supply-and-demand model, drought assumptions, permitted capacity, peak demand and consumptive use before commitments to major new users. [[Data Centers -- Potomac River Basin Water Impact Projections]]; [[Behind the Spires City Update 07-07-2026]].

## Wastewater phosphorus: compliance before expansion assumptions

The retained April 29 reporting states that the City plant discharged **10,827 pounds of phosphorus in 2025 against a 5,720-pound annual limit**, approximately **1.89 times** the limit, with exceedances in four of five years. It reports MDE considering penalties and City work with Moonshot Missions. This review has not obtained the underlying discharge-monitoring reports or a later enforcement disposition. A reported maximum daily penalty is **exposure, not an assessed fine**. [[Frederick Wastewater Plant -- Chronic Phosphorus Discharge Violations]].

The immediate oversight package should include the permit and reporting period, monthly loads and flows, influent trends, operational causes, corrective actions, responsible staff, expected completion dates and MDE correspondence. Compare operational adjustments with capital improvements using expected compliance performance, lifecycle cost and implementation risk. A recurring exceedance warrants investigation; the clipping alone does not establish the engineering cause or prove treatment capacity is exhausted.

The golf course's reported use of 16–31 million gallons of treated effluent annually during 2020–2025 is a reuse baseline. It is not automatically an equivalent amount of new potable-water capacity or a credit against every discharge requirement. [[Frederick Golf Course Effluent Irrigation Data -- Water Use Comparison for Data Center Debate]].

## Mason's Law: enacted obligations and next milestones

**Chapter 356 (SB 189)** was approved **May 12, 2026**, effective **October 1, 2026**. The enacted text, visually checked for deleted language, governs:

| Deadline | Requirement |
|---|---|
| July 1, 2027 | Inventory number and location of covered existing public and private open drainage inlets |
| April 1, 2028 | Develop a safety-prioritized improvement plan and notify affected private owners |
| April 1, 2031 | Improve covered inlets included in that plan |

The law defines covered inlets and exclusions; it does not require repair of every storm drain. Plans must explain exclusions for inlets lacking a legitimate public-safety risk. Improvements may include grating or fencing, with a hydraulic-capacity exception. Municipalities bear installation costs and may seek state cost-sharing. New covered inlets require approved grating. The private-property maintenance/liability provisions require careful application to the installed improvement. [Enacted law, particularly Local Government §4-112, pp. 5–9](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/Chapters_noln/CH_356_sb0189e.pdf).

**Frederick implementation questions:** Who owns the inventory, what records already exist, which assets require field visits, and how will staff document ownership, exclusions, hydraulic review, public risk, private-owner notification and funding? Put the work schedule and responsible department into the budget calendar now. The original news note's broader “all inlets” framing and May 13 signing date are superseded by the chapter text.

## Flood protection and development

**Carroll Creek levee:** June 4 records report a 6–0 approval of a $395,000 Bayland analysis contract for three segments under CIP 350024. Accreditation and a map revision are objectives of the work, not outcomes established by the contract award. Request the engineering milestones, submittal status, deficiencies, capital implications and resident communication plan. The cited 22 NAC 12 properties represent a reported exposure group; mandatory insurance depends on the eventual map and applicable financing requirements. [[Hearing Brief 2026-06-04]]; [[Hearing Notes 2026-06-04]].

**Gateway Property:** August 6 records report approval of rezoning G-26-7 with floodplain-grading permit conditions. Rezoning is not an MDE permit or a determination that downstream risk is resolved. Check the actual permit, approved plans, conditions and public-use restrictions before describing the site as ready. [[Hearing Brief - Gateway Property 2026-08-06]]; [[Hearing Minutes 2026-08-06]].

**Husky Park:** the source research reports approximately 1.04 acres in mapped Zone AE. Mapped acreage does not describe all drainage risk, and construction conditions should not be confused with bid authorization. Coordinate the floodplain review with the park, easement and road questions in [[Frederick Parks and Recreation]]. [[Workshop Brief - Husky Park Rebuttal 2026-08-27]].

## Utility affordability and shutoff prevention

The retained program note describes an administrative residential shutoff threshold above $350, payment plans and one-time leak forgiveness. Treat these as **documented practices at the note's date**, not permanent statutory rights or a guarantee for a particular account. Verify current written rules, notices, appeal procedures and eligibility with Finance. [[Water Shutoff Prevention -- Frederick Existing Programs and Proposed Legislative Actions]].

Reported leak credits were $68,742 across 55 accounts in FY2025 and $48,038 across 26 accounts in FY2026 **through February 17**. The second period is incomplete; it cannot establish an annual increase or decrease.

Pending ideas include interest relief during repayment, turn-on/turn-off fee flexibility, a funded assistance program, voluntary bill contributions and codification. Their dated roadmap status belongs in [[Parks and Infrastructure - In-Progress Plans]]. Before adoption, define eligibility, funding, landlord/tenant access, application burden, interaction with leak credits, service protections, fiscal controls and an appeal route.

## Options and tradeoffs

| Option | Why consider it | Cost and implementation test |
|---|---|---|
| Fund wastewater corrective actions with milestones | Reduces recurring compliance risk | Demonstrate expected performance; include operators, chemicals, energy and monitoring |
| Complete risk-based drainage inventory and improvements | Meets safety obligations and targets exposure | Fieldwork, engineering, private access, notification, grants and maintenance |
| Coordinate water, sewer, drainage and road projects | Avoids repeated excavation and disruption | Requires shared scheduling and clear cost allocation |
| Use green infrastructure or permeable surfaces where suitable | Can manage runoff and heat together | Test soils, loading, utilities, clogging and maintenance; no universal lifespan advantage |
| Create a targeted assistance and repayment framework | Protects access to essential service | Recurring revenue effect, fair eligibility and administrative capacity |
| Require lifecycle and growth-capacity analysis | Makes future costs visible before commitments | Staff/modeling cost; publish assumptions and uncertainty |

Regional sediment-enforcement and Potomac Interceptor stories are useful stress tests for inspection, redundancy, emergency contracting and public communication. They do not establish Frederick violations, City-system contamination or a current health advisory. Keep their dates and operator boundaries explicit. [[Developer Sediment Enforcement -- Maryland $4.1M Gunpowder River Settlement]]; [[Potomac Interceptor Collapse -- Aging Infrastructure Risk and Monitoring Gaps]]; [[Potomac Sewage Spill -- Sediment Persistence and Lingering Public Health Risk]].

## Council checklist and measures

Before authorizing spending, ask for: asset owner; legal/permit requirement; current condition; alternatives; capital and annual costs; fund and rate effects; procurement scope; completion milestones; maintenance owner; and distribution of benefits and burdens.

A quarterly dashboard should report:

- Water-main breaks, outages, losses, asset renewal and reserve/coverage measures.
- Wastewater permit exceedances, load trends and corrective-action completion.
- Covered drainage inlets inventoried, high-risk sites addressed and statutory milestones.
- Flood projects by engineering, permit, construction and accreditation stage.
- Arrears, shutoffs, reconnections, repayment completion and assistance reach, using privacy-protective aggregates.

Report denominators, time periods and adopted targets. Neither dollars spent nor complaints closed alone establishes safety or compliance.
