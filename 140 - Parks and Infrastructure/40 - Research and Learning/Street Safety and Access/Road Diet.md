# Road Diet (Roadway Reconfiguration)

## Reading context

This May 2025 learning reference preserves its general road-diet explanation. The retained FHWA PDF provides guidance; its presence does not establish that Frederick has implemented a particular road diet. The PDF remains in _Sources, so the original phrase "in this folder" reflects this note's former location. Screening volumes and expected crash reductions require corridor-specific engineering review. Use [[Frederick Streets, Traffic Safety, and Transit]] for the core reference and [[Road Diet Information Guide.pdf]] for the retained guidance.


A road diet converts a four-lane undivided roadway to a three-lane configuration: two through lanes plus a center two-way left-turn lane. The reclaimed space is reallocated to bike lanes, sidewalks, or turn pockets. When timed with scheduled repaving or overlay work, the primary cost is restriping -- making it one of the highest-value safety investments available at the local level.

## Safety Outcomes

The FHWA documents crash reductions of 19 to 47 percent on road diet corridors. 72% of pedestrian fatalities occur mid-block, precisely the condition road diets address by reducing vehicle speed differential and eliminating the weaving and passing behavior endemic to four-lane undivided roads. The center turn lane removes turning conflicts from through-traffic lanes, which is the primary mechanism behind crash reduction.

## Who Benefits

Road diets improve conditions for all road users: pedestrians gain safer crossing conditions and often new sidewalk space; cyclists gain dedicated lanes; drivers gain reduced conflict points and more predictable traffic flow. The "complete streets" framing is accurate here -- a road diet is typically the lowest-cost path to a complete streets outcome on an existing corridor.

## Application Conditions

Road diets work best on four-lane undivided segments carrying under approximately 20,000 vehicles per day. Above that threshold, the three-lane configuration may not accommodate peak traffic volumes without spillback. Traffic counts are the first analytical step before any proposal.

## Frederick Context

Frederick has existing road diet infrastructure (see the Road Diet Information Guide PDF in this folder). Any new corridor proposals should document: current ADT, pedestrian crash history, existing sidewalk gaps, and proximity to schools or transit stops. The cost-effectiveness argument is strongest when road diet work is bundled with a scheduled repaving project.
