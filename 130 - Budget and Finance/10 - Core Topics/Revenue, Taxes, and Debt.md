# Revenue, Taxes, and Debt

## Purpose

This page supports Council decisions on what the City taxes and at what rate, which revenue tools it can use, and how it borrows. Budget rules are in [[City Budget and Fiscal Condition]].

## Taxing authority

- The City may levy taxes on assessable property within its boundary, plus a general assessment on real property for water mains, sewers, streets, sidewalks, curbs, gutters, and storm sewers on any equitable basis Council sets (Charter Art. VII, § 9).
- All real and tangible personal property in the City is taxable for municipal purposes unless State law exempts it, using State assessments (§ 10(a)). Council may exempt or reduce the rate on agricultural property, exempt manufacturers' buildings and equipment, and adopt property tax credits authorized by State law (§ 10(b)-(d)).
- Taxes are due July 1; Council sets early-payment discounts and interest by resolution (§ 11).
- Maryland is one of nine states with no local sales tax; local governments rely on property and income taxes. A legislative panel is studying alternatives such as restaurant taxes ([[Maryland Is One of Nine States Without Local Sales Tax Authority]]).

## Current rates and reliance

- **Real property.** City residents pay three rates: City ($0.7055 per $100 in FY2026), the County's in-City rate ($1.0079), and the State ($0.112) ([Frederick County tax rates, 2025-26](https://frederickcountymd.gov/DocumentCenter/View/356864/TAX-RATES-2025-2026)). The FY2027 City rate: verify. The State rate has been 11.2 cents since 2007; residential assessments rose an average 13% in the latest cycle, phased in over three years ([[Maryland State Property Tax Rate Held at 11.2 Cents -- FY2027]]).
- **Business personal property.** The City levied $1.55 per $100 on personal property in 2022-23 ([SDAT tax rates](https://dat.maryland.gov/Documents/statistics/TaxRates_2022-2023.pdf)); Frederick County levies none on ordinary business property ([Maryland Commerce](https://commerce.maryland.gov/Documents/ResearchDocument/Frederickbef.pdf)). Current City rate: verify. This matters for large equipment-heavy uses such as data centers.
- **Reliance.** Real property tax supplies about 75.5% of General Fund revenue, 83.8% counting all taxes, more than Hagerstown's 65% to 70% ([[FY2026 Q3 Budget Update Analysis 2026-08-10]]).

## Payments in lieu of taxes (PILOT)

The City negotiates PILOT agreements, sometimes jointly with the County, to set payments for specific projects such as affordable housing. The Worman's Mill Court Apartments PILOT was pulled from the September 17, 2026 agenda ([[Hearing Minutes 2026-09-17]]). Terms and policy for PILOTs: verify whether the City has a written policy.

## Tax increment financing (TIF)

TIF freezes a district's assessed value and dedicates future growth above that baseline to repay bonds or project costs in the district ([[TIF]]). A 40-year review of Chicago's TIF districts found little of the growth inside them was attributable to TIF projects, that schools, parks, and other bodies lost access to that growth while districts were active, and that most captured revenue went downtown and to wealthier areas ([[Chicago Tax Increment Financing Study Finds Minimal Growth Attribution]]). Frederick's outstanding TIF obligations: verify.

## Debt

- The City may borrow on its full faith and credit. Bonds outstanding may not exceed 3% of the total assessed value of taxable property, excluding water and sewer bonds. Council must approve every bond issue, set terms, and approve sale prices (Charter Art. VII, § 13).
- Short-term borrowing is capped at $1 million in total, repaid from general taxes (§ 14).
- General obligation bonds are repaid from taxes and carry lower rates; revenue bonds are repaid from project revenue such as utility fees ([[Bond Concepts]]).
- Credit ratings, outstanding debt, and remaining capacity under the 3% limit: verify with Finance. Frederick County holds AAA ratings from all three agencies; the City's ratings are separate.

## Accounting standards

Strong Towns argues that governmental accounting standards understate infrastructure liabilities and the long-term cost of development patterns ([[GASB Accounting Standards -- Flaws in Municipal Finance and Public Comment Opportunity]]).

## Authority map

| Lever | Who controls it | Council role |
|---|---|---|
| Tax rates | Council, with budget | Direct |
| Exemptions and credits | Council, within State law | Direct |
| Fees | Council by ordinance | Direct |
| Sales or other new taxes | State legislature | Advocacy |
| Bonds | Council approval required | Direct |
| TIF districts and PILOTs | Council, by agreement | Direct |
| Assessments | State | None |

## Policy options

| Option | Addresses | Tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|
| Written PILOT and TIF policy with public benefit and reporting requirements | Deal-by-deal concessions | Less negotiating flexibility |
| Track and publish TIF and PILOT performance | Unverified benefits | Staff time |
| Review business personal property rate and exemptions | Fairness and large-equipment uses | Business opposition |
| Debt policy with capacity targets below the 3% limit | Credit stewardship | Limits borrowing |
| Advocate for local revenue options at the State | Property tax reliance | Uncertain at State level |

## Decision framework

- Who pays, by income and neighborhood?
- Is the revenue ongoing or one-time?
- What would the project have done without the incentive?
- What do other taxing bodies lose under a TIF?
- How much debt capacity remains, and what is it reserved for?

## Metrics

- Share of General Fund from property tax.
- Debt outstanding as a share of the 3% limit.
- PILOT and TIF revenue versus projections; affordable units delivered under PILOTs.
