# Priority-Driven Budgeting -- Concept Reference

## Reading context

This April 2026 research summary retains its GFOA source and method description. Use [[Priority-Driven Budgeting]] for the Council decision reference and [[Budget and Finance - In-Progress Plans]] for implementation options. The method summary does not establish that Frederick has adopted PDB.

## Summary

Priority-driven budgeting (PDB) is a structured alternative to incremental budgeting developed and documented by the Government Finance Officers Association. It replaces the traditional "last year plus adjustments" approach with a process that starts from available revenue, identifies community outcome priorities, scores every program against those priorities, and allocates funding in ranked order. VP Nash introduced the GFOA paper at the April 6 Government Operations Committee meeting.

## The Core Inversion

This is the single most important concept. Traditional budgeting asks: how much does each department need? PDB asks: what results does the community need, and which programs actually produce them? Everything else follows from that shift.

A related reframe: revenue comes first. PDB starts by establishing what money is actually available — and explicitly separates ongoing revenues from one-time sources — before any expenditure analysis begins. Departments do not submit "needs." The organization starts with a ceiling.

## The Eight Steps

**1. Identify available resources.** Establish the revenue ceiling before anything else. Distinguish ongoing revenues from one-time sources. Structural imbalance — one-time money funding ongoing operations — must be identified and named at this step.

**2. Identify priorities.** Define the community's strategic priorities as measurable, citizen-facing outcome statements. Not departmental objectives. Not mission statements. Results expressed in the voice of the citizen. Example from Mesa County: "People in our community feel safe." A strategic plan, if one exists, is the starting point.

**3. Define priority results precisely.** Use strategy maps — cause-and-effect diagrams — to clarify what each priority actually means for this specific community. "Safe community" means something different in Walnut Creek, CA than in Grand Island, NE. This step reveals the community's identity and sets the scoring criteria. Cross-functional staff teams build the maps, ideally with citizen input.

**4. Prepare decision units.** Break the budget into scoreable units. Two approaches: "offers" (customized service packages departments propose to achieve a result — more innovative, more disruptive) or "programs" (existing service clusters — less conceptual leap, faster to implement). Units should be large enough to be meaningful, small enough to be accountable. Rough guidance: not more than 10% or less than 1% of fund expenditures.

**5. Score decision units against priority results.** Each program or offer is scored on how effectively it advances the priority results. Scoring can be self-assessed by departments (with peer review) or done by cross-functional evaluation teams. Scores must be evidence-based and tied to the priority definitions established in Step 3.

**6. Compare scores.** Compile scores into a ranked list across all programs. This is the "moment of truth." The ranking should be logical and unsurprising if the prior steps were done well. Peer review and stakeholder engagement at this step build legitimacy.

**7. Allocate resources.** Draw a funding line on the ranked list at the point where cumulative program costs equal available revenue. Programs above the line are funded; below the line are not. The board and staff discuss programs near the line — redesigning, reducing service levels, or shifting resources between priority areas.

**8. Create accountability.** Funded programs must deliver the results they were scored on. Performance measures, program review decision trees, and efficiency audits (including Lean process review) close the loop. This step is the least mature in most implementations.

## Two Legitimacy Requirements

PDB requires both to succeed:

**Democratic legitimacy:** The priority-setting process genuinely reflects what residents value. Requires real public engagement — not just whoever shows up to a town hall. In Frederick's context, this means intentional outreach to Spanish-speaking residents and ALICE households, not just homeowners and civic regulars.

**Substantive legitimacy:** The scoring process is technically sound and consistently applied. Peer review and cross-functional evaluation teams are the primary mechanisms. Without an independent audit function, gaming the scoring is a real risk.

## Key Risks

- Requires executive branch buy-in to fully implement. Council cannot compel the program inventory or scoring without the Mayor's cooperation.
- Self-scored programs create moral hazard — departments inflate relevance scores. Peer review mitigates but does not eliminate this.
- Staff-intensive. Research participants often studied PDB for up to two years before launching. Full implementation is not a one-cycle project.
- Priority-setting can encode existing inequities if engagement is not deliberately inclusive.
