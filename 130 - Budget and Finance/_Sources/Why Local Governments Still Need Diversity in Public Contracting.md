# Why Local Governments Still Need Diversity in Public Contracting

## Reading context

This August 2026 article note is supporting source material. Use [[City Budget and Fiscal Condition]] for the core fiscal reference and [[FY2027 Budget -- Office of Vendor Engagement and Compliance (1116)]] for the dated department analysis. The article does not establish City policy or the present legal status of any contracting program.

## Summary

At the American Contract Compliance Association's National Training Institute, four former Atlanta mayors argued that minority-contracting programs remain sound economic development policy even as affirmative action and DEI face sustained legal and political attack, and identified two concrete tensions officials should plan for: emerging firms feeling shut out by repeat winners, and established minority firms getting pushed out of public work for being "too successful" while still facing private-sector discrimination.

## Key Points

Columnist Jabari Simama moderated a panel of four former Atlanta mayors (Bill Campbell, Shirley Franklin, Kasim Reed, Andrew Young) reflecting on 50 years of minority-contracting policy in Atlanta. Their consistent framing: this was never a social program bolted onto economic development, it was the economic development strategy, expanding the pool of businesses able to compete for public contracts to build companies, jobs, and wealth broadly. Reed specifically rejected the "we can't find qualified firms" excuse as a myth. Franklin traced Atlanta's growth from a minor rail town to a major metro directly to sustained inclusion.

On surviving the current legal and political attacks on affirmative action and DEI, the mayors' recurring advice was direct advocacy: compliance and procurement staff should communicate proactively with mayors, county executives, governors, and legislators rather than assume political leadership already understands what's at stake, and elected officials in turn need to actually listen and ask hard questions rather than react only to political winds.

Two structural tensions worth planning for, both surfaced directly at the conference: first, a participant's blunt complaint that "the same companies get all the business" points to a real risk that contracting programs calcify around already-established winners rather than opening pathways for new firms. Second, an established Black-owned contractor described succeeding on public joint ventures only to be excluded from the same majority partners' private-sector bids, illustrating how success in public contracting can paradoxically penalize a minority firm rather than help it grow, since majority partners may not carry that relationship into private work.

Simama's conclusion: the answer isn't choosing between emerging and established minority firms, effective programs need to build capacity ladders for both, while also addressing the underlying structural barriers (limited bonding capacity, undercapitalization, weak cash flow, restricted credit access) that constrain minority- and women-owned businesses regardless of how many contracts they win.

## Relevance

Useful framing and a concrete two-part risk model (repeat-winner concentration, and the "too successful for public work but still excluded from private work" trap) for any future look at how Frederick structures MBE/WBE participation in its own procurement and contracting processes, particularly given the current legal climate around DEI-labeled programs nationally.
