# Guinness Open Gate Closure Shows the Risk of Incentives Without Clawbacks

## Summary
Diageo is closing Guinness Open Gate in Baltimore County eight years after state and county officials changed brewery law, rezoned the site, and granted Enterprise Zone tax credits to land it. The county got no advance notice, and the remaining land is likely headed for logistics warehouses.

## Key Points
The Halethorpe site made alcohol from 1933 until Diageo, owner since 2001, closed the Seagram-era bottling plant in 2015 and laid off more than 100 workers. Diageo then reinvested about $90 million to open Guinness Open Gate in 2018 as a Dublin-style tourist brewery. Its last public day is Sunday, November 1.

Public support came in three forms. The General Assembly lifted the cap on on-site brewery production and sales and changed operating hours over objections from distributors and other brewers, after Diageo promised a "beer halo" that would grow the whole Maryland industry and said it had chosen Maryland over easier states. The county council rezoned the site. The state Department of Commerce called the Enterprise Zone credit critical to winning the project, and Diageo received $247,000 in credits from 2017 to 2022.

The retreat came in stages. Diageo ended large-scale brewing in 2023 and sold much of the land to MRP Industrial for $35.7 million for a logistics hub called Crossings 95. Baltimore Blonde production moved to New York. Current economic development director Jonathan Sachs said Diageo gave the county no notice and attributed the closure to the broader beer industry downturn: per the Brewers Association, closures of brewpubs and taprooms have outpaced openings since about 2023. Neighbor Dennis Clampitt, who lobbied for the brewery in Annapolis, now expects warehouse truck traffic and asks why more elected officials aren't speaking up.

## Relevance
A clean Maryland case for the question César keeps running into: what the public gets back when a company that received special treatment leaves. The credits here were modest, but the statutory and zoning changes were permanent while the company's commitment was not, and nothing in the deal required notice or repayment. It belongs alongside the data center tax break debate, where Abilene's 85 percent abatement and Virginia's capital tax breaks raise the same issue at much larger scale. For any Frederick incentive or development agreement, the questions are whether there are clawbacks tied to operating periods or jobs, notice requirements on closure, and what the zoning permits if the original use goes away.
