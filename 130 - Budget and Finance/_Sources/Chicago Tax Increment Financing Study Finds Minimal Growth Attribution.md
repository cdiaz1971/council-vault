# Chicago Tax Increment Financing Study Finds Minimal Growth Attribution

## Summary

A University of Illinois Chicago Great Cities Institute study of over 40 years of Chicago tax-increment financing found very little of the property-tax growth inside TIF districts was actually attributable to TIF-financed projects, and that other taxing bodies (schools, parks, libraries, counties) are shut out of that captured growth for as long as the district stays active.

## Key Points

TIF works by freezing a district's taxable property value at a baseline, then diverting all future property-tax growth above that baseline into a fund for the district's redevelopment projects rather than to the general tax base. The pitch is that the redevelopment itself causes the growth, so the diversion is self-funding.

UIC's Great Cities Institute reviewed over 40 years of Chicago TIF activity and found that very little of the actual increase in property tax revenue within TIF districts could be attributed to the TIF-financed projects themselves, undercutting that core justification. While a district is active, schools, parks, libraries, community colleges, counties, and other taxing bodies do not share in any of the growth in the tax base generated inside it. The report also found that most of the captured property-tax increases were diverted toward projects downtown and in already more affluent areas, rather than toward the distressed areas TIF is nominally meant to help.

## Relevance

Directly relevant to the Housing Production Target Resolution, which names CDFI/TIF financing as one of its nine priority recommendations. This study is a concrete empirical counterweight worth weighing before leaning on TIF as a financing mechanism: if growth attribution is weak and the tool tends to redirect revenue toward already-affluent areas, that has real implications for whether TIF is the right lever for Frederick's affordable-housing production goals versus other financing tools in the resolution.
