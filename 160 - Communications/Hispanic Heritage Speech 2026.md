# Hispanic Heritage Speech 2026

## Summary
César's speech for the City of Frederick Hispanic Heritage event on 2026-10-06, tracing Hispanic heritage through its Spanish, Indigenous, and African roots. Kept as a reference example of César's speaking voice.

## Speech

I was born and raised in Puerto Rico, an island the Indigenous Taíno called Borikén. The Spanish conquered it and gave it the name we use today. They brought enslaved Africans with them, and in 1898 the island was colonized by the United States.

Spain itself was already a mix before a single ship crossed the Atlantic. For centuries, Muslim and Jewish communities shaped its language, its food, its architecture, and its ideas. From Spain we got our language, much of our faith, and our names. My Hispanic heritage carries all of these influences.

When those Spanish ships did cross the Atlantic, they arrived in places that were already home to millions of people. The Taíno in Puerto Rico, Cuba, and Hispaniola. The Maya, the Aztec, the Inca, and hundreds of other nations. Their knowledge of the land, their foods, and their words are still with us. "Huracán," "hamaca," "barbacoa," and "canoa" are Taíno words. The words chocolate, tomato, and avocado come to us from Nahuatl. The potato came from the Andes and went on to feed the world.

Then there is the African influence. Millions of Africans were brought to the Caribbean and Latin America in chains. What they built, under conditions most of us can't imagine, is woven into everything we call Latino culture today. In Puerto Rico, you hear it in bomba and plena. You taste it in mofongo, which has its roots in West African cooking. You hear it across the region in salsa, bachata, merengue, and reggaeton. Take the African drum out of Latin music and there isn't much left.

That history includes conquest and slavery, and we shouldn't pretend otherwise. But it also produced something remarkable. Spanish, Indigenous, and African roots, braided together over five hundred years into cultures that are distinct from each other and still recognizably family. I can sit at the same table with someone from El Salvador, Costa Rica, or Guatemala, argue about whose food is best, and understand each other perfectly.

That's what we're celebrating this month. Not one origin, but many, and what people made of them together.

Here in Frederick, Latino families are a big part of what keeps this city running. If you've moved into a new home in this city, eaten at a restaurant downtown, had someone you love cared for in a nursing home, or watched a kid graduate from a Frederick County school while a parent who works two jobs cheered in the stands, you've seen what this community gives.

I'm the first Latino to serve on the Frederick City Council, and the first to serve as its president. I'm proud of that, and I don't take it lightly. But a first only means something if there's a second, and a third, and eventually a day when nobody bothers to count anymore.

So to the young people here especially, look at your city government and see a place for yourself in it. Apply for a board or commission. Come to a council meeting and tell us what we're getting wrong. Run for something. This city belongs to us as much as it belongs to anyone.

This month, and every month, celebrate all of where you come from. The Spanish, the Indigenous, and the African. Cook the food, speak the language to your kids, play the music loud enough that your neighbors come over to ask what it is, and then feed them. Being proudly Latino and being proudly from Frederick are not two different things. They're the same thing.

## Voice Notes
This is César's own structure and framing, written after he rejected two Claude drafts. What he changed tells you what his voice is. He anchors the speech in where he is from rather than how he arrived, and he does not use self-deprecating framing about his own path. He prefers history told in plain chronological order, with conquest, slavery, and colonization named directly and without dwelling on them. He rejected folk sayings that did not resonate with him personally, so borrowed sayings should not be used to stand in for his heritage. He left out comparisons to other immigrant groups, keeping the focus on Latino heritage itself. He shifted "belongs to you" to "belongs to us," placing himself inside the community he is addressing. The tone is warm, concrete, and grounded in specifics (words, foods, music) rather than abstractions, with no policy commitments in a ceremonial setting.
