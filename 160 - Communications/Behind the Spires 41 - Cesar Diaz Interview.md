# Behind the Spires 41 - Cesar Diaz Interview

## Summary

Full interview with Council President Cesar Diaz on the city's Behind the Spires podcast, covering his background, path to running, the City Values Act, growth and development, e-bikes and walkability, and his governing philosophy of government reaching toward residents rather than waiting for them. Primary source for his own public framing on each of these topics.

## Background and Path to Running

Born and raised in Puerto Rico, moved to Maryland in 1991, settled in Frederick in 1997 with his then-wife and older son. Has lived in every district of the city over nearly 30 years, watching it roughly double in size. Works in information technology alongside the council role.

Recruited to run through immigration advocacy work with RISE Coalition of Western Maryland, specifically around the 287(g) campaign. When District 2 had no candidate, people in that network approached him. He deflected for a long time -- his framing: "I spent most of my time yelling at elected leaders or politicians. I never really thought about it. Yelling at politicians is kind of a family thing." His son finally pushed him: "Dad, you spend my whole life yelling at the TV. Maybe this is your opportunity to actually do some things." His stepson added: "You always tell me you should try everything." Between the two of them, he decided.

## What He Heard Knocking Doors

Three things came up repeatedly, both in District 2 and at citywide candidate events: housing affordability, traffic and congestion (especially in Hillcrest), and a feeling that development is out of control. He notes he does not personally agree with "out of control" as a characterization, but takes the underlying concern seriously. These three issues are still the dominant themes in constituent email.

## Council President Role

Described the learning curve as steep and unexpected -- "I didn't know what to expect, so I didn't know how to expect it to be different." Credits Phyllis Hane (City Clerk) and Clara Jackson (Legislative Manager) with carrying a significant share of the operational load. Hane still prepares what she calls his "stage directions" for every meeting eight months in. The production team's work booking rooms and managing special events was something he had not anticipated as part of the job.

Constituent contact is primarily by email (cdz@cityoffrederickmd.gov). Volume tracks the agenda -- when a development proposal or ordinance is active, email spikes around it. Encourages residents to sign up for agenda emails through the Public Meetings page so they can track what is coming before the council rather than reacting after.

## Centro Hispano and What It Taught Him

Teaches computer classes to Spanish-speaking residents at Centro Hispano. Notes he is not a trained instructor but credits his mother, a retired teacher, for whatever facility he has at explaining things. The classes surfaced the interlocking nature of barriers he had understood intellectually but now saw directly: the woman who brought her child to class because she had no childcare, the person who missed two sessions because a shift change meant no pay if she did not go. "You don't think how childcare or even transportation affects the ability for someone to take a class who is taking it because they want to be able to solve that first problem -- which is paying for childcare or transportation."

He described computer access as a threshold issue: "It's almost impossible to look for a job these days without a computer. Even something like emailing your kids' teachers or signing them up for little league."

## City Values Act

Framed it as addressing two distinct problems. The first was operational: what are city employees actually supposed to do if immigration enforcement shows up at a city facility? Having it as ordinance rather than policy gives staff something to point to directly -- call supervisor, request a warrant, manage the encounter through a chain of command. The second was about perception in the community: people were afraid to come to city facilities. Not just day-to-day activities like shopping or taking kids to school, but pools, parks, rec centers, and city benefit programs. "I would tell them about benefits the city had available and they were afraid to come apply for them in person." The ordinance is intended to signal that city spaces are safe to use.

On public safety concerns: the CVA explicitly does not restrict general law enforcement or emergency response. It applies only to immigration enforcement and only requires a supervisor notification and warrant process -- it does not prohibit federal enforcement.

The public hearing for the CVA was one of the moments he named as personally significant, alongside being sworn in and being chosen as council president. City Hall was packed. One person raised concerns; the rest of the room was supportive.

## Growth, Development, and the LMC

Frederick has roughly doubled in size during his nearly 30 years there. The council is currently weighing a full Land Management Code overhaul against targeted amendments, examining whether the code has kept pace with development patterns. He mentioned form-based code as an approach worth examining -- it allows neighborhood-level flexibility while enforcing contextual fit. The council has already said no to developer requests that would have changed the character of newer but established neighborhoods: "It just doesn't match what's there, what people expected."

## Traffic, E-Bikes, and What Walkability Actually Means

DPW presented a comprehensive traffic management plan; a long-delayed sidewalk study is also nearing release. E-bikes came up immediately after the swearing-in -- the very first public commenter raised them. He takes the equity dimension seriously: in District 2, an e-bike is sometimes the only affordable transportation option available. But the safety concerns are real, and the question of where bikes are left is also unresolved.

His walkability critique is sharp and worth capturing directly: a developer once described a small strip mall as "walkable" because it had a sidewalk in front of it. His response: "I knew where this was and I'm like, well, you're not walking anywhere." He cited someone who walked from downtown to the Golden Mile to meet him during the campaign and described crossing Route 15 on-ramps with no shade: "Distance-wise, that's not very far. But what does walkability mean? Is it safe? Is it pleasant?"

## Governing Philosophy and Priorities

His central framing: it is not about getting people connected to government, it is about government getting connected to people. "That direction is important. How do we make ourselves more visible so that people feel more comfortable coming to us with issues?" He described talking to residents who had sat on a problem for three, five, six years without reporting it because it never felt comfortable to do so.

Westside Regional Park is a named priority for District 2, both the project itself and ensuring residents have genuine input in shaping it. He also named his historical absence of District 2 from adequate city attention as something to actively counter -- not just doing the work, but making sure people know what is being done.

On his place in Frederick history: "I'm the first Latino member of city council and that's awesome. I'll be proud of that forever. But I'm really going to be excited for the third or the fourth. When more people feel they can see themselves in government, that's great and that's a big goal."

Quick round answers: coffee to start the day; In The Street as the Frederick tradition he never misses; sends visitors to the Golden Mile for food ("We have the best restaurants in the city").
