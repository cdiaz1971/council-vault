## Summary

WebinarTV, a company that bills itself as a search engine for webinars, has been secretly scanning the internet for publicly-accessible Zoom meeting links, recording those calls without participant consent, and converting them to AI-generated podcasts for profit. The company in some cases notified meeting organizers only after the fact -- as a promotional pitch.

## Key Points

The recordings include meetings participants explicitly chose not to record. One documented case involved a Zoom call for educators discussing how to protect students from ICE after the 2024 election -- the organizer deliberately did not record because participants included local elected officials and district leaders who needed political cover. WebinarTV recorded and published it anyway.

The mechanism is straightforward: Zoom links posted publicly (in emails, calendar invites, social media) can be scraped, and anyone with the link can join and record. WebinarTV automated this at scale. There is no indication Zoom's terms of service provide meaningful protection -- public links are public.

This is an instance of the "ask forgiveness rather than permission" pattern that characterizes many AI data-collection businesses. The company is monetizing other people's content and relationships without compensation or consent, then reaching out afterward as a promotional strategy.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick City Council and city government use Zoom for public meetings, community engagement, and internal communications. This story is a direct signal: any Zoom link shared publicly -- even for a "private" community meeting -- should be treated as potentially recordable by third parties. Practical implications: council meetings should be hosted with waiting rooms and registration requirements; community meetings involving sensitive immigration, political, or personnel topics should use platforms with stronger access controls or in-person formats. This also connects to the broader surveillance governance question of what the city can and cannot control when using third-party platforms for official functions.
