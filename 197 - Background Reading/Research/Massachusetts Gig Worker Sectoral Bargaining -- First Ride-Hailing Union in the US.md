# Massachusetts Gig Worker Sectoral Bargaining -- First Ride-Hailing Union in the US

## Summary

Massachusetts became the first state to certify a union for ride-hailing drivers in May 2026, with the state's Department of Labor Relations recognizing the App Driver's Union as the exclusive bargaining unit for nearly 70,000 Uber and Lyft drivers. The model uses sectoral bargaining -- setting standards across an entire industry rather than at individual worksites -- and allows drivers to maintain independent contractor status while collectively bargaining.

## Key Points

The legal pathway was built in stages. Massachusetts voters approved the right to unionize in November 2024 via Ballot Question 3. A 2024 state law then established the mechanics: drivers could begin organizing with 5% peer support (well below the 30% threshold under the National Labor Relations Act), and could trigger the bargaining process with 25% support. Once certified, the bargaining unit negotiates industry-wide standards, not individual employer contracts.

The certification is the largest private-sector bargaining unit since Ford's River Rouge plant joined the UAW in 1941. The Center for American Progress called it a model for the gig economy broadly. Both Uber and Lyft signaled willingness to bargain in good faith, which is a notable departure from their typical posture in labor disputes.

The Trump administration, the NLRB, or the Supreme Court could potentially challenge the framework by reclassifying ride-hailing drivers as employees -- which would subject them to federal labor law and potentially nullify the state model. This is the primary legal risk.

Similar efforts are underway in Illinois and Minnesota. The model could spread quickly if it survives legal challenge.

## Relevance

This is primarily state-level policy with limited direct local authority implications. However, it is worth tracking for two reasons. First, gig workers -- including many in Frederick's immigrant community -- lack the protections that traditional employees receive, and this model demonstrates that state-level intervention is possible without full reclassification. Second, the sectoral bargaining mechanism is a policy design worth understanding: it operates at the industry level, not the firm level, which is how municipal living wage ordinances sometimes work. If Maryland were to pursue a similar framework, it would shape workforce conditions in Frederick.
