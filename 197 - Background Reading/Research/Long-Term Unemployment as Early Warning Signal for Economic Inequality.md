## Summary

The April 2026 jobs report showed headline growth that exceeded expectations, but Demos surfaced a more troubling picture beneath the aggregate number. One in four unemployed workers has been searching for six months or more -- the long-term unemployment threshold that economists treat as a leading indicator of structural economic distress rather than cyclical adjustment. Black workers face disproportionately longer periods of unemployment compared to white workers at every education level.

## Key Points

Long-term unemployment is not the same as unemployment. A person counted as "unemployed" in the headline figure may have lost a job last week. A person in long-term unemployment has been unable to find work for six or more months despite active searching. The distinction matters because long-term unemployment erodes skills, networks, and employer perception in ways that compound over time.

The racial gap in long-term unemployment duration is a structural feature, not an anomaly. It reflects differential access to networks, geography, discrimination in hiring, and the spatial mismatch between where Black workers live and where job growth concentrates.

Demos frames this through its mission of building multiracial democracy and economy -- the implication being that a labor market that produces unequal long-term outcomes by race is not a functioning democracy's economy.

## Why This Matters for Cesar

Frederick is not immune to these dynamics. The ALICE Report for Frederick County documents how large portions of working households cannot cover basic needs even when employed. Long-term unemployment in the regional labor market directly feeds housing instability -- when people can't find work, they can't sustain rent.

This is also relevant framing for conversations about affordability and workforce development at the council level. The standard economic headline ("jobs are growing") can obscure the structural inequality that Frederick's most vulnerable residents are actually experiencing.
