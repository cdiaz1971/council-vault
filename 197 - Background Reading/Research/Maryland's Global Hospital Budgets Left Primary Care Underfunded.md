# Maryland's Global Hospital Budgets Left Primary Care Underfunded

## Summary
Maryland's hospital global budget model controlled costs but never built the primary care capacity it assumed, and the new AHEAD model's 2027 investment targets are the first real lever to fix that.

## Key Points
Since 2014, Maryland hospitals have been paid under global budgets set annually by the Health Services Cost Review Commission rather than fee-for-service, removing the financial incentive to fill beds. On its own terms it worked. The state holds down hospital spending, keeps employer premiums comparatively low, and ranks seventh nationally in the Commonwealth Fund's 2025 health system scorecard.

What the reform never delivered is the primary care system the whole design depends on. Family medicine residency training in Maryland has been flat for a decade, 28 new residents in 2014 versus 27 in 2024, while Virginia and Pennsylvania meaningfully grew their pipelines, including programs targeted at rural and underserved communities. The state's own primary care investment analysis found spending flat or declining for some Medicare populations between 2021 and 2023, with the sharpest disinvestment concentrated in Baltimore City and parts of western and southeastern Maryland.

The structural reason is built into the payment model itself. A global budget rewards a hospital for cutting this year's avoidable admissions. A new residency slot or community clinic takes years to pay off, and the savings it generates leak out to other hospitals and insurers rather than showing up on the investing hospital's own ledger. For a safety-net hospital with little spare capital, that math rarely closes.

On January 1, 2026, Maryland moved from its Total Cost of Care model into the federal AHEAD model, committing for the first time to setting annual primary care investment targets beginning in 2027. The piece argues the targets only matter if they are tied to actual financing weighted toward underserved ZIP codes rather than a statewide average, paired with protected funding for new family medicine residency positions, a defined revenue-sharing mechanism for community health centers and independent practices, and outcome measures that track appointment availability and continuity of care rather than dollars spent.
