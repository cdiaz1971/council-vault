# Cornell Case Shows Voluntary Intoxication Gap in State Consent Laws

## Summary
Nineteen states, including New York, define mental incapacity in sexual assault law as involuntary intoxication only, so a voluntarily intoxicated but conscious victim can fall outside it. The Cornell case has revived a push to close that gap, as Minnesota, Michigan, and Texas already have.

## Key Points
The core problem is definitional. A person can be awake, responsive, and moving while too impaired to understand what is happening or decide anything about sex. Whether the law treats that person as incapable of consent depends on the state and, in many states, on how they became intoxicated.

New York shows how narrow statutory wording decides outcomes. Its penal code recognizes physical helplessness (unconscious or physically unable to communicate unwillingness) and mental incapacitation, but limits the latter to a substance administered without consent. A severely intoxicated person who drank or used drugs voluntarily and stays conscious can fit neither category. NYU law professor Erin Murphy describes such victims as able to fall through the cracks. Prosecutors are then left with forcible compulsion or clearly expressed non-consent provisions.

The Cornell facts: a student alleged she was gang raped by members of the Chi Phi fraternity in October 2024 after drinking at a bar and being pressured into using ketamine for the first time. All seven men deny it. The criminal investigation produced no charges. The Tompkins County DA said the evidence did not support a prosecutable case under New York law, and the New York Times later reported that the condensed police statement sent to his office omitted that she reported being raped. Cornell's own process expelled two men and suspended two, and the chapter was closed in 2024. She filed a civil suit in September 2026 against the men, the fraternity, Cornell, a bar, and her sorority. The contrast between campus sanctions and no criminal charges illustrates the gap between disciplinary standards and statutory elements.

Legislative state of play in New York: two bills broadening incapacity and redefining physical helplessness passed the Senate unanimously this year but stalled in the Assembly. The Assembly has since formed a 15-member working group ahead of the 2027 session, and Governor Hochul has assigned the attorney general to investigate the case. The New York State Association of Criminal Defense Lawyers opposes the changes, arguing there is no loophole and that a voluntary-intoxication standard is too vague to enforce fairly and raises wrongful conviction risk.

The national picture, from a 2022 Journal of the American Academy of Psychiatry and the Law review: in 2021, 21 states limited incapacity to involuntary intoxication, 25 recognized either voluntary or involuntary intoxication, and six handled it through case law. Minnesota amended its law in 2021 after its Supreme Court overturned a conviction involving a voluntarily intoxicated victim. Michigan followed in 2023, and Texas in 2025 added an express provision covering a person known to be too impaired to consent, bringing the involuntary-only count to 19. National comparison is messy because states use different terms (incapacitated, incapable, impaired, unable to resist), and courts read similar words differently. Victim advocates add that enforcement is the bigger problem, varying even across jurisdictions within one state.

## Relevance
This is state criminal law, outside the City's authority, so it is background rather than an agenda item. It is useful as a clean example of a pattern that does show up in council work: a narrow definition in a statute quietly deciding who is protected, independent of what the law's purpose says. Open question worth checking before citing this anywhere: which bucket Maryland falls into. The article does not say, and I have not confirmed Maryland's current statutory definition.
