## Summary

People's Policy Project published a detailed primer on the current US healthcare situation, framed as groundwork for the 2028 policy primary. The piece maps how money flows through the system (households and governments to insurers and employers, then to providers), identifies the major dysfunction nodes, and makes the structural case for single-payer as the mechanism that collapses all of them.

## Key Points

The US spends roughly 17% of GDP on healthcare versus around 11% for peer nations -- the gap is not explained by superior outcomes. Uninsurance remains widespread: roughly 1 in 5 adults goes uninsured over any four-year period, and 1 in 4 non-elderly adults experiences gaps in coverage.

The employer-sponsored insurance (ESI) tax subsidy is deeply regressive. Low-income workers receive a 14.2% tax benefit for ESI while high-income workers receive 41.9% -- the subsidy is largest for people who need it least and is an enormous deadweight transfer to higher earners. Flexible spending accounts (FSAs) cost workers roughly $4 billion per year in forfeitures due to the use-it-or-lose-it structure. Health savings accounts (HSAs) have evolved primarily into backdoor retirement accounts for higher earners, and a company called Truemed has been enabling questionable HSA/FSA expense claims.

Medicare Advantage fraud is extensive -- every major insurer has been targeted by enforcement. Private insurers retain 15-20% of premiums as overhead and profit versus Medicare's 2%. The employer mandate creates perverse scheduling incentives (sub-30-hour cutoffs) that concentrate in low-wage service work.

The author endorses Medicare for Kids as a near-term gradualist step while noting that single-payer collapses all identified failure modes simultaneously.

## Why This Matters for Cesar

This is reference-level policy background for conversations about healthcare costs as an affordability dimension. The ALICE Report for Frederick County documents healthcare as one of the core budget line items that pushes working families into ALICE status. The regressivity of the ESI subsidy is also useful framing for progressive tax policy arguments -- it is a massive upward redistribution that most people do not know exists.
