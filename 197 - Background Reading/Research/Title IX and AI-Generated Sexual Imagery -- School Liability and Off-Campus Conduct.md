# Title IX and AI-Generated Sexual Imagery -- School Liability and Off-Campus Conduct

## Summary

The Radnor deepfakes case crystallizes an unresolved Title IX question: whether schools have legal obligations to respond to AI-generated sexual abuse imagery created off-campus on personal devices, when the perpetrator-victim relationship exists solely because of the school.

## Key Points

Title IX prohibits sex-based discrimination in schools receiving federal funding, and courts have extended it to sexual harassment and abuse that creates a hostile educational environment. The traditional jurisdictional boundary is school-operated devices and school grounds. The Radnor case breaks that boundary: the images were created at home, on a personal device, but the school community is what made the relationship -- and thus the harm -- possible.

The victim's mother named the core argument: "My daughter would not know this other boy if they were not in school together. The entire school knows about it. She's been calling me for weeks to come home early. She can't concentrate, it's affecting her every day at school." The harm is undeniably playing out in the school environment, even if the act originated outside it.

Radnor's administrative response -- offering victims early dismissal and isolated lunch -- addressed the school environment problem by further excluding the harmed students. This inverts the intent of Title IX's hostile environment standard.

Pennsylvania criminalized malicious deepfakes in 2024, giving schools a clear criminal law peg. But whether criminal liability on the perpetrator also triggers school institutional obligations under Title IX is unsettled. The Department of Education has broad regulatory authority to clarify this, and advocates are pressing.

## Relevance

This will be litigated and legislated nationally over the next 2-4 years. For César's work, it's useful background for any state-level legislative conversations about AI harm, and for positioning Frederick as a city that takes AI safety seriously. The parallel to local governance: municipalities face analogous jurisdictional questions when harm originates in a private online context but plays out in public space (harassment campaigns, doxxing, etc.).
