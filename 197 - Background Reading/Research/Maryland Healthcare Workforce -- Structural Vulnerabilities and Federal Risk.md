# Maryland Healthcare Workforce -- Structural Vulnerabilities and Federal Risk

## Summary

Maryland's healthcare sector is the state's largest industry and top job creator, employing 427,000 people and accounting for 8% of GDP and 13% of total wages. A new Comptroller report documents severe structural vulnerabilities: workforce aging and shortages, over-reliance on immigrant labor, and an incoming $2.7 billion federal Medicaid cut under H.R. 1 that threatens roughly 20% of the state's current Medicaid budget.

## Key Points

Healthcare is simultaneously Maryland's economic backbone and a system under significant strain. The sector added 51,000 jobs since 2022 and has led job creation for seven of the past ten years -- but that growth signals rising healthcare consumption, not necessarily a healthy economy. As Comptroller Lierman noted, more healthcare spending crowds out investment in housing, childcare, food, and education.

Workforce vulnerabilities are acute. Registered nurses have the largest gap of any occupation: 9,000 open positions per month against only 1,800 new hires per month. About 40% of nurses plan to retire or leave within five years. Behavioral health and primary care face the sharpest staff shortages.

The immigration dimension is significant. Roughly 30% of Maryland's healthcare workers are immigrants -- a higher share than in neighboring states and the US average. The report explicitly flags this as a vulnerability to federal anti-immigration enforcement. Combined with the state's aging population (65+ is the fastest-growing cohort), the workforce pipeline problem compounds.

Federal funding cuts from H.R. 1 will reduce Maryland's Medicaid budget by approximately $2.7 billion (20%) when fully implemented in FY2027. The work requirement provisions are projected to cost 130,000 Marylanders their coverage.

The preferred mitigation strategy identified in the report is investing in preventive and primary care to reduce downstream costs -- including the Medically Tailored Meals program for food-insecure patients with chronic conditions.

## Relevance

The immigration-healthcare connection is directly relevant to Frederick's immigration policy work. Enforcement actions that suppress immigrant labor availability in healthcare will worsen workforce shortages that are already at crisis levels statewide. The Medicaid funding cut is a budget-level issue that will flow down to municipal human services capacity. This is a useful data point for the budget session and for framing City Values Act arguments around economic self-interest, not just principle.
