# Black Women as the True Economic Indicator

## Summary

Demos makes the case that mainstream jobs report coverage obscures ongoing labor market disparities by focusing on aggregate numbers. Black women face higher unemployment rates, lower wages, and more precarious working conditions even in periods when the headline unemployment rate looks healthy. Using Black women's outcomes as the baseline for evaluating economic health reveals structural inequalities that aggregate data conceals.

## Key Points

The argument is methodological: if an economy is structurally excluding a specific population, aggregate measures will smooth over that exclusion. Black women's labor market outcomes correlate strongly with broader economic dysfunction because they are among the last to benefit from recoveries and the first to suffer in downturns. Their outcomes are not a niche concern but a structural signal.

The parallel from Demos: tax policy changes from H.R. 1's Republican megabill have increased profits for corporations and the ultra-wealthy while households navigate cuts to Medicaid and SNAP. The same structure that produces good headline numbers can simultaneously produce worsening conditions for the most exposed workers. Those workers are disproportionately Black women.

Their equity tax framework proposes five reforms: wealth taxes, expanded credits, and other mechanisms designed to fund public goods for communities historically excluded from wealth-building.

## Relevance

Useful framing for budget season arguments and for countering "the economy is fine" talking points. At the council level, the question of which economic indicators to track when evaluating Frederick's housing, labor, and human services policies matters. Using disaggregated data that surfaces who is actually being left behind is better governance practice than relying on aggregate measures.
