# Howard Street Tunnel Double-Stack Completion -- Decades-Long Infrastructure Investment as Economic Lever

## Summary

The Howard Street Tunnel in Baltimore, constructed between 1891 and 1895, was expanded to allow double-stacked shipping containers on railcars for the first time, completing a project that took decades of political wrangling and $491M in combined investment. The project is projected to add 160,000 containers annually to the Port of Baltimore and redirect 1.2 billion truck miles to rail over 30 years.

## Key Points

The tunnel's brick-and-concrete construction left it roughly 1.5 feet short of the 21-foot vertical clearance needed for double-stacked containers. Closing that gap required not just the tunnel itself but nearly two dozen associated infrastructure projects across the Baltimore corridor. Double-stacking actually began on May 4, 2026; the ribbon-cutting ceremony on June 22 marked the official opening.

The funding breakdown: Maryland contributed $217M (the largest share), CSX contributed $130M, Pennsylvania chipped in $23M, the federal government provided a $125M grant, and the port received $16M for double-stack equipment upgrades. The project's completion comes against a backdrop of already-record port traffic -- 50 million tons of cargo in 2025, the port's second-best year, even while recovering from the Francis Scott Key Bridge collapse in March 2024.

Gov. Moore framed it explicitly as a jobs and economic dignity story, not just a logistics story. Sen. Ferguson connected it to a decades-long political effort that outlasted several near-misses, including a 2016 deal that fell apart and a 2019 attempt that finally held.

The environmental co-benefit is significant: 1.2 billion truck miles avoided over 30 years, saving an estimated 137 million gallons of fuel. Freight moved to rail is a real decarbonization lever, not a symbolic one.

Nearby, Tradepoint Atlantic is beginning dredging work on a new deep-berth container terminal at Sparrows Point, indicating the port's growth trajectory extends beyond this project.

## Relevance

This is a Maryland case study in what sustained infrastructure investment actually looks like -- multi-decade, multi-stakeholder, requiring political persistence across administrations and near-failures. Gov. Moore's framing ("it's about the workers") is a useful rhetorical model for how physical infrastructure investment is narrated as democratic and economic justice, not just engineering.

This connects to the Infrastructure of Democratic Survival Layer 3 framework: physical infrastructure (rail, port capacity, freight decarbonization) as the material precondition for economic participation and community stability. The Howard Street project is not Frederick's project, but the model -- sustained public investment in physical infrastructure as a long-horizon democratic commitment -- is.

For council communications, the contrast between this project's decades-long timeline and the tendency to defer local infrastructure investment (see [[Deferred Road Maintenance Is a Structural Fiscal Problem, Not an Engineering One]]) is worth holding.
