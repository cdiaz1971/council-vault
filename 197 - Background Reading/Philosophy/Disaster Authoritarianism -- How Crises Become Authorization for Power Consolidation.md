# Disaster Authoritarianism -- How Crises Become Authorization for Power Consolidation

Scot Nakagawa distinguishes disaster authoritarianism from Naomi Klein's disaster capitalism. Where disaster capitalism uses crisis to extract economic value, disaster authoritarianism uses the same window to extract political power. In practice the two are inseparable: the emergency suspends normal resistance to radical restructuring, and the authoritarian project uses that suspension to build architecture that is very difficult to roll back once the emergency passes.

## The Three-Phase Sequence

The mechanism follows a recognizable sequence. In the first phase, the crisis hits and coordination legitimately flows toward executive authority. In the second phase, emergency powers get extended, normalized, and repurposed for political control rather than emergency management. In the third phase, the scapegoating that framed the crisis becomes policy: targeted communities face enforcement, legal jeopardy, and withdrawal of democratic protections.

The historical example Nakagawa anchors this to is Pombal's response to the 1755 Lisbon earthquake. The disaster gave Pombal cover to break aristocratic power, expel the Jesuits, and rebuild the city as a monument to centralized state authority. The authoritarian habit of mind installed then -- that emergency justifies surrendering accountability -- echoed forward through Portuguese history to Salazar's Estado Novo nearly two centuries later.

## The Current American Context

The scapegoats in the current US context are already identified before any new crisis arrives: immigrants, trans people, Black communities, Muslims, political dissidents. A major disaster does not change the target. It removes the remaining constraints on how the target is treated. Each crisis is not just an emergency to manage but an opportunity the authoritarian project was already positioned to exploit.

## Why This Matters for César

This framework is foundational for understanding the political moment Frederick sits inside. The City Values Act, the Municipal ID program, and the Frederick Rapid Response Network are all forms of pre-crisis solidarity infrastructure -- exactly what Nakagawa argues democrats need to build before the trigger moment, not during it. The data center debate, immigration enforcement posture, and even budget decisions about emergency management all touch this analysis.
