# Economic Concentration as the Root of Authoritarian Resurgence

## Summary

The post-1989 democratic transitions built electoral institutions while oligarchic economic structures consolidated underneath them. Those economic concentrations are what fueled the authoritarian resurgence of the 2010s and 2020s -- making economic distribution a democracy question, not a separate policy question.

## Key Points

Three reasons economic analysis gets marginalized in pro-democracy work:

1. The dominant Western democracy-promotion framework since the 1990s treats democracy as procedural (elections, courts, rights) and economic structure as a separate question outside the mandate. This was partly justified through anti-communist framing.

2. Much pro-democracy funding comes from sources whose own wealth depends on the economic structures movements would need to challenge. The analytical marginalization is partly a funding-structural phenomenon.

3. Economic analysis is harder -- it requires political economy, labor organizing, and financial system architecture knowledge that is less familiar to a sector built by lawyers and political scientists.

The cost of this blindness: post-1989 transitions produced oligarchic economic structures that political institutions couldn't contain. Those structures are what enabled Orban's Hungary, Putin's Russia, Erdogan's Turkey, and Trump's US.

Principles for addressing concentrated wealth in post-transition contexts:
- Rule-of-law approaches work better than expropriation (transparent asset recovery, anti-corruption prosecution, antimonopoly law, beneficial ownership transparency)
- The goal should be structural rather than punitive -- restructure the economic architecture so the conditions producing oligarchic wealth cannot reproduce
- Economic restructuring must happen simultaneously with political consolidation, not sequenced after it. "Deal with the economy later" means the economy has secured itself against you by the time you get there

## Relevance

The economic blindness pattern applies directly to housing policy. Winning a housing production target resolution is procedural democracy. Whether that production actually produces affordable housing, or whether it produces another round of market-rate units that displace existing residents, depends on the economic structure underneath the policy. The CLT, inclusionary zoning, and MPDU conversations are the economic-structure work that determines whether the democratic win actually serves the people it was meant to serve.
