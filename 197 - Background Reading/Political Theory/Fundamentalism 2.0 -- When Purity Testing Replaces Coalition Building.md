# Fundamentalism 2.0 -- When Purity Testing Replaces Coalition Building

Fundamentalism 1.0 is the familiar pattern: a group, often a majority, attempts to impose specific beliefs -- usually rooted in revelation rather than shared reason -- on the broader society. Fundamentalism 2.0 is something newer: the individual who expects universal agreement with their own framework, treating any deviation as a fundamental error requiring correction.

The key mechanism is algorithmic media. When algorithms reflect our own views back to us consistently, we begin to experience disagreement not as a normal feature of a pluralist society but as evidence that something has gone wrong -- that others are simply not paying attention, or are acting in bad faith. This produces what the piece calls "bean soupification": judging something not meant for you as if it were, and concluding it is therefore wrong and should not exist.

## The organizing implication

Movements weakened by Fundamentalism 2.0 fail not because they were wrong, but because they could not build the coalitions needed to win. Purity testing at scale creates cultural fission -- groups subdivide until each person becomes their own isolated faction. The author's formulation: it is not enough to be right. You have to be right together, and willing to become more right.

The prescription is not abandoning conviction but recovering the capacity to work with people you imperfectly agree with -- and recognizing that this work is much easier in person than through screens.

## Why this matters for César

This is a direct theoretical grounding for the coalition-building challenges Frederick progressives face. The tension between YIMBY and anti-displacement advocates on housing, between immigration advocates and political moderates, between LGBTQ rights groups and potential labor allies -- all of these involve real people doing Fundamentalism 2.0 to each other. The strategic question is not how to find people who already agree, but how to create conditions -- probably in-person -- for trust to develop across partial disagreement.
