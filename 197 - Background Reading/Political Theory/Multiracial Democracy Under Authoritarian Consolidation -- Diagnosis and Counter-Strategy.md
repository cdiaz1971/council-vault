# Multiracial Democracy Under Authoritarian Consolidation -- Diagnosis and Counter-Strategy

## Summary

Two pieces captured in the same session -- Scot Nakagawa's "By Design" and Sabeel Rahman's Demos reflection -- make a paired argument that needs to be read together. Nakagawa names what is being dismantled and why. Rahman names what must be built in response. Neither is sufficient without the other, and the combination produces a strategic frame that goes well beyond either piece individually.

## The Diagnosis (Nakagawa)

Race is not a side effect of the current consolidation agenda. It is the architecture. Every major cut -- to arts funding, HUD, Medicaid, SNAP, civil rights enforcement, the federal workforce, public universities, public-sector unions -- falls with precision on the institutional infrastructure through which multiracial democracy has been built and sustained over the last 60 years.

The federal civil service is the clearest case. It has been, for nearly a century, the primary institution of Black middle-class formation in the United States, beginning with the post office and extending through the New Deal-era expansion. The mass attrition of federal workers is not a neutral efficiency project -- it is the targeted dismantling of that ladder while replacing civil service positions with politically loyal contractors outside the reach of civil rights law.

The regime is doing two things simultaneously. First, producing racial content for its coalition -- the raids, the DEI purges, the deportation theater -- to sustain a politics of grievance. Second, structurally weakening its opposition. The pro-democracy coalition is overwhelmingly multiracial. Attacking the institutions and economic foundations of that coalition is pre-positioning for the next phase of the fight.

**The strategic implication Nakagawa draws:** A movement that frames this primarily as chaos, corruption, or incompetence is missing the architecture. And a restoration to the pre-2024 status quo will not undo the damage -- because the institutional infrastructure being targeted was already weaker than it needed to be.

## The Counter (Rahman)

Sabeel Rahman stepped into leadership at Demos just weeks after Trump's Muslim ban in 2017 and learned the defining lesson of that tenure: transformational change must begin with people power. His charge was twofold -- protect communities under immediate threat and advance the ideas needed to build more inclusive democracy -- and he learned that these cannot be sequenced. You cannot wait until the emergency passes to do the institution-building work, because the emergency is partly caused by the weakness of existing institutions.

Meeting coordinated efforts to consolidate power requires more than resistance. Resistance without an affirmative agenda produces exhaustion and retreat. What is needed is a bold, inclusive vision and the organized constituencies capable of sustaining it when administrations change or courts reverse course.

**The strategic implication Rahman draws:** Political victories are insufficient if they are not rooted in durable organized power. The institutions that can sustain multiracial democracy must be built, not merely defended.

## The Synthesis

The two arguments fit together as diagnosis and prescription. Nakagawa tells you what is being dismantled and why -- with enough structural clarity that you can prioritize where to defend and what to rebuild. Rahman tells you the mode of response -- people power first, affirmative agenda alongside resistance, institution-building as the long game.

The combined frame produces a specific strategic question: which institutions, in this community, are doing the work that Nakagawa identifies as targeted -- providing economic stability, cultural belonging, civic participation, and legal protection to multiracial communities -- and what does it take to strengthen them?

At the local level, that question has concrete answers. The federal workforce ladder is not something a city can rebuild, but a city can:

- Maintain civic infrastructure that immigrant and working-class communities of color can use regardless of federal posture (Municipal ID, City Values Act, rapid response network)
- Protect access to local services that federal policy is making more precarious (housing stability, public health access, schools)
- Build organized constituency relationships with the communities whose institutional infrastructure is under attack -- not just as service recipients but as political actors
- Resist the fiscal coercion mechanism Abbott used in Grand Prairie -- a city with a clear values commitment backed by legal infrastructure is harder to pressure than one that has not prepared

The Rahman frame also warns against the defensive crouch. Frederick's democratic resilience work cannot be purely reactive -- waiting for the next federal action to respond to. It requires building the affirmative relationships and institutional commitments now, while there is still time to do it from a position of some agency.

## Why This Matters for Frederick

The local policy tracks most directly implicated:

**City Values Act.** The Grand Prairie case (Epic Eid) is an instance of the exact mechanism Nakagawa describes applied at state level -- fiscal coercion used to suppress minority community organizing. The City Values Act must be designed to resist this, not just declare values. Cost-recovery provisions and legal support infrastructure are not optional.

**Municipal ID.** The Palantir ELITE note documents that ICE now aggregates 30-40 datasets including HHS data. A Municipal ID that leaks data into that aggregation pipeline fails its purpose. Design must reflect the actual threat model.

**Housing production.** Building housing at scale that allows working-class communities of color to remain in Frederick is not just an affordability intervention -- it is a multiracial democracy intervention. Displacement by market forces accomplishes structurally what aggressive enforcement accomplishes legally.

**Coalition relationships.** Centro Hispano, SSCM, AACF, Frederick Rapid Response Network, and Mobilize Frederick are doing the people-power work Rahman describes. The council's relationship to those organizations should be reciprocal and sustained, not transactional.
