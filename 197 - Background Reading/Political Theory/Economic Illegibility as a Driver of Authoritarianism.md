## Core idea

The shift from an industrial to a financialized, platform-based economy did more than redistribute wealth upward. It made economic exploitation structurally invisible. In the industrial era you could see the factory, picket the plant, organize against a boss. In the platform economy the extraction happens through rent-seeking algorithms, private equity debt-loading, monopoly platform taxes, and data harvesting -- mechanisms that are everywhere and nowhere simultaneously.

Nakagawa calls this the collapse of economic legibility: the inability of ordinary people to understand how the economy actually works, who is extracting value from them, and what they could possibly do about it.

The consequences are political, not just economic. When you know you are being screwed but cannot name the mechanism, you have rage without a clear target. Authoritarian movements fill that vacuum. They do not offer legible analysis -- they offer visible enemies. The strongman says: I can see the real problem. Trust me. I will fight them for you.

Economic illegibility also makes multiple ideological frameworks simultaneously collapse. Market fundamentalism breaks because the free market is rigged by monopolies. Labor organizing breaks because you cannot picket an algorithm. Meritocracy breaks because people did everything right and still fell behind. Small-government conservatism breaks because deregulation is precisely what enabled the extraction. Each framework promised to explain and fix things. None of them can anymore -- which creates the political opening authoritarianism exploits.

## Why this matters for César

This is the structural backdrop for every local policy fight. When residents resist change -- to zoning, to immigration policy, to budget priorities -- some of that resistance is rooted in illegibility-induced anxiety. They sense loss of control without being able to name it. Understanding this does not mean accommodating that anxiety by abandoning policy goals. It means the communication work has to make the mechanisms visible: naming who benefits, who extracts, who actually bears the cost. Frederick's [[City Values Act]] and immigrant community protection work are directly entangled in this -- the "immigrants taking jobs" narrative is a classic illegibility substitution. So is the resentment around housing costs, where the extractive machinery of private equity, LIHTC complexity, and financialized landlords is invisible but the immigrant neighbor or the new apartment building is not.
