# Anti-Authoritarian Playbook -- What If We Lose? Planning for Resistance Under Authoritarian Consolidation

## Summary

Scot Nakagawa argues that strategic realism requires pro-democracy movements to plan not just for preventing authoritarian consolidation, but for how to survive, resist, and eventually win if consolidation succeeds — drawing on lessons from Poland's Solidarity, Chile, and South Africa.

## Key Points

**The normalization problem:** Authoritarian regimes do not require majority support — they only need majority acquiescence. Fear, exhaustion, normalization, and fragmentation convert passive disagreement into acceptance. The strategic task is preventing that conversion.

**What survival requires:** Before strategy comes protection. Legal defense infrastructure, economic support networks for those who lose livelihoods, secure communications, distributed leadership with no single points of failure, and advance planning for who must leave the country and how they stay connected. Organizations need vulnerability assessments now, diversified funding, and membership data protection.

**Maintaining resistance capacity under authoritarian rule:** Open resistance becomes costly. Resistance movements need cell structures, secure communications, counter-surveillance training, and sustainable pacing. Resistance is a marathon — culture, community, mutual aid, and small victories sustain morale across years or decades. Youth integration ensures transmission to the next generation.

**Preventing normalization:** The regime's most powerful weapon is not violence but normalization. Countermeasures include collective memory preservation (oral history, commemoration, strategic outrage at key moments rather than constant outrage), maintaining spaces where dissent is socially normal, and building parallel institutions in education, media, legal services, and governance.

**Trigger moments:** Authoritarian regimes appear stable until they collapse. Economic crises, succession struggles, internal splits, unexpected mobilizations are historical trigger points. Movements must scenario-plan, maintain readiness, and have clear demands and coalition coordination ready to deploy.

**Historical parallels:** Poland's Solidarity movement planned for martial law before it came. When martial law arrived, they survived eight years underground and eventually won. Movements that failed to plan were crushed.

**The call to action:** Begin this planning now, while prevention is still the primary goal.

## Relevance

This piece speaks directly to César's political context as a progressive municipal leader operating during a period of federal democratic erosion. The framework has practical applications at two levels.

At the community level: César's relationships with Centro Hispano, SSCM, and the Frederick Rapid Response Network already reflect elements of this infrastructure — community protection, mutual aid, rapid response documentation. The Nakagawa framing gives strategic vocabulary for why that work matters beyond the immediate crisis.

At the strategic level: The piece provides a framework for thinking about what local elected officials can do to protect democratic institutions and community capacity before the window closes. Frederick's City Values Act work and immigration protection ordinances sit within this longer arc.

The "preventing normalization" section — maintaining spaces where dissent is normal, building counter-narrative culture — is particularly relevant for public communications strategy.
