# Three Pillars of Civil Resistance -- Twin Cities Framework

## Summary

When ICE conducted enforcement operations in Minneapolis and St. Paul in early 2026, what allowed communities to respond rapidly and effectively was not improvised -- it was infrastructure built over years. The authors identify three pillars that made coordinated community protection possible: a human network of volunteers and mutual aid systems, organizational connective tissue built through nonprofits and locally-owned businesses, and robust public space that gives civic life somewhere to happen.

## Key Points

**The human network.** Volunteers organized school watches, corridor patrols, and mutual aid delivery. The speed of mobilization was possible because of prior civic culture: years of organizing, high participation rates, a dense nonprofit ecosystem, and immigrant communities with deep organizing traditions. The George Floyd response in 2020 further strengthened these networks and created relationships that could be reactivated.

**The connective tissue.** Nonprofits and advocacy groups had spent years building trust and operational capacity before the moment of crisis. Small locally-owned businesses provided gathering spaces and coordinated community presence, often before formal political response materialized. A decentralized communications infrastructure -- Signal groups, real-time alert systems, license plate tracking, supply coordination platforms -- allowed information and resources to move quickly across neighborhoods.

**Public space as civic foundation.** Parks, sidewalks, schools, and commercial corridors are not just amenities -- they produce the relationships and informal networks that make rapid civic action possible. When federal enforcement empties public spaces through fear, civic life contracts and community capacity degrades. The Twin Cities had invested in a rich public realm; the authors note this is also where the cities' severe racial disparities are most visible.

The framework closes with an explicit warning: "The time to build these systems is not when the crisis arrives. It is now."

## Relevance

This is the most actionable framework for Frederick's democratic resilience work that has appeared in the vault. The three-pillar model maps directly onto what César is doing: supporting immigrant community organizations (connective tissue), pushing for civic infrastructure investment in parks and public spaces (third pillar), and building the political relationships that activate networks when needed (human network). 

The key policy implication for a local leader: "Supporting community-based organizations is not peripheral to governance, but central to the resilience and success of civil society." That is a council-level argument for sustained funding of Centro Hispano, SSCM, Golden Mile Alliance, Frederick Rapid Response Network, and peer organizations -- not as charity but as resilience infrastructure.

The public space argument also reinforces the case for parks and open space investment in underserved Frederick neighborhoods, which typically have both the worst infrastructure and the highest concentration of immigrant families.
