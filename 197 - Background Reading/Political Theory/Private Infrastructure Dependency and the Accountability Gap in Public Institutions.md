# Private Infrastructure Dependency and the Accountability Gap in Public Institutions

## The Pattern

Three notes processed on May 13, 2026 point at the same underlying dynamic from different angles.

Maryland just passed the Utility RELIEF Act partly because AI data centers are straining the electrical grid in ways that shift costs onto ordinary ratepayers. The state's response was to create a tariff -- a mechanism to make data centers bear some of the infrastructure costs they impose. Before the law, those costs were real but invisible in the rate-setting process: utilities absorbed them, regulators approved rate increases, and households paid without knowing why.

At roughly the same moment, the ShinyHunters group breached Instructure's Canvas LMS, exposing 9,000 institutions to potential student data theft. Universities didn't get hacked -- their vendor did. But students, faculty, and institutions bore the disruption, the reputational risk, and the ongoing phishing exposure. The accountability gap between where the failure occurred (Instructure's infrastructure) and where the consequences landed (public and quasi-public institutions) was total.

The GASB piece adds a third dimension. If the accounting standards that govern municipal finance systematically undercount infrastructure liabilities -- which Strong Towns argues they do -- then the true cost of public dependency on privately-operated or privately-built infrastructure is not visible in the budget documents that elected officials and residents use to make decisions. Cities can appear solvent while accumulating obligations that don't show up until something breaks.

## The Common Structure

In each case the structure is the same:

A public institution (a municipality, a university, a state government, a ratepayer) depends on infrastructure it does not own or operate. The private actor controlling that infrastructure makes decisions optimized for its own interests -- maximizing cloud lock-in, minimizing security investment relative to growth, extracting grid capacity for AI workloads. The costs of those decisions -- rate increases, data exposure, deferred maintenance liabilities -- flow to the public institution. The accounting, regulatory, and legal frameworks that are supposed to make this dependency visible and the costs attributable are inadequate, outdated, or captured.

The result is a persistent accountability gap: private actors capture the upside of serving public institutions, public actors absorb the downside of private actors' failures and cost-shifting.

## Why This Is a Council-Level Issue

Frederick is actively navigating this dynamic in at least two policy tracks.

The data center zoning text amendment is precisely a question of whether Frederick can require private actors -- data center developers -- to bear some of the infrastructure costs they impose on the city's grid, roads, and water systems before those costs become invisible public liabilities. Maryland's new tariff framework is a state-level precedent that supports the local CBA approach.

The City Values Act and municipal technology decisions (any SaaS procurement, any vendor relationship with resident data) implicate the Canvas pattern: when Frederick buys technology, it is creating dependency on private infrastructure. The security posture, data residency practices, and contractual accountability of those vendors are a public interest question, not just a procurement question.

GASB is more abstract but not irrelevant. If the financial reporting standards Frederick uses to communicate fiscal health to residents and to make infrastructure investment decisions don't capture the true cost of private infrastructure dependency, Frederick may be systematically underestimating its obligations.

## What This Suggests

The policy responses emerging at the state level -- tariffs, procurement standards, data center regulations -- are attempts to close the accountability gap by making private actors internalize costs they previously externalized. They are necessary but insufficient: they work after the dependency is already established, and they require sustained political will to enforce.

The upstream intervention is procurement and permitting discipline: requiring cost accountability before a dependency is created, not after. That means community benefit agreements with teeth at the permitting stage, vendor security and data-sovereignty standards in technology procurement, and fiscal impact analysis that uses accounting standards rigorous enough to capture long-term infrastructure liabilities.
