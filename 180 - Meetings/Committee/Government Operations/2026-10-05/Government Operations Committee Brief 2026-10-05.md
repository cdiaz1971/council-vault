## Presentations and Reports

**III.A, Frederick County Police Accountability Board, 2025 report.** The County PAB and its Administrative Charging Committee are briefing on their 2025 annual report. Background they will walk through: the PAB and ACC were created by County law in April 2022 under the Maryland Police Accountability Act of 2021, and FPD came under the framework on July 1, 2024, after its collective bargaining agreement expired June 30, 2024. Two 2025 changes matter. SB 533 (effective October 1, 2025) set hard clocks: 395 days from complaint to ACC decision for public complaints, and one year and a day for internal cases. County Bill 25-08 (August 2025) added PAB subcommittees and ride-alongs, set the ACC to meet monthly or as needed, moved the chair to a two-year term, and specified representation for the City of Frederick, Brunswick, and Thurmont.

The 2025 numbers: 44 misconduct complaints countywide, only 12 of them filed with the PAB (32 went straight to the agencies). The ACC reviewed 48 complaints covering 97 alleged violations and 90 officers, and recommended administrative charges in 6. The PAB's own recommendations are to build out ACC reporting metrics (complaint origin, geographic and demographic trends, remediation tracking), align County and State records retention, track whether outreach changes reporting, and seek a State amendment letting the ACC recommend mandatory training without charging.

The presentation gives no FPD-specific breakdown. That is the main gap to press on: how many of the 44 complaints and 6 charging recommendations involve FPD, how many FPD complaints were filed directly with FPD versus the PAB, and who currently holds the City's seat under Bill 25-08. The fact that nearly three of four complaints bypass the civilian board is worth naming, since a board that mostly sees cases after agencies investigate them has limited visibility. The demographic and geographic reporting the PAB itself recommends is exactly what you'd want for District 2, given immigrant residents' reluctance to file anything with police in the current enforcement climate. No City vote is involved; the City's levers are its PAB seat, budget questions to FPD, and any resolution asking the State for the training amendment.

**III.B, Discussion of City Studies (Nash).** Nash asked committee members to summarize City studies, working from a March 2026 list of more than 60 reports (B.2), and plans to publish the summaries. Her executive summary says more summaries are coming at a future meeting. Tonight's set: Nash did four (Climate Action Plan for Government Operations, Parks and Recreation Master Plan, Sites of Enslavement, Water and Sewer Rate Study), Adkins did the TPMA Housing Gap study, and the CEAP and Tree Canopy summaries are unattributed in the packet. Most of the "legislative action" ideas are requests for staff updates, KPIs for the budget process, and turning recommendations into a tracked work plan for the next Council. That fits Nash's priority-driven budgeting work and is low risk.

Three summaries touch your agenda directly. The **Water and Sewer Rate Study** (NewGen, December 2024) recommends rate increases through FY2030 to cover costs and hit a 3% minimum cash balance; Nash wants a deep dive into operating costs and cost savings. That connects to the [[Water and Sewer Affordability Ordinance]], which proposes a low-income credit funded outside the rate base, so any rate conversation is a natural place to pair increases with relief. The **Parks Master Plan** summary asks for a park-by-park project and CIP update, which [[Parks Plan vs Current CIP]] has already started. The **Sites of Enslavement** summary flags Phase III (markers, walking tour, education, 2026 to 2027) and asks whether funding is adequate, worth supporting.

**Adkins' Housing Gap memo (B.8) is the one to prepare for.** He calls the TPMA projection of 13,774 units by 2035 unreliable because it leans on a ten-year window and historical averages, and he argues the County has under-permitted: County permits (excluding the City) fell from 1,030 in 2022 to 398 in 2025, roughly 3,500 below its historical pace of about 1,500 a year, while the City issued 2,123 of the 2,419 multifamily permits countywide from 2022 to 2025 (87%). His asks are a City and County meeting on permitting, County fiscal support for municipalities absorbing growth (Ready to Serve fee, recordation tax, tax equity, CIP help for housing infrastructure), and a question of whether the LMC rewrite and the LMC audit and text amendments from the housing studies should be merged into one track.

Much of this helps you. Resolution 26-15's production goal is the task force's 4,000 units, not the 13,774 figure, so his critique of the demand projection doesn't touch the goal itself, but it does touch the TPMA report the resolution adopted as part of its evidence base. The safest framing is to agree the projection is a ceiling-type estimate while pointing out that his own permit data shows the City already carries the county's multifamily load, which is the argument for the resolution's call on the County to set comparable goals and for tax equity. His LMC sequencing question is legitimate and cuts toward efficiency; keep in mind the Housing Certainty Act vesting clock (G-26-11) when anyone talks about pausing either track. Note too that Adkins' summary is pasted from an email to Clara Jackson ("Bam - One done"), which will read oddly if Nash publishes it as-is.

**III.C, Mobile Voting Pilot (Mobile Voting Project).** The nonprofit Mobile Voting Project (Bryce Bennett, Michelle Feldman) is pitching Frederick to pilot app-based voting in the 2029 City election, with the group covering implementation costs through grants. The case: about 14,000 people chose the Mayor and Council in 2025, under 25% turnout; app voting helps military and overseas voters, voters with disabilities, domestic violence survivors, and people with tight schedules. The system (VoteSecure, open source) encrypts the ballot on the phone, posts every encrypted ballot to a public hash-chained bulletin board, lets voters verify their ballot on a second device, and prints paper ballots for counting. Cited results: Denver 2019 and Seattle's conservation district (turnout doubled, then tripled), West Virginia, Utah County, Nevada's EASE system, Halifax, and Anchorage and Juneau in 2025. Next steps they want: a City partnership for 2029 and City support for authorizing legislation next session, sponsored by Sen. Lewis Young and Del. Kerr.

Three things to hold onto. First, legal authority: the City can't do this without State law. The Mayor already sent a letter supporting SB 727 (municipal electronic transmission systems), and the deck's request to pass a bill "in the coming session" implies SB 727 did not pass in 2026; confirm. The letter also cites "HB1066" once in a way that looks like a cross-filed bill number slip. Second, administration: the deck says "you run your own elections," but the Roadmap shows you have a 2029 City Election County Coordination request in progress. If the County Board of Elections ends up administering 2029, a City-only app pilot depends on the County's willingness and systems, so the two decisions need to be made together. Third, security and equity: the deck's case studies are mostly narrow pilots (UOCAVA and disabled voters, a conservation district), not a general municipal election, and mainstream election-security bodies (notably the 2018 National Academies "Securing the Vote" report) have recommended against internet ballot return; the materials concede the voter's own phone is the weakest point. On equity, any authentication system must work against the City's separate supplemental registry for noncitizen voters, and any biometric or identity data collected from those voters needs ironclad limits given the current enforcement climate. SB 727's data-use clause and Maryland's Data Privacy Act are the starting points, but ask who holds the identity data, for how long, and whether it ever leaves the City.

A reasonable posture: interested in the access gains, especially for disabled and overseas voters, and open to supporting enabling legislation with strong privacy terms, without committing the City to a 2029 general pilot tonight. A narrower first step (UOCAVA and disabled voters only) would match where the track record actually is.
