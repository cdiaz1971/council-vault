# Public Works Committee Brief -- 2026-05-26

## Summary

Pre-meeting brief for the May 26, 2026 Public Works Committee workshop covering two items: the e-bike ordinance (still unresolved after two prior sessions) and Brehm's rental licensing fee proposal that would cut the fee from $240 to ~$150 and reduce Housing Fund revenue.

## Bottom Line Up Front

Two items. The e-bike ordinance is a carry from prior sessions and still has unresolved definitional and enforcement issues flagged by the Active Mobility Advisory Committee (AMAC). It is administration-driven, introduced under your name, and needs your substantive engagement on the equity and mobility access questions AMAC raised. The rental licensing fee item is a Brehm initiative that proposes cutting the current fee from $240 to ~$150 per license and embedding that reduced structure directly into ordinance language rather than leaving it to the annual fee schedule. That fee cut has direct implications for your Real Rent Ordinance work and the Housing Fund. Both items are workshop discussions only; no votes today.

---

## Item 1: E-Bike Ordinance (Draft G-26-___)

**What it does.** The draft ordinance creates a new Chapter 33, Article II regulating Electrically Powered Micromobility Devices (EPMDs) citywide. Its core operative provisions: e-motos (high-powered electric motorcycles, 3kW-15kW, 30+ mph) are banned from all travel ways; commercial rental of shareable dockless e-bikes and scooters is prohibited within the city with a $400 per-incident fine; EPMDs are banned from downtown commercial and business district sidewalks; Carroll Creek Park is off-limits unless the city posts signs permitting use; and city-owned paths and shared-use pathways are also off-limits by default unless posted as open. Helmet requirements apply to riders 16 and under. Violations carry $100 fines; impoundment authority is included.

**Procedural posture.** This item was tabled at a February 12 special work session, discussed again at the March 26 work session, and is now at Public Works Committee for the third round. The draft is still not finalized. The executive summary explicitly states it is subject to further refinement based on council feedback and legal review. It is introduced under your name on behalf of the administration.

**AMAC letter (May 5, 2026).** The Active Mobility Advisory Committee submitted a detailed critique identifying several problems with the draft as written.

On definitions: AMAC notes that the e-moto definition in the ordinance does not cleanly track state law, leaving a gap where vehicles that don't meet any Maryland statutory category could be over- or under-regulated. They recommend the ordinance explicitly tie e-motos to the "motorized minibike" definition in Transportation Article §11-134.4 as a default classification for anything that fails the tests for e-bikes, scooters, mopeds, motor scooters, or motorcycles. This matters for enforcement clarity.

On harmonization: AMAC flags that §33-6(g) as written could be read to ban EPMDs from 25 mph local roads, which would effectively exclude them from most city streets where analog bicycles are permitted. They want the language revised to track state bicycle law instead. They also oppose the mandatory bell requirement in §33-7(c), arguing it is impractical given handlebar configurations and causes unpredictable pedestrian reactions. And they flag that the passenger prohibition in §33-6(e) would effectively ban cargo bikes and bikes with child seats, a major use case for working families.

On enforcement: AMAC supports the safety objective but urges officer and public education as the primary implementation mechanism.

**Equity flags.** The passenger restriction issue AMAC raised has direct implications for lower-income residents who use cargo bikes and e-bikes with child seats for transportation and child care logistics. The default-closed approach to city-owned paths and Carroll Creek Park means the city will have to actively post signage to open access rather than restrict it, which has resource implications and will affect who can use those facilities in practice. The dockless rental ban removes a potentially affordable on-demand transportation option, though the existing city ban was already in place. The Carroll Creek and path default-closed posture warrants clarification: intentional policy or drafting artifact?

**Questions worth asking.** What is the enforcement resource plan? How many FPD officers have the training to distinguish an EPMD from an e-moto? What is the plan for outreach to the immigrant and working-class communities that use these devices for daily transportation? Is the Carroll Creek default-closed approach intentional?

## Item 2: Rental Licensing Fee Rate (Brehm Initiative)

**What it does.** Council Member Brehm is proposing to revise §12.5-82 to codify a fee formula directly in the ordinance rather than leaving the fee amount entirely to the annual fee schedule. His formula: the Director calculates operating costs annually, then the fee is set at 150% of those costs (operating cost plus 50% markup). Staff estimate operating costs at ~$100 per license on a two-year renewal cycle. Under the Brehm formula, the fee would be ~$150 per license every two years. The current fee is $240 per license.

**The fiscal math.** There are approximately 7,800 licensed rental properties. At $240, the program generated $912k in revenue in FY24 and $971k in FY25, against operating expenditures of $281k and $268k respectively. That produced net revenue of $631k (FY24) and $703k (FY25) above operating costs. Under Brehm's proposal at ~$150 per license biennially ($75/year), annual revenue would be roughly $585k, against ~$100/unit operating cost ($390k/year for the 15% annual inspection floor). The net surplus available for the Housing Fund shrinks substantially.

The current draft's §12.5-84 allocates all revenues to the City Housing Fund to support affordable housing development, rental assistance, and preservation of affordable units. The 50% markup over operating costs is what generates the Housing Fund contribution. Brehm's proposal preserves that structure, but cuts the base from which the surplus is calculated because it ties the formula to operating costs rather than to a policy judgment about what the Housing Fund should receive.

**The tension with your Real Rent work.** The rental licensing fee is the threshold trigger in your Real Rent Ordinance for the Mandatory Fee definition. The fee rate decision here may have downstream implications for how the Real Rent Ordinance is drafted. More substantively: the current program is generating roughly $600k-$700k in net Housing Fund revenue annually. Cutting the fee by $90/license is a meaningful reduction in housing affordability infrastructure funding at exactly the moment the city is trying to build that capacity.

**The draft ordinance (2.B).** Brehm's full draft ordinance revision is a comprehensive rewrite of Article V, not just the fee section. Key provisions include: mandatory registration beginning January 1, 2027; biennial licensing with 15% annual random inspection requirement; a detailed maintenance standards list tied to IPMC codes; revocation process with a 3-inspection escalation before license loss; a 60-day notice to vacate for tenants in revoked units with HHS referral and financial assistance; a public database of owner names, license status, and property classification (with tenant PII excluded); anti-retaliation protections supplementing MD Code §8-208.1; and a March 1 annual report to Council. Short-term rentals (30 days or fewer) are included in the definition of dwelling unit. New construction is exempt for 8 years after certificate of occupancy.

**Equity flags.** The 60-day notice to vacate and HHS referral language is meaningful protection for displaced tenants. The anti-retaliation provision is worth tracking. The 8-year new construction exemption is a significant carve-out: it was likely intended to encourage ADU production but it exempts a wide range of new rental stock from safety inspections for nearly a decade. That could affect habitability in newly constructed affordable units.

**Questions worth asking.** Why is 150% of operating cost the right Housing Fund contribution floor rather than a higher policy-driven multiplier? What was the original basis for the $240 fee and why is that the wrong number rather than the right starting point? How does the exemption for units receiving federal, state, or local funding interact with LIHTC properties post-occupancy? What is the plan for language access in the public outreach program under §12.5-86?

---

## Watch Items

The rental licensing fee discussion is the more consequential item politically. Brehm controls the pace on this because he is the author, but the fee structure decision will shape the Housing Fund's capacity for years. The e-bike ordinance is nominally yours to shepherd (your name is on it), but the substance is administration-driven. The unresolved AMAC issues give you legitimate grounds to slow-walk or sharpen the draft before moving it forward.
