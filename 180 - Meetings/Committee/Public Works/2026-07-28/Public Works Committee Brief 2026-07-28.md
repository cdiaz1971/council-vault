# Public Works Committee Brief 2026-07-28

**Public Works Committee -- July 28, 2026, City Hall**

**Bottom Line**

This is a substantive housing policy session, not a procedural one. Two items with real legislative consequence are on the table: Brehm's Housing Solutions Fund Ordinance gets its first committee workshop, and four outside organizations give formal MPDU reform testimony. Both items are precursors to full-council action. You are not a committee member but you have direct stakes in both -- the Housing Solutions Fund touches your Housing Target Resolution, and the MPDU reform trajectory will shape whatever comes out of the Inclusionary Zoning track in the active threads. No votes are taken today.

---

## Item 1 -- Housing Solutions Fund Ordinance (Workshop)

Brehm is submitting this as a follow-on to the City Affordable Housing Taskforce recommendations. The ordinance does two things: it repeals Section 19-18 and replaces it with a new Chapter 19.5 that establishes a City Housing Solutions Fund to collect MPDU program revenues and any other funds designated by the City Council, and it sets a priority spending hierarchy for those funds.

The priority hierarchy in the draft is worth reading carefully. Administrative costs are capped at 3% of total fund receipts and are listed first; below that come LIHTC financing, rental assistance for lower-income households, home repair and ADA upgrades for low-income homeowners, and housing support for people with disabilities requiring supportive or service-enriched housing. The Director is authorized to establish regulations and administer distributions consistent with those priorities, and a sixth catch-all category allows the Director to fund other programs advancing the same goals.

The annual report requirement is solid. By September 1 each year, the Director must report on revenues by source, projects funded, units created and people assisted, projected incoming funds and pending applications, anticipated operating costs, and legislative or policy recommendations. That reporting structure is well-designed for accountability.

**Risk flags for Item 1:**

The fiscal impact field in the executive summary reads N/A, which is a red flag. The equity impact statement and strategic plan goal fields also show N/A. A fund ordinance with no stated fiscal impact, no equity analysis, and no strategic plan alignment is a problem -- not necessarily a fatal one at the committee stage, but worth noting. The fund cannot do anything useful without knowing how much money is in it. Beyond Shelter's letter establishes the baseline: assuming all known pipeline projects are fully realized on schedule, forecasted MPDU fee revenue over the next five years reaches only $2.5 million -- not enough to meet stated inventory demand. The ordinance's usefulness is entirely contingent on the Council designating additional revenue sources beyond MPDU fees, and the draft gives no guidance on what those might be or at what level.

The "Director discretion" provision in Sec. 19.5-02(b)(6) is broad. Giving the Director unilateral authority to fund programs outside the named priorities without a Council approval step is a governance gap that may draw scrutiny.

## Item 2 -- MPDU Program: Presentations from Housing Community Representatives

Four organizations are presenting. Their positions cluster into two camps.

**Builder/market camp (FCAR and FCBIA):** Both organizations express general support for the fee-in-lieu concept but caution against fee increases that could reduce project feasibility. FCAR's core standard is whether any modification will result in more affordable homes built. They push for incentives alongside regulatory requirements -- density bonuses, parking reductions, streamlined review, publicly owned land for higher-density development. FCBIA notes the city anticipates collecting over $18.2 million in fee-in-lieu from approved projects expected to produce 9,200 units, and argues focus should shift from fee increases to reducing the cost of delivering MPDU units. They also flag that density bonuses are nearly impossible to access given the current regulatory environment.

**Nonprofit/community camp (Beyond Shelter and Habitat):** These two are more analytically grounded and more aligned with your policy direction.

Beyond Shelter's central finding is that the MPDU program is not backlogging any projects -- zero stalled projects were identified at both city and county levels -- which means the problem is not that fees are too high but that developers simply are not motivated to build affordable housing in Frederick as currently defined and governed. This directly undercuts the FCBIA/FCAR feasibility argument.

Habitat recommends retaining the fee-in-lieu option as part of a toolkit while keeping on-site MPDUs as the preferred outcome. They support a graduated fee structure tied to market value or anticipated sales price, so higher-value developments contribute proportionately. They also recommend multi-year Housing Fund investment priorities with annual performance reporting, and they name specific priority funding areas: permanent homeownership, affordable rental production, housing preservation, and home repairs for low-income owners.

Both nonprofit organizations implicitly support the Housing Solutions Fund concept -- their ask is that it be well-capitalized and directed at long-term affordability rather than administrative overhead.

**Risk flags for Item 2:**

The FCBIA letter contains data that warrants verification. FCBIA cites $18.2 million in anticipated fee-in-lieu from 9,200 approved project units, compared to only 1,300 MPDU units from projects not paying the fee (including LIHTC). If that arithmetic is accurate, it strengthens the case for keeping fee-in-lieu but calibrating it more carefully rather than eliminating it. Ask staff to confirm these numbers.

Beyond Shelter's $2.5 million five-year projection is the most alarming data point in the entire packet. If that figure is accurate, the Housing Solutions Fund is being established to hold an amount of money that cannot make a material dent in the stated 13,800-unit need. The ordinance needs a capitalization strategy, not just a spending priority list.

## Watch List

Brehm chairs Public Works and is the author of both the Housing Solutions Fund Ordinance and the MPDU reform agenda. He controls the pace of both threads. His fee-in-lieu math and his fund capitalization assumptions will drive what comes out of committee. Watch whether he accepts or resists the Beyond Shelter framing -- if he accepts that the demand problem is developer motivation rather than fee levels, the reform will look different than if he accepts the FCBIA feasibility argument.

The executive summary for Item 2 lists Hugh Gordon (FCAR), Danielle Adams (FCBIA), Nick Brown (Beyond Shelter), and Eric Anderson (Habitat) as presenters. Public comment after the workshop could bring tenant advocates or additional community voices.

The connection between Item 1 and the Housing Target Resolution is direct. The resolution names the Affordable Housing Commission, city-owned land RFP, and zoning reform as priority recommendations. The Housing Solutions Fund Ordinance is the financing mechanism that would sit underneath all of those. If the fund is undercapitalized from the start, the resolution's production targets are aspirational rather than operational. Consider flagging this connection publicly at the meeting or using it to push for the capitalization question to be answered before the ordinance advances to full council.
