# Council Hearing Brief Non-Gateway Items 2026-08-06

## Bottom Line

The non-Gateway business at this meeting is a mix of a second read vote, a routine fee correction, two introductions, and a lease approval. The Hood College athletic facility lease is the item requiring the most preparation -- it is a 50-year land use commitment on public parkland in District 2, and the fiscal record is incomplete. The Rodent Control Ordinance (G-26-6) is Nash's bill at second read; the version posted for tonight incorporates revisions by Nash addressing the Zack and Rachel redline from July 31, and the two structural defects identified in the prior version have been resolved. The Special Events Commission introduction is co-sponsored under your name alongside Brehm -- two committee-stage issues remain live. The Mixed Use Vertical LMC amendment is introduction only.

## VIII.B.3 -- Hood College Athletic Facility Lease, Westside Regional Park (Vote)

**What the Council is voting on.** Approval of a 50-year Athletic Facility Lease Agreement between the City and Hood College for the construction and shared use of an NCAA-compliant baseball facility on approximately five acres in the undeveloped southern portion of Westside Regional Park, located on Contender Way in District 2.

**District 2 context.** Westside Regional Park is in your district. This is not a citywide parks item that happens to come before the full council -- it is a long-term land use commitment on public parkland your constituents use. You have particular standing to put questions on the record tonight, both as Council President and as the member representing the affected community.

**Deal structure.** Hood finances and constructs the facility at an estimated cost of approximately $3 million. The City leases the land to Hood for $1 per year. The initial term is 50 years beginning when the facility is complete and usable by Hood, with successive 10-year automatic extensions unless either party gives at least two years' notice before the end of the then-current term. Permanent improvements become City property upon installation.

**Scheduling.** Hood anticipates approximately 117 priority-use days annually for baseball and fundraising. The remaining calendar -- roughly 248 days -- is available for City and community programming, subject to maintenance and turf-use requirements.

**City financial exposure.** The City's ongoing obligations include: 60% of agreed artificial turf maintenance, repair, and replacement costs; waiver of City permitting and inspection fees; utilities; and groundskeeping and maintenance outside the facility. No cost estimate for the turf maintenance share is provided anywhere in the executive summary or the lease. Synthetic turf fields of this scale typically cost $500,000 to $800,000 to replace on a cycle of approximately 8 to 10 years. The City's 60% share of that recurring cost is a real, ongoing fiscal obligation falling on the city budget -- and it is undisclosed. Ask staff for a projected cost estimate before or during the meeting.

**Naming rights.** Hood retains the right to pursue naming rights and signage revenue, subject to City review and approval. The revenue goes entirely to Hood. This is public parkland in District 2 being commercially branded for a private institution's financial benefit, with the City having approval authority but no share of the proceeds. Whether the City negotiated any revenue share, and why not, is a reasonable question for the record.

**Term risk.** The City has no general right of early termination. In 2076, if this park use no longer serves community needs, the City's options depend on Hood's cooperation or a breach finding. The 50-year term with rolling 10-year extensions is long for a public park asset. This is not a fatal flaw for a public-private facility deal, but it is worth naming.

**Community scheduling access.** The 248 non-Hood days are theoretically available for community use, but the lease is silent on what mechanism ensures that community programming -- particularly youth leagues and recreational users who may not have the organizational capacity to navigate a city scheduling process -- actually gets booked and is accessible to District 2 residents. Ask staff what the scheduling and access process looks like in practice.

**What to put on the record tonight.** Three questions are worth stating before the vote:

First, ask staff to provide a projected cost estimate for the City's 60% turf maintenance obligation over the initial 50-year term. This number should exist before the Council votes.

Second, ask whether the City negotiated any share of naming rights revenue, and if not, why not.

Third, ask what mechanism ensures that community scheduling for the 248 non-Hood days is actively managed and accessible to District 2 residents, including youth leagues and lower-income users.

None of these questions require opposing the deal. The broad structure is reasonable and Hood is a legitimate community partner. But the fiscal gap, naming revenue question, and scheduling accessibility question deserve answers before a 50-year commitment is made on District 2 parkland.

## VIII.D.1 -- Rodent Control Ordinance G-26-6 (Second Read / Vote)

Nash's bill. The version posted for tonight is Nash's revised draft, not the version that was the subject of the July 31 Kershner and Gray redline. Nash addressed the two structural defects identified in prior analysis -- the obligation language and the right-of-entry provision -- and the revised version is in good shape for a vote.

**What the revised version does.** Inspection authority under Sec. 12.5-92(a) and (b) is clear: the code official may enter any building or premises with consent or a lawfully issued warrant. That is the right framing -- it preserves constitutional limits while establishing affirmative authority. Owner responsibility for rodentproofing and remediation is clearly assigned throughout Sec. 12.5-91 and 12.5-92. The prior version's language that could have been read to assign liability to occupants for conditions they did not create is gone.

The RAT Plan mechanism in Sec. 12.5-95 is the most significant structural feature. It triggers a coordinated city response when a neighborhood-level infestation is confirmed, including integrated pest management, code enforcement sweeps, public communications, and emergency budget amendments if additional resources are needed. The annual public report (due February 1, presented to Council by March 1 under Sec. 12.5-96) creates an accountability loop worth naming publicly as a feature of the bill.

The vacant building compliance provision in Sec. 12.5-92(e) requires yearly inspections and certified pest control remediation for registered vacant buildings -- a meaningful addition that addresses the concern that abandoned properties become vectors for neighborhood infestation.

**One remaining question for the record.** The COMAR 15.05 cross-reference in Sec. 12.5-92(d) directs that remediation be conducted "in accordance with COMAR 15.05," Maryland's pesticide regulation. Confirm with Rachel that she explicitly cleared this cross-reference as not creating preemption exposure, rather than simply passing it through as a citation. If she reviewed the full ordinance and signed off, that is sufficient -- but it is worth knowing whether she addressed it specifically.

**Minor drafting note for Nash.** Sec. 12.5-91 has a redundancy between subsections (b) and (c) -- both say essentially the same thing about maintaining buildings free of rodent infestations and repairing rodentproofing. Worth flagging to Nash for a technical clean-up before final passage. It will not affect enforceability tonight.

**Posture.** Supportive yes vote. Nash leads and defends. If administrative capacity concerns come up in discussion, the RAT Plan's emergency procurement authority and the annual reporting cycle are the right answer.

## VIII.D.4 -- Fee Schedule Ordinance G-26-8 (Introduction / Vote Same Night)

Submitted by Rachel Gray on behalf of the administration. Routine housekeeping. The Council enacted G-26-2 in May establishing the FY2027 master fee schedule, then passed G-26-3 in July on vehicles for hire, which removed specific taxicab fee amounts from the Code and referenced the fee schedule instead. This ordinance updates the fee schedule to reflect the taxicab fee structure discussed when G-26-2 passed.

Fee levels are modest: new taxicab permit $50, renewal $25, taxicab driver's license initial $25, renewal $25. Legally clean, finance-reviewed, follow-through item. Vote yes.

## VIII.D.5 -- Special Events Commission Ordinance G-26-9 (Introduction)

Co-sponsored by you and Brehm. Introduction only -- no vote on August 6.

The commission structure as drafted: up to seven voting members -- five ex officio (COO, Code Enforcement representative, FPD representative, a Frederick County Government representative designated by the County Executive and appointed with Council consent, and a Council member designated by the Council President) plus two public members appointed by the Mayor with Council consent. The commission issues, conditions, and revokes special event permits; recommends noise ordinance variances to Council; and approves alcohol consumption on public property. That last authority was previously held by the full Council under Sec. 6-19 -- this ordinance amends that section to transfer the authority to the commission.

The 90-day decision clock on a complete application is in the text, and inaction is deemed denial rather than deemed approval. The large private event threshold is set at 1,000 persons.

**Note the Gateway link.** Condition 2 of Ordinance G-26-7 (the Gateway rezoning) provides that if the City subsequently establishes an Events Management Commission, that body automatically steps into the COO's role for EMP administration at the Gateway facility. If G-26-9 advances and a commission is constituted, the connection is seamless. Worth naming publicly tonight if the opportunity arises.

**Two issues to track in committee.**

First, the application lead-time is 180 days for any special event. Confirm with staff whether 180 days is workable for the Gateway cricket facility given how tournament and federation scheduling works internationally. If events are booked on shorter cycles, the lead-time will create a problem for the commission immediately after it is constituted.

Second, the review criteria in Sec. 18-12(d) are framed as factors the commission "shall review" rather than mandatory findings the commission "shall make." The distinction matters for due process and for the basis of any future appeal or challenge. Raise this in committee and direct to Gray -- the question is whether the current framing is sufficient or whether findings language should be added before a vote.

## VIII.D.6 -- Mixed Use Vertical LMC Amendment (Introduction)

Submitted by Planning Director Gabrielle Collard. Introduction August 6, vote August 20.

This amends LMC Sections 604, 838, and 1002 to allow residential uses on the first floor of a mixed-use building in MU-1 and MU-2 zones, subject to design standards. Current code bars residential on the first floor of a mixed-use building. The immediate driver is the Brickworks project south of East South Street. Planning Commission voted 4-0 for approval in December 2025.

Before the August 20 vote: confirm from Collard that the design standards are specific enough to prevent the amendment from undermining active ground-floor retail corridors elsewhere in the city. The amendment applies citywide, not just to Brickworks.

Introduction only on August 6. No decisions needed.
