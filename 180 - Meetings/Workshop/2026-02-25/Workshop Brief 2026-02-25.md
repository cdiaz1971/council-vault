# ITEM III.B  
**Discussion of an Ordinance to amend Section 417 of the Land Management Code (MU-1 and MU-2), subsection (d), Phasing**

---

## Decision Required

Whether to proceed with Ordinance G-25-___ amending Section 417(d) of the Land Management Code to replace the current uniform phasing requirements for Mixed Use projects with a project-specific phasing plan proposed by the developer and approved by the Reviewing Authority, including administrative modification authority by the Department.

---

## Current Status / Background

Section 417 governs Mixed Use Districts (MU-1 and MU-2). Subsection (d) currently mandates a uniform phasing structure requiring:

1. No more than 75% of residential building permits before at least 25% of non-residential floor area is constructed; and  
2. No more than 75% of non-residential floor area before record plats for at least 25% of dwelling units are recorded.

The amendment was initiated by the developer of the Renn Quarter project, who asserts that market conditions have delayed non-residential development while residential portions have progressed.

The proposed ordinance would:

- Require the Master Plan for a MU project to include a proposed phasing schedule subject to Reviewing Authority approval; and  
- Allow the Department to authorize modifications to an approved phasing schedule administratively, provided it does not affect adequacy determinations under Chapter 4.

The Planning Commission held hearings on June 6, August 11, and September 8, 2025. On September 8, 2025, the Commission voted 4-0 to recommend denial. The Commission found the proposal inconsistent with the Land Management Code and the 2020 Comprehensive Plan and cited the absence of empirical economic data demonstrating that current regulations are problematic.

---

## What Changes if Approved

If adopted:

- The mandatory 75% / 25% reciprocal construction thresholds would be eliminated.
- Developers would propose individualized phasing schedules at the Master Plan stage.
- The Reviewing Authority would approve phasing plans based on consistency with MU district purpose and Comprehensive Plan.
- The Department could approve subsequent phasing modifications administratively.
- Administrative approval would be permitted so long as it does not alter previously issued Adequate Public Facilities determinations.

---

## Financial Impact

The proposed amendments do not have a fiscal impact on City government.

---

## Legal / Policy Implications

- Amends Appendix A, Land Management Code §417(d).
- Shifts regulatory framework from fixed statutory thresholds to discretionary approval.
- Delegates authority to Planning Department staff to approve phasing modifications administratively.
- Council must consider whether the amendment implements Comprehensive Plan policies and remains consistent with existing regulations.
- Planning Commission concluded it does not implement the 2020 Comprehensive Plan and recommended retaining the current structure.
- Potential implications for public participation, as modifications would not necessarily require Planning Commission hearings.

---

## Stakeholders Affected

- Developers of MU-1 and MU-2 projects.
- Property owners in Mixed Use districts.
- Neighborhood Advisory Councils (MU-2 is a floating zone applicable citywide).
- Existing and future residents of Mixed Use developments.
- Commercial tenants relying on delivery of non-residential components.
- Planning Department staff.
- Planning Commission (role potentially reduced in modification approvals).

---

## Risks / Unintended Consequences

- Residential build-out without timely delivery of promised commercial uses.
- Delay or permanent reduction of non-residential components.
- Reduced leverage to ensure “complete neighborhoods.”
- Perception of weakened regulatory predictability.
- Reduced public oversight if modifications occur administratively.
- Potential precedent affecting all current and future MU projects citywide.

---

## Open Questions / Ambiguities

- Criteria for Reviewing Authority approval of initial phasing plans beyond general Comprehensive Plan consistency.
- Standards governing administrative modification approvals.
- Whether public notice or hearing would accompany phasing modifications.
- Whether safeguards exist to prevent long-term deferral of non-residential uses.
- Whether alternative targeted relief (project-specific amendment) was considered.

---

## Three Clarifying Questions to Ask Staff

1. What objective criteria would guide approval or denial of a proposed phasing schedule under the revised language?  
2. How would the City ensure delivery of non-residential components if market conditions continue to delay them?  
3. Why is an administrative modification pathway preferable to a Planning Commission hearing process, as suggested in the Commission’s letter?

---

## Executive Summary (Neutral)

This ordinance proposes replacing the existing uniform phasing requirements for Mixed Use (MU-1 and MU-2) developments with project-specific phasing schedules proposed by developers and approved by the Reviewing Authority. The amendment would also allow administrative modifications to approved phasing schedules, provided adequacy determinations are unaffected. The proposal originated from the Renn Quarter development, where residential construction has progressed but non-residential components have lagged due to market conditions. The Planning Commission reviewed the amendment across three hearings and voted 4-0 to recommend denial, citing inconsistency with the Land Management Code and Comprehensive Plan and lack of economic justification. The amendment has no fiscal impact on City government. The primary policy consideration is whether to retain a predictable, uniform phasing structure or move toward discretionary, project-specific flexibility. The decision will apply broadly to all current and future MU projects citywide.

**Their Core Business — And Why Renn Quarter Is An Outlier**

This is the most important thing to understand about tonight's discussion. Matan's development pipeline is more than twice the size of its 7 million-square-foot existing portfolio, and they're actively expanding into industrial and warehouse properties across Virginia, with recent full-building leases in Stafford and Ashland. [Commercial Observer](https://commercialobserver.com/2025/11/dsv-matan-nova-industrial-lease/) Their recent wins include a 161,500-square-foot lease with Montgomery County Public Schools at a life sciences and industrial complex in Gaithersburg, alongside tenants like AstraZeneca. The pattern is clear: Matan is very good at industrial, warehouse, and large commercial real estate. Their track record at mixed-use residential with ground-floor retail is a different business, and Renn Quarter is their largest experiment in that product type. That context matters when they claim they can't find retail tenants — finding a Dunkin or a yoga studio for 31,920 sq ft of neighborhood retail on Monocacy Blvd is a fundamentally different task than leasing a 200,000 sq ft industrial building to DSV.

Horton — the City was co-marketing this vision. That creates some shared responsibility for how this plays out.

Matan's own website still describes Renn Quarter as "a new, vibrant neighborhood connected to the heart of downtown Frederick" with "convenient retail shops and amenities" just steps away, and markets "future retail space available at the heart of 1,000+ new residences." [Matan](https://mataninc.com/properties/renn-quarter) They are actively using this language to attract both residents and potential commercial tenants — which makes the "market is too soft" argument harder to sustain.

**The Political History**

When Matan sought the original Renn Farm rezoning in 2015, the project faced resistance from city officials, the Airport Commission, and residents over proximity to Frederick Municipal Airport. [Frederick NewsPost](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/politics_and_government/levels_of_government/municipal/renn-farm-development-faces-potential-setback-tied-to-airport-concerns/article_f3f23452-4d64-5825-8208-66b4dc3459e2.html) The rezoning was contested but eventually approved. Some of the critical public comments from that era were skeptical of Matan's intentions, and some of that skepticism looks prescient now.

**Bottom Line for Tonight**

Matan is a sophisticated, well-resourced company that is thriving in industrial real estate right now. They are not a distressed developer. The argument that market conditions make the Renn Quarter retail infeasible is weakened by the fact that their business overall is doing quite well — they're choosing where to deploy capital, and neighborhood retail in East Frederick apparently isn't a priority. The residents' instinct that this is less about an impossible market and more about where Matan wants to focus its resources is not unreasonable.

The critical question you should press on in tonight's discussion: if the Council grants this amendment or a modified version, what specific mechanism prevents Matan from permitting the last 263 residential units and then simply letting the retail site plans lapse again permanently? Nothing in the proposed ordinance answers that question.
