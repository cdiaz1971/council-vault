# Item C -- Affordable Housing Action Resolution: Meeting Prep

**City Council Work Session, September 10, 2026. Submitted by Council President Díaz. Review and discussion only; not yet introduced.**

## Bottom Line

This is your resolution and your vehicle for claiming the Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force recommendations as council priorities before the Mayor's Office frames implementation as his. It reads well and is legally low-risk, since Section 7 explicitly disclaims any enforceable legal effect. The real work tonight is substantive, not procedural: catch two drafting gaps before this goes back out for a redraft (the MPDU threshold reduction isn't paired with the fee and depth changes that make it a net affordability gain, and there's no explicit tenant-protection or anti-displacement companion beyond a general Section 5 statement), and get a read from the room, especially Adkins and Brehm, before you lock language for actual introduction.

## Where This Stands in the Process

Per the Legislative Process note, a work session workshop takes no votes; it's full-council discussion and feedback only. The Executive Summary form (C.1) confirms this directly: "The Council President will review the Council's initial feedback and work with City staff to refine the proposed language before presenting an updated draft for consideration at a future City Council public meeting." So tonight produces feedback, not a First Read. The Notion Legislative Roadmap has this tracked as "Resolution on Affordable Housing Action in the City of Frederick," Status: Drafting, Priority: High, Track: Legislation, Legal Review Cleared: No. Next Action on file: "Individual council member outreach to build support, then prep for committee agenda placement." Whatever you hear tonight should feed that outreach.

Per your standing instructions, actual redraft language is chat work, not this note's job. This brief flags what needs to change; the drafting happens with you afterward.

## Section-by-Section Read of the Draft (C.2)

**Background recitals.** Establish the crisis numbers: nearly one in three households cost-burdened, roughly half of renters cost-burdened and over a quarter severely so, a 3.1% rental vacancy rate against a 5.1% statewide rate, and home prices up more than 60% in the first two years after January 2020. These track the TPMA Report figures already codified in the passed Housing Production Target Resolution, so there's no new factual exposure here, just restatement.

**Section 1, Acknowledgment of Task Force Findings.** Formally endorses "Opening Doors in Frederick" (July 2026) and the TPMA Report as "the foundational evidence base for housing policy in the City of Frederick." This is the clause that does the political work of claiming the report, but endorsing findings is not the same as endorsing every recommendation, and the resolution is careful to only name specific recommendations as "high-priority" in Section 4, not adopt the report wholesale.

**Section 2, Commitment to Action.** Directs City departments to treat the Task Force recommendations "as an actionable policy work plan." This is aspirational language directed at the Administration, not a council commitment to specific legislation, and it's consistent with the resolution's non-binding character established in Section 7.

**Section 3, Production Goals.** States the 1,377 units/year target and adopts "the Task Force's estimate of 4,000 additional units over ten years as a minimum production floor contingent upon full implementation of the Task Force recommendations." Compare this against the Housing Production Target Resolution you already passed (May 2026), which set a 1,400 units/year benchmark and a 15% affordability target (33% sub-target at 60% AMI). The two documents use slightly different annual figures (1,377 vs. 1,400) drawn from the same TPMA data at different points of refinement; that's not a real inconsistency but is worth being able to explain if someone asks why the numbers don't match exactly. More importantly, this resolution doesn't restate or reference the 15%/33% affordability percentage targets from the passed resolution at all. If you want this resolution to reinforce rather than sit beside your existing production framework, that's a linkage to consider adding explicitly.

**Section 4, High-Priority Recommendations (A through I).** The nine items: an Affordable Housing Commission, a cross-departmental implementation team and Navigator position, APFO reform, MPDU modernization (25-to-10 unit threshold), an 8-10 month expedited review pathway, financing tools (TIF, CDFI, PILOT, fee waivers), city land disposition reform, missing-middle/ADU/faith-based zoning reforms, and an annual production dashboard. Two of these already have their own life on your Notion roadmap independent of this resolution: "RFPs for City Land" is in Committee Review, and "Inclusionary Zoning Reform" is Pre-Legislative. Worth noting tonight that adopting this resolution doesn't start those items from zero, it puts council weight behind work already moving.

The MPDU item (D) is the one that needs the fix flagged above. [[MAHTF Final Report Policy Brief -- 2026-07-16]] is explicit: "If the threshold drops to 10 without accompanying increases in affordability depth or in-lieu fee rates, you could end up with more projects subject to MPDU requirements but producing weaker affordability outcomes per project. Move threshold reduction and fee increase together." [[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]] independently arrives at the same conclusion and specifies the numbers: raise the in-lieu fee from $2.00 to $12.00 per square foot, add a tiered threshold (full MPDU at 15+ units, in-lieu only at 7-14, exempt below 7) rather than a flat drop to 10, and hold the 12.5% baseline as a floor. As drafted, Section 4(D) names only the threshold change. That's the gap.

**Section 5, Anti-Displacement.** A general commitment to "pursue parallel strategies to preserve naturally occurring affordable housing, explore targeted rent stabilization tools, maintain and extend affordability covenants, and prevent displacement." This is the resolution's only tenant-protection language, and it's aspirational and unspecific, no named ordinance, no timeline, no reference to the Real Rent Ordinance or the rental licensing rewrite already in your own pipeline. The MAHTF brief's flag applies here too: "The report is supply-side in its dominant framing... You need to be on record that production and tenant protection are not substitutes." Section 5 gestures at this but doesn't actually connect to the specific tenant-protection legislation you're already running. Naming the Real Rent Ordinance and the rental licensing rewrite explicitly here would close that gap without adding legal risk, since it's descriptive of existing efforts, not a new commitment.

**Section 6, Annual Reporting.** Directs HHS to publish an annual Housing Production Report starting FY2028, presented at a public meeting. Straightforward accountability mechanism, tracks the Task Force's own recommendation, and creates a natural yearly checkpoint tied to this resolution specifically, separate from the Annual Housing Report already required by the passed Housing Production Target Resolution beginning FY2027. Two annual reports with overlapping content and different start years and different sponsoring departments (HHS here, Mayor's office under the earlier resolution) is a coordination question worth asking staff about tonight, not something to leave for someone to notice after both are law.

**Section 7, Foundation for Future Action.** The disclaimer clause: "This resolution does not create enforceable legal obligations or supersede existing law." This is why legal review risk is low regardless of what changes in redraft, the resolution is a policy statement, not a mandate.

**Section 8, Advocacy and Governmental Partnerships.** Commits to transmitting the resolution to the state and federal delegations, the Governor, the County Executive, and the County Council. Standard, low-risk, and useful for the state and county "asks" the Sept 8 brief separately catalogs (recordation tax reduction, MARC Brunswick Line service frequency as a housing lever).

## Political Dynamics in the Room Tonight

Per [[MAHTF Final Report Policy Brief -- 2026-07-16]]'s assessment: Nash, Hempel Irani, Shackelford, and Taylor are all likely favorable to the core recommendations. Adkins and Brehm are the named question marks, for different reasons.

**Adkins** is supply-side but not progressive on housing: he supports production and better developer contribution structures, but there's no documented record of him supporting affordability mandates beyond current MPDU levels. His 35-year planning background makes him a potentially constructive voice on the APFO reform and zoning pieces if engaged directly, per the same brief, but he's also capable of using process expertise to slow items he's not sold on. Nothing here should read as opposition to this specific resolution, since it's non-binding, but the MPDU and APFO recommendations inside it are exactly where he'd have substantive, not just procedural, questions.

**Brehm** sits in the progressive-moderate lane and has three stated priorities, affordable housing, managed development, and sustainability, that align with this resolution's premise. He's also the author of the rental licensing fee reduction proposal (cutting the biennial fee from $240 to roughly $150), which is in direct tension with the Housing Fund capitalization argument this resolution's Section 4 financing recommendations rest on. The MAHTF brief calls this out explicitly as unresolved. If it comes up tonight, the honest answer is that both positions can't hold at once, and that's worth surfacing rather than letting it sit as a quiet contradiction between two council members' priorities.

## Recommended Posture for Tonight

Welcome the discussion as the first public step in making this the council's agenda, not a ratification of the Mayor's report, consistent with the "Immediate" recommendation in the MAHTF brief: state plainly which recommendations the council is taking up and that you intend to move faster than a typical mayoral implementation timeline. Ask for the room's reaction to the Section 4 list as a whole before getting into specifics. If MPDU comes up, be ready to say the threshold number in the draft is incomplete on its own and that the redraft will pair it with the fee and depth changes. If displacement or tenant protection comes up, connect Section 5 explicitly to the Real Rent Ordinance and rental licensing work already underway rather than leaving it as a freestanding aspiration. Do not resolve the Brehm rental-licensing-fee tension tonight; naming it as a known open question is enough for a workshop.

## What Happens After Tonight

Per the Executive Summary's own Next Steps and the Notion roadmap's Next Action: take tonight's feedback, work with staff on a revised draft (the MPDU pairing and the Section 5/tenant-protection linkage are the two changes flagged here), and do individual council member outreach before this comes back for introduction and committee placement. The actual redraft language should be worked through with you directly in chat rather than produced in this note.
