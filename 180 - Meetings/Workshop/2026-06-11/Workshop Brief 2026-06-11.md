# Data Center Worksession Brief -- June 11, 2026

## Summary

Executive briefing and question strategy for the June 11 data center worksession, synthesizing presentations from Turner (Loudoun County), Lapp (OPC), and Griffin (DED), with questions designed to build a record against approving the Trammell Crowe ZTA.

---

## Bottom Line Up Front

This worksession is a policy orientation, not a vote. The council is being asked to absorb three distinct perspectives on data centers -- an economic development case from staff, a consumer protection warning from Maryland's Office of People's Counsel, and a cautionary case study from Loudoun County. No action is required at this meeting. The real decision point comes when the Trammell Crowe ZTA moves from Planning Commission to council, which is still months away.

César's strategic goal is to use this worksession to build a record that supports declining the ZTA when it arrives. There is substantial community opposition. The political calculus is clear. The task now is to ask questions that surface the weaknesses in the pro-data-center case, create moments of uncertainty in the record, and signal to the community that the council is taking their concerns seriously without tipping his hand prematurely.

Two of the three presenters are effectively making the case against approval. Turner is describing a cautionary tale from the highest-density data center jurisdiction in the world. Lapp is documenting direct ratepayer harm. Griffin is the only presenter making the affirmative case, and his fiscal projections contain exploitable weaknesses.

The most effective posture is not opposition -- it is rigorous skepticism. Ask questions that require honest answers. Let the presenters build the record.

---

## The Three Presenters and What They Say

### Mike Turner -- Loudoun County Vice Chair, Ashburn District

Turner built the most data-center-dense jurisdiction on earth and is now managing the consequences. Loudoun permitted 250-plus data centers over 16 years, generated $1.3 billion in annual data center tax revenue, and then in March 2025 ended all by-right development because the power grid is structurally oversubscribed. Power demand is now 5.4 gigawatts and growing toward a projected 14-plus gigawatts by 2030. The planned transmission infrastructure will not keep up.

His 15 best practices are the most operationally useful material in the packet. Key ones:

Never allow by-right development. Require council approval every time.

Never sign a non-disclosure agreement with a developer.

Require pre- and post-construction noise studies with binding limits on both audible noise (dBA) and low-frequency tonal noise (dBC). The dBC component has the worst health effects and is typically ignored.

Require substations to be on-site or adjacent and included in the application. Never allow separate accessory use approval.

Minimum 500-foot setback from residential. 1,000 feet preferred.

Require written utility confirmation of exactly how much power will be delivered and by what route before any approval.

Turner's structural point: communities that allowed unconstrained grid-dependent development are now hostage to transmission decisions made by utilities and PJM, not by local elected officials.

### David Lapp -- Maryland Office of People's Counsel

Lapp's testimony is the most consequential document in the packet and the one most likely to be underweighted at the meeting. His message is direct: data centers are the primary driver of electricity demand growth in the PJM grid, and that growth is already raising residential utility bills. Maryland residential customers are paying $10 to $21 more per month in capacity costs. The most recent capacity auction alone raised prices across PJM by more than $6.5 billion.

The state's Utility Relief Act created some protections -- a large load tariff that shifts more costs to data centers, a registry requirement, and a clean capacity program. But these are partial measures. The PSC now has authority to direct costs back to specific data centers, but that requires action at the state level, not by the city.

The residents who pay higher electric bills are the same working families and ALICE households the council represents. This is the equity argument against data centers and it is grounded in OPC's own data.

### Richard Griffin -- Director of Economic Development

Staff's position is clear: permit data centers with conditions, modeled on the county's CDI Overlay Zone. The fiscal case rests on tax revenue potential and industry diversification. The key comparison is data centers vs. warehousing and logistics -- the actual choice on the Trammell Crowe site.

The fiscal case has three significant weaknesses:

Neither the city nor the county currently charges a business personal property tax. In Loudoun, that tax accounts for two-thirds of data center tax revenue. Without it, the city's take is limited to real property taxes alone.

Under current law, data centers would likely qualify for the city's New Jobs Tax Credit, reducing real property tax liability for six years. This is buried in the memo.

The economic impact analysis was commissioned and paid for by Trammell Crowe, the developer and ZTA applicant. It was prepared by Sage Policy Group in June 2026. It is not independent analysis.

The memo also notes that the State of Maryland study required by SB 116 is due September 1, 2026, and recommends the city follow the county's CDI Overlay model, which took roughly two years to develop. Both of those facts support an argument for delay.

## Questions by Presenter

### Mike Turner

"Supervisor Turner, you ended by-right data center development in Loudoun in March 2025 after 16 years of permitting. If you could go back to 2008 when Loudoun began actively recruiting data centers, what is the one condition you would have required from the start that you did not?"

"You mentioned that even halting new construction will slow but not solve Loudoun's power problem. Does that mean the grid impacts from data centers already approved are essentially locked in regardless of what a jurisdiction does going forward?"

"You recommend a 500-foot minimum setback from residential, with 1,000 feet preferred. In your experience, have any data center applicants voluntarily proposed those setbacks, or does it only happen when the jurisdiction requires it?"

"What has community opposition looked like in Loudoun, and at what point did it become a political factor the Board could not ignore?"

### David Lapp

"Mr. Lapp, your presentation shows Maryland residential customers are already paying $10 to $21 more per month in capacity costs driven largely by data center demand growth. If Frederick permits data centers, should Frederick residents expect their utility bills to increase further?"

"The Utility Relief Act created a large load tariff designed to shift costs back to data centers. In your assessment, does that tariff fully insulate existing residential ratepayers from cost increases, or does it only address incremental future costs?"

"The state study required by SB 116 is due to the Governor and General Assembly by September 1, 2026. Should a local jurisdiction wait for that report before making any land use decisions about data centers?"

"Is there any mechanism by which a local government -- as opposed to the PSC -- can require a data center to bear the full cost of transmission and capacity upgrades its demand requires?"

### Richard Griffin

"Director Griffin, your memo notes that data centers would likely qualify for the city's New Jobs Tax Credit, reducing real property tax liability for six years. Can you tell the council what the projected annual real property tax yield would be in years one through six after that credit is applied?"

"The fiscal projections in the Trammell Crowe economic impact analysis were prepared by Sage Policy Group, commissioned and paid for by the developer. Has the city commissioned any independent fiscal analysis?"

"Neither the city nor the county currently has authority to levy a business personal property tax on data centers. How much of the favorable fiscal comparison between data centers and warehousing depends on the city eventually obtaining that authority?"

"The county's CDI Overlay Zone is described as a model for the city to follow. The county took about two years from its workgroup convening to final ordinance adoption. You're also recommending the city wait for the state study due in September. Is it your recommendation that the city take no action on this ZTA until both of those processes are complete?"

---

## Watch Items

Adkins and Shackelford are likely receptive to the economic development framing. Adkins has a planning background and will be drawn to the industry-diversification argument. The business personal property tax angle is the most effective way to complicate that framing without appearing anti-development.

The last question to Griffin is the highest-value exchange at this meeting. If he says yes to delay, staff is on record recommending the council wait. If he hedges, press on why the city would move faster than the state's own study timeline.

The ZTA is still at Planning Commission. Nothing at this worksession creates any obligation. The value of this meeting is in shaping the standards and the record before the developer's framing takes hold at the council level.
