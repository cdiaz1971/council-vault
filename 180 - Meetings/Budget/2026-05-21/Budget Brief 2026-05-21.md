# FY2027 Budget -- Property Tax Revenue Hearing Brief (2026-05-21)

## Bottom Line

This presentation is an opening for structural tax reform conversation, not just a revenue update. Barkdoll has surfaced alternative rate structures without advocating for any of them. Tonight's goal is to establish a public record of Council interest in two specific directions: a residential/non-residential split rate and restoration of the business personal property tax base. Neither change is on the table for FY2027. Both are worth anchoring in the record for FY2028 deliberations.

## What the Presentation Shows

The net assessable base has grown from roughly $7.3B in FY2017 to a projected $13.7B in FY2027, nearly doubling over a decade. The growth rate peaked around FY2023-2024 and is now decelerating, projected at roughly 8% for FY2027 after a high of around 11-12%. Real property tax revenue has tracked the base closely, with actual collections consistently meeting or modestly exceeding budget. The current uniform rate is $0.7055 per $100 of assessed value.

The presentation identifies the split-rate option as legally available under state law, with a table showing five scenarios ranging from $0.5055 residential / $1.2384 non-residential to $0.6855 residential / $0.7599 non-residential. All scenarios are presented as revenue-neutral relative to the current rate. The tiered-by-value model is flagged as problematic because the State cannot calculate the constant yield tax rate or homestead credit under that structure, though the presentation does not say the obstacle is insurmountable.

On business personal property, the presentation confirms the 2015 phase-out has cut the taxable base nearly in half, from $158.5M in FY2018 to a projected $85.5M in FY2027. Budgeted revenue from this source has fallen from $2.46M to $1.32M over that period. The rate remains at $1.55 per $100 but is being applied to a shrinking base.

## Equity Frame

The current uniform rate is regressive in effect for lower-value residential properties relative to high-value commercial holdings. The split-rate option directly addresses this by allowing the city to reduce the burden on residential property owners while increasing it on commercial. For renters, the benefit is indirect -- lower residential assessments may reduce cost pressure on small landlords -- but the apartment class ($803M assessable) is coded as its own property type, so any residential rate reduction would not automatically apply to apartment buildings without a specific policy choice. That is a gap worth tracking.

The business personal property phase-out was a business-friendly policy decision made in 2015. Reversing or halting it would restore a revenue stream that the commercial sector has been relieved of, which could support either rate relief elsewhere or direct investment in services used by lower-income residents.

## Watch Items

The contradictory tax rate deadline -- state law requires June 20, City Code says July 1, and the County needs the rate by end of May -- is a real administrative problem. If the County sets assessment data and the city cannot formally act until after the County's deadline, the city is operationally dependent on County cooperation. This is not a tonight issue but worth flagging for future charter or code review.

## Questions for Tonight

**Q1.** The presentation shows the city has authority to set different rates for residential versus non-residential property classes. If the Council directed staff to model a revenue-neutral scenario that shifts more of the burden onto commercial property and reduces it on residential, what would that analysis require and how long would it take to prepare?

**Q2.** The tiered rate approach by assessed value was flagged as problematic because the State cannot calculate the constant yield or homestead tax credit under that structure. Are those obstacles specific to the tiered-by-value model, or would they also apply to a straightforward residential/non-residential split rate?

**Q3.** The 2015 resolution phasing out business personal property tax for most businesses has reduced that revenue base by nearly half over a decade. Does the Council have authority to halt or reverse that phase-out, and has staff modeled what revenue restoration would look like?

## Strategic Notes

Tonight is record-setting, not action-forcing. The ask is staff modeling, not a rate change. Questions 1 and 3 both request concrete deliverables -- a modeling timeline and a restoration analysis -- which gives the Council something to follow up on in FY2028 budget prep. Question 2 is designed to surface whether the tiered-by-value approach has a viable path, or whether the split-rate is the only structurally feasible equity tool available.

The apartment class tax treatment remains an open question. Raising rates on apartments passes cost to renters. But leaving them lumped with owner-occupied residential in any future rate reduction means landlords capture the benefit. Staff should eventually be asked to model apartment class treatment separately.
