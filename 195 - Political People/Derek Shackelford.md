## Political Profile

Shackelford is the most senior Black elected official in the City of Frederick and has built his political identity around racial equity, economic justice, and progressive municipal governance. He consistently frames policy debates through the lens of structural racism and the Black church tradition, invoking civil rights language in public addresses and council testimony. He is ideologically progressive within the Frederick Democratic mainstream, though not an insurgent -- he operates through institutional channels and has cultivated a collaborative, minister-statesman public persona.

His power base is built on a combination of the Black community, progressive Democrats, and long-term community relationships from his decades in youth athletics coaching and ministry. He has little visible labor union support and no documented developer donor network. His political brand emphasizes community presence and longevity over ideological sharpness.

On the council, Shackelford has been a reliable vote on equity and housing measures but has also shown a willingness to protect his own institutional positioning. The December 2025 council president race exposed a harder edge: he publicly campaigned against colleague Katie Nash for the president seat, coordinated with outgoing Council Member Kelly Russell to raise conflict-of-interest concerns about Nash's lobbying work, and used the controversy to position himself as the candidate of integrity. He lost that bid when the new council elected César Díaz as president instead -- meaning he has now been passed over twice for the top council role (in 2021, when Nash took it on vote-count tradition, and in 2025, when the new council chose Díaz).

In public communication, he is measured, sermon-inflected, and frequently invokes historical memory. He tends to speak in moral register rather than policy technocracy. He self-identifies strongly as a Frederick native and uses that to establish authority and belonging.

## Vulnerabilities and Controversies

### Council President Race 2021 and 2025 -- Institutionalism vs. Stated Integrity Narrative -- MEDIUM value
The most revealing window into Shackelford's political character is the two-part council president conflict spanning 2021 and 2025. In 2021, when Katie Nash won the most votes citywide and took the president pro tempore role by tradition, Shackelford was reportedly among those who did not want Nash in that role. Council Member Kelly Russell sent a memo at the time suggesting Shackelford take the role as the only Black member, framing it as a diversity argument. Outgoing Council Member Donna Kuzemchak later alleged Shackelford and Russell were maneuvering against Nash personally, not on principle.

In 2025, Shackelford publicly sought the presidency again. Russell wrote a public letter backing him and raising Nash's lobbying conflicts. Shackelford denied any coordination was strategic, saying: "I have never and will never compromise my character or integrity for a position. It's not worth it." Kuzemchak directly accused him of trying to "manipulate the system so that a person they do not like does not have a position of power." He ultimately lost the presidency to newly elected Council Member César Díaz, marking the second consecutive time he was passed over.

The opposition research value: his narrative of principled, collaborative leadership is undercut by documented evidence of factional maneuvering for institutional power, combined with a denial he cannot fully substantiate. A written record of the 2021 internal communications reportedly exists and has been referenced by Russell herself.

### Term Limits Opposition -- LOW-MEDIUM value
He voted against term limits in the 2024 charter changes -- a position that can be used to contrast his stated commitment to community-driven governance with a desire to preserve personal incumbency advantage. The term limit he voted against is now the same one that caps his own tenure after 2029.

### City of Hope Nonprofit Transparency -- MEDIUM value (pending investigation)
As CEO of a nonprofit he founded, with no public 990 filings traceable under the organization's apparent name, there is an open question about financial activity, any city contracts or grants received, and whether his dual role as councilmember and nonprofit CEO presents a conflict. Not a confirmed controversy -- a gap that warrants investigation before it can be assessed.
