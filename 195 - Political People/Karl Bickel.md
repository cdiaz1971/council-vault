## Political Profile

Bickel is a progressive Democrat rooted in the community-policing tradition, ideologically aligned with the reform wing of law enforcement rather than partisan electoral organizing. His candidacy is built on professional credentialing and institutional critique rather than grassroots mobilization. He positions himself as a policy expert running against what he frames as the ideological drift and management failures of incumbent Sheriff Chuck Jenkins -- specifically Jenkins's embrace of 287(g) immigration enforcement, ties to far-right constitutional sheriff groups, and what Bickel characterizes as a nepotistic management culture.

His public voice is analytical and data-heavy. He has written multiple op-eds for Maryland Matters on 287(g), police accountability boards, and federal immigration overreach. He writes and speaks like a former federal policy analyst -- measured, citation-forward, and credentialed -- not like a street cop. This is both his strength and his liability in a sheriff's race, where voters often weight operational experience and community familiarity heavily.

Bickel has been running for this seat since 2010, losing three consecutive times to Jenkins. His 2022 loss was by roughly 14 points (57%-43%), his closest race to date. His fourth run in 2026 comes in a changed environment: Jenkins was federally indicted on machine gun charges in 2023 (charges dropped November 2024), and Governor Moore signed a bill banning 287(g) agreements statewide in February 2026 -- Bickel's signature issue for four cycles. He faces two Democratic primary opponents in 2026: Tony Lawson (Frederick City Police officer, former FCSO deputy) and Jason Cabel Ciemielewski. The primary is June 23, 2026; general election November 3, 2026.

Bickel served on the public safety committee of County Executive Jessica Fitzwater's transition team, signaling alignment with county Democratic leadership.

## Vulnerabilities and Controversies

### 1976 Fatal Shooting -- Washington, D.C. -- MEDIUM value

In May 1976, while serving as an MPD officer, Bickel shot and killed a suspect during a building search on K Street in Washington, D.C. He and a partner responded to a woman's report that someone was in the building; the two split up, convinced there was no danger. Bickel encountered a suspect who attempted to take his service weapon, and Bickel fired, killing the man. He publicly disclosed this incident for what he described as the first time in June 2015 at a Thurmont police department event on in-custody death policy. He disclosed that the suspect was on parole on two counts of homicide at the time and reflected openly on the emotional toll. No charges were filed; the shooting appears to have been cleared as justified use of force.

**Opposition research value:** MEDIUM. The incident is over 50 years old, was legally cleared, and Bickel chose to disclose it himself in a supportive policy context. He framed it as evidence of the psychological toll of use-of-force -- which is politically defensible. An opponent could attempt to revive it, but there is no evidence of cover-up, disciplinary action, or unresolved questions. Primary value is as a background item to monitor for opponent use, not a standalone line of attack.

### Fired from FCSO in 1994 -- LOW value

When Jim Hagy won the 1994 sheriff's race, he dismissed Bickel two days before taking office, stating he needed a second in command he personally trusted. Jenkins has used this repeatedly in debates, framing it as evidence that Bickel was originally a crony appointment and then discarded. Bickel's supporters counter that this is routine political turnover and that Hagy's stated reason was loyalty, not performance.

**Opposition research value:** LOW. A well-worn attack that Jenkins has deployed in every cycle. Bickel's response is established and credible. The firing was political, not disciplinary. Not a fresh vulnerability.

### No current law enforcement experience -- MEDIUM value

Bickel has been entirely outside operational law enforcement since 1994 -- more than three decades in federal policy work. Jenkins and others have repeatedly characterized him as a "government bureaucrat" and "sideline critic." Bickel's rebuttal is that national-level expertise in community policing training and technical assistance is more relevant to leading a modern sheriff's office than patrol experience.

**Opposition research value:** MEDIUM. This attack has traction in a sheriff's race because the position is fundamentally operational and managerial. The 2026 field compounds this: Democratic primary opponent Tony Lawson is a current law enforcement officer, giving primary voters an alternative if they weight operational experience. This is the sharpest clean line of attack against Bickel from within the Democratic primary.

### Four-time loser narrative -- MEDIUM value

Bickel has now run four times without winning: 2010 (acknowledged "never got off the ground"), 2014 (lost 63%-37%), 2018 (lost ~52%-48%), 2022 (lost 57%-43%). The pattern is persistence without victory in a county Jenkins has dominated.

**Opposition research value:** MEDIUM. The counterargument is that the political environment has genuinely shifted -- Jenkins was federally indicted, 287(g) is now banned statewide, and Bickel's issue platform has been validated by the legislature and governor. Still, "why him this time" is an open question that primary opponents can exploit.
