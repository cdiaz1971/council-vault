I’m looking forward to our discussion this evening. I wanted to share some background material that could help inform (y)our work (based on Casey Anderson’s article I shared: [https://ggwash.org/view/101548/what-are-planning-hearings-actually-for](https://ggwash.org/view/101548/what-are-planning-hearings-actually-for) ) to improve the process by which public input is received during public hearings, and expand that conversation to consider other systems in which public input is touted to be important, but in my opinion has limited impact, and could be improved. 

  

Public input is garnered through public comments given at hearings and committee meetings, collected through ecomments and emails submitted to city staff, and through public surveys about specific topics. Less frequently, public input is gathered through small area plan design charrettes, and through meetings for specific spaces and projects, like those that are being for the West End library, West End park, and West End Community center. The former ways have limited effectiveness, while I believe that latter ones are more effective. 

  

Limits of each system: 

Committee meetings: held during the day and not in accessible places for all community members. The meeting agendas are not clearly found on the City website site. These meetings, when the topic comes from the planning commission, require sustained engagement from the public to reach this point. Likely the topic has been reviewed multiple times by different people and the public is required to attend each time for maximum consideration. This is very difficult for most citizens. 

  

Public hearings: this is the final step in the process and if the public attends at this point, it’s usually too late to make changes - only a denial is possible at this point. It is very expensive for an applicant to get this far and then have to go back and redo the process. If it’s a by-right element of the project and not a request for a modification, potential lawsuits could come if the City Council denial isn’t related to a narrow limits of what the LMC can regulate. 

  

Ecomments and emails: May not be given much weight or consideration. May be related to aspects of the project that the Council cannot control. Public may not be present or listening to the online broadcast to hear the applicant, staff, commissioners’, or council members’ comments. 

  

Public surveys on selected topics: surveys are generated in-house. They aren’t created by professionals - aren’t delivered in a valid way to measure what they intend to measure I.e. aren’t valid ‘needs assessments’, they have to be short by design, survey results aren’t systematically evaluated or shared with the public, and there is no implementation process or evaluation process in place that would measure or quantify if feedback is incorporated into policy.

  

I think the article I shared expressed the limits and roles of public input well. The public aren’t subject matter experts. They aren’t policy makers or lawyers or urban planners. They are the end users. They understand their community and individual needs and know what’s working and what needs improvement based on their ‘user’ experience. They are the clients and their feedback can’t fully inform policy decisions. But they will be able to tell you if the policies are functioning as intended. 

  

Consequently, the public input process at the committee and public hearing level, is at the place in policy where public input is the least effective. This problem, in its worst case scenario, creates frustration, anger, skepticism of the process, and ultimately citizen disengagement and public apathy and antipathy towards government and the systems that guide it.

He also makes a great point by noting that 

“Future residents cannot attend hearings to speak for homes that don’t yet exist. Regional economic vitality, climate stability, and fiscal sustainability decades usually have weak constituencies if they have any organized public support at all.”

  

“This creates an asymmetry: those who benefit from blocking change (existing homeowners) can organize opposition, while those who would benefit from change (future residents, transit riders, workers seeking housing near jobs) are invisible. The rule of law — applying adopted standards consistently regardless of political pressure — is what enables the political system to act on behalf of the whole public.”

  

This candid and clear statement on the limitations of the public hearing setting for new developments could be used to support the use of form-based land use codes (FBCs). As part of the FBC process, an initial charette, which produces a community vision document, informs the code design.  It allows early public input that makes sure their community values are expressed and considered throughout the design and drafting process. Since public input and community values are part of the process in writing the land use code, there should be less disagreement that the overall rule of law aligns with the best interests of the “whole public,” and future public hearings on individual properties can be limited to the types of inquiries Mr. Anderson outlined in his public dialogue solution (which reads to me as a cross-examination, and can be hard for the public to stick to, in my experience, but at least the roles are clearly defined and disregard for some public comments can be justified.)”
