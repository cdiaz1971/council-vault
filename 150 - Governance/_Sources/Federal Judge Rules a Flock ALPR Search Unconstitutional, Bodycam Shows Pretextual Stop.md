# Federal Judge Rules a Flock ALPR Search Unconstitutional, Bodycam Shows Pretextual Stop

## Summary
Body camera footage obtained by 404 Media shows an Oklahoma deputy searching Flock and Motorola ALPR records on a driver before pulling her over, then using her multi-state travel history to manufacture probable cause; a federal judge ruled the warrantless ALPR search unconstitutional and suppressed all resulting evidence, including 91 pounds of meth found in the search.

## Key Points
- U.S. District Court Judge Sara Hill ruled that Tulsa County Deputy Sheriff Freddie Alaniz's search of Flock and Motorola ALPR systems on driver Melissa Kyle was an "unconstitutional warrantless search," "indiscriminate mass surveillance," and a violation of her Fourth Amendment rights -- even though the resulting search of her vehicle turned up roughly 91 pounds of methamphetamine, which the court ordered suppressed as "fruit of the poisonous tree."
- Bodycam footage, obtained by a 404 Media reader who purchased it in person from the Tulsa County courthouse clerk (the court did not post it online despite its use as evidence), shows Alaniz searching Flock and Motorola's Vigilant system on Kyle's rental car before fully pulling her over, triggered only by noticing California plates.
- Alaniz's in-car laptop showed 79 total ALPR hits on the car over 30 days, about 50 during Kyle's few-day trip, compiled from cameras owned by multiple states, counties, and even a private Lowe's hardware store -- illustrating how ALPR networks aggregate location data across jurisdictions and private entities into a single searchable travel history.
- The stop was nominally for a lane-change signal violation; Alaniz told Kyle she'd only get a warning, then used the ALPR travel-history printout to interrogate her about trip details and flag minor discrepancies as a basis for "reasonable suspicion" to search the vehicle.
- Judge Hill's ruling rejected the government's reasoning explicitly on normalcy grounds, writing that interstate travel, losing track of the day, and nervousness around police are ordinary behaviors for "everyday Americans," not indicators of criminality.
- Flock has reportedly limited nationwide data-sharing from California ALPR cameras specifically after past misuse for immigration-enforcement and abortion-related searches -- meaning the same Tulsa deputy could not pull California-side hits, only the Arizona border-crossing record.
- The case is one of a growing set of court rulings and local contract cancellations (also covered elsewhere in this folder) treating ALPR historical-search capability, not just individual camera hits, as a Fourth Amendment search requiring a warrant.
