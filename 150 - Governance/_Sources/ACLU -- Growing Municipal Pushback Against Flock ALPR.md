# ACLU -- Growing Municipal Pushback Against Flock ALPR

## Summary

Jay Stanley, ACLU Senior Policy Analyst, documents an accelerating national movement against Flock ALPR contracts at the municipal level. The driving force is the recognition -- now grounded in documented cases rather than hypothetical concern -- that local plate reader data flows directly into ICE's immigration enforcement operations, and that no policy commitment made by a city or county can reliably prevent it.

## Key Points

Stanley's central argument is that the boundaries between local surveillance infrastructure and federal enforcement are porous and practically unenforceable. When a city deploys Flock cameras, the data does not stay local. Flock's architecture connects subscribing agencies into a nationwide network, allowing any officer with access to search any other jurisdiction's plate history without a warrant or stated reason. ICE has exploited this by routing searches through friendly local agencies rather than contracting with Flock directly -- the same indirect access pattern documented through Florida fish and wildlife officers and other intermediaries. Because the access is indirect, cities have no clean line to draw and no reliable mechanism to enforce the promises they make to residents.

Stanley quotes Flock CEO Garret Langley's 2022 response to Vice News on whether Flock could be used for immigration enforcement: he said yes, if legal in a state, Flock would not be in a position to stop it -- and that they give customers the tools to decide. Stanley's point is that this framing is false. If your data is shared across hundreds of agencies nationwide, you do not actually have the tools to decide anything. Any of those agencies can share it with ICE, anti-abortion prosecutors, or any other federal or state actor with an enforcement interest.

The Texas case sharpens this. A police officer used Flock's nationwide system to search for a woman who had self-administered an abortion -- illegal under Texas law. This is not an immigration story. It illustrates that the threat model extends to reproductive rights, political activity, protest attendance, and any other behavior that a hostile state government might decide to criminalize or surveil.

Stanley documents a series of cities where Flock has faced organized opposition since mid-2025:

Austin ended its Flock contract outright. Council Member Mike Siegel's framing was direct: the city should not be participating in Trump's mass surveillance programs. Denver's city council rejected a Flock contract proposal unanimously, with the mayor acknowledging that conditions had changed substantially since the pilot began. Evanston revoked out-of-state access to its ALPR database after public records revealed at least seven ICE searches had been conducted against the city's data, in apparent violation of an Illinois state law banning data sharing with ICE. Richmond cut off data sharing with ATF after learning that ATF had been making immigration-related searches without notifying the city.

In jurisdictions that have not terminated contracts, Stanley describes growing interrogation by elected officials. Gig Harbor, Washington saw a police chief grilled over the math of network sharing -- one councilmember pointed out that Puyallup alone connected to 593 other networks, meaning any data shared with Puyallup was effectively shared with hundreds of agencies. Syracuse councilors demanded to know how local plate data ended up searched 4.4 million times by police around the country, with some of those searches going to ICE despite stated policy prohibitions.

Stanley notes a parallel track at the state level: legislation imposing new limits on ALPR surveillance is advancing in multiple states, and a Virginia lawsuit is arguing that plate readers of sufficient density constitute a Fourth Amendment violation by creating routine tracking of people not suspected of any crime.

### The DeFlock Thread

One detail worth holding: a man named Will Freeman runs a project called "DeFlock" that maps the physical locations of LPR cameras across the country. Flock sent him a cease-and-desist. The Electronic Frontier Foundation took his case and shut down the threat. This is the same pattern of vendor intimidation used against researchers and journalists, and the EFF's involvement is a resource to note for anyone building a public accountability strategy around surveillance technology.

## Relevance

This piece is the strongest single-source argument for why any policy commitment Frederick makes around Flock -- whether through ordinance, contract language, or internal policy -- is structurally insufficient without addressing the network sharing architecture itself. The cities that have made the most meaningful change have ended contracts or revoked external network access entirely, not added policy riders.

The Austin and Denver comparables are directly usable in a council context. Both are peer cities, both acted in 2025, and both grounded their decisions in the same ICE data-sharing evidence now available about Flock's architecture. The Evanston case is particularly instructive for Maryland: Evanston had a state law banning ICE data sharing and Flock still exposed the city's data to seven ICE searches. State-level protection is not sufficient if the network architecture bypasses it.

For the [[City Values Act]] specifically: Frederick cannot credibly commit to immigrant protection while maintaining a Flock contract that participates in the nationwide plate reader network. The indirect access problem -- ICE using friendly agencies as pass-throughs -- means a contract that says nothing about immigration enforcement is not actually neutral. It is participation.
