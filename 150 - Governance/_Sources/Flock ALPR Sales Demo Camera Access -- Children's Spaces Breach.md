# Flock ALPR Sales Demo Camera Access -- Children's Spaces Breach

Flock Safety, the ALPR and surveillance platform used by many municipal police departments, confirmed that sales employees accessed live cameras in Dunwoody, Georgia as part of sales demonstrations to other law enforcement agencies. The cameras accessed included a children's gymnastics room, a school, a playground, a Jewish community center, and a pool.

The breach was discovered by Dunwoody resident Jason Hunyar, who obtained Flock's access logs through a public records request. His blog post, "Why Are Flock Employees Watching Our Children?", catalyzed community outrage.

Flock disputed some of the characterizations but confirmed the access occurred.

## Why this matters for César

Frederick has active discussions around ALPR deployment. This incident is a concrete example of how vendor access to municipal surveillance infrastructure can extend well beyond what residents or elected officials expect or authorized. The Flock model -- city contracts with vendor-managed infrastructure -- means the vendor retains access that the city may not control or audit.

Key questions for any ALPR governance framework:
- What access does the vendor retain after deployment?
- Are there audit logs, and who can see them?
- What notification is required when vendor employees access city camera feeds?
- Are there categories of locations (schools, parks, community centers) that warrant additional protection?

This is directly relevant to the governance framework work underway at [[150 - Governance]] and the broader [[Data Center Community Benefit Agreements -- Policy Models]] thread on vendor accountability in municipal tech contracts.
