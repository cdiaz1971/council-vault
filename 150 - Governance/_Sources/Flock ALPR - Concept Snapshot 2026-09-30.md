# Flock ALPR

Flock Safety sells a nationally networked automated license plate reader system to local law enforcement. What began as plate-and-timestamp capture has expanded, feature by feature, into a broader surveillance platform. Across the source notes behind this page, three patterns recur: capability expands quietly and past what the system was originally sold as, accountability happens only when a journalist or resident forces it, and the vendor treats a council's decision as something to manage rather than something to obey.

## How the system works, and how it grew past plates

Cameras log plate, vehicle make and color, and location and time, searchable in a cloud database any subscribing agency can query without a warrant. The network has reached roughly 6,000 communities and 5,000-plus agencies, and Flock's own figure, cited in every sales pitch, is that its cameras contribute to solving about 10% of reported crime nationally, some 700,000 cases a year ([[Flock ALPR Cameras in 6000 US Communities Centralized Database Without Court Approval]]).

Three expansions moved the system well past that pitch. ALPR units began capturing Bluetooth device identifiers, so a vehicle can be tracked by the phone inside it even when the plate itself isn't read ([[Flock ALPR Tracks Bluetooth Devices -- Police Stalking Cases and Surveillance Creep]]). Flock's FreeForm feature, launched in 2025 for vehicle descriptions, now lets an officer search hundreds of cameras at once using a plain-language description of a person, "heavy-set male, black and white hat," and public-records data shows officers using it for race and political-affiliation descriptors despite Flock's stated guardrails against exactly that ([[Flock FreeForm Search Tracks People by Description Not Just Plates]], [[Flock FreeForm Search Used to Track People by Race Clothing and Political Affiliation]]). Anne Arundel County, Maryland ran a FreeForm search for "white jeep with trump flag" across 198 camera networks, a Maryland example, not a hypothetical one.

## Federal and ICE access

Flock has no direct contract with ICE, but access happens through agencies that do have Flock contracts and run searches on ICE's behalf. Florida's Fish and Wildlife Conservation Commission has done this ([[Flock ALPR -- ICE Access via State and Wildlife Agencies]]). CBP has direct access through a pilot program Flock initially denied and later confirmed, along with the Secret Service and the Navy's criminal investigation division ([[Flock Safety Whistleblower Jonathan Paz Reveals ICE Deception and Council Manipulation]]).

State-level protection does not close this pipeline. Evanston, Illinois had a state law banning data sharing with ICE, and ICE searched its data seven times anyway. Syracuse's council learned its plate data had been searched 4.4 million times nationally, some by ICE, despite a stated local policy against it ([[ACLU -- Growing Municipal Pushback Against Flock ALPR]]). For Frederick, this means the Sheriff's 287(g) termination under SB 245/HB 444 does not by itself sever this pipeline; the wildlife-agency case shows ICE access can route through any agency holding a Flock contract, not only police departments.

## Individual misuse

This is a separate, well-documented problem from institutional access. Officers in at least nine states have used Flock to surveil partners, exes, or people they had taken an interest in, including a Costa Mesa officer convicted alongside child pornography charges and a Brevard County, Florida deputy who did it the same week he was named Deputy of the Year ([[Flock Safety ALPR Misuse for Personal Stalking]], [[Brevard County Deputy Used Flock ALPR to Stalk Ex-Girlfriend]]). A separate case involving a different ALPR vendor's system shows the same pattern: an officer placed a hot-list alert on a woman he had met once, after illegally pulling her records, and the department's own detective told her on camera this kind of misuse recurs ([[Cop Used License Plate Reader Database to Stalk Woman]]). Flock's own count of confirmed misuse cases has moved from an initial claim of about 15 to a public acknowledgment of "a lot," with independent reporting from the Washington Post and the Institute for Justice now counting at least 50. The company's stated safeguard is audit logs, which detect misuse after the fact rather than preventing it, and only help if someone actually reviews them.

## Corporate conduct

CEO Garrett Langley told Ohio officials that reporting on a documented abortion-tracking case was "entirely false," despite court and police records confirming it, addressed to the exact officials who decide on contract renewal ([[Flock CEO Lied to Ohio Officials About Abortion Tracking Case]]). Former Flock government affairs manager Jonathan Paz, an elected city councilor before joining the company, has said on the record that his job was to keep community opposition from turning into a council vote against the technology, and that leadership told him and other employees for years that Flock had no relationship with ICE while building the access described above ([[Flock Safety Whistleblower Jonathan Paz Reveals ICE Deception and Council Manipulation]]). Flock sales staff separately accessed live camera feeds in Dunwoody, Georgia, including a children's gymnastics room, a school, a playground, a Jewish community center, and a pool, to demonstrate the product to other departments, discovered only after a resident filed a public records request ([[Flock ALPR Sales Demo Camera Access -- Children's Spaces Breach]]). Fusion centers in at least six states have monitored anti-Flock social media accounts and the DeFlock mapping project's organizing activity, and their bulletins blur lawful advocacy together with a smaller pattern of camera vandalism ([[Fusion Center Surveillance of Anti-Flock Activism]]).

## Entering a city, and resisting an exit

State public-safety grants are the usual entry point, lowering apparent cost and creating friction later if a city wants out; Bandera, Texas faced a $15,000 cost to leave. Flock sends former elected officials to make its case in communities, the same legitimacy strategy Paz described from inside, and trains police officers directly on how to argue for the technology at council meetings ([[Flock Safety -- Corporate Playbook for Selling Surveillance to Local Governments]]). Bandera, population 900 with eight cameras, shows organized opposition can still win a vote: after months of resistance including camera vandalism, its council voted 3-2 to cancel ([[Bandera Texas Cancels Flock Contract After Community Resistance]]).

Winning the vote is not the same as getting the cameras removed. In Verona, Wisconsin, the council voted to end its contract in November 2025; Flock objected to the city removing its own equipment, said it was unsure whether remote disable was even possible, and routed the case as "maintenance" rather than removal, until the city covered the cameras in trash bags in January while still waiting, with removal finally happening in February ([[Flock Camera Removal in Verona Wisconsin Municipal Pushback and Offboarding Barriers]]). In Troy, New York, after its council voted to end its contract, the mayor declared a state of emergency to keep the cameras running. A synthesis note pairing the Verona case with a similar pattern at the New Orleans Police Department (quietly testing weaponized-drone policy language, then retreating once noticed) frames both as the same underlying behavior: institutions treating a democratic decision as an obstacle to manage rather than an outcome to accept ([[Surveillance Vendor Resistance to Democratic Exit Flock Verona and NOPD as Pattern]]).

## National trend

Over 140 cities have rejected Flock outright according to DeFlock's tracking, and more than 30 have canceled existing contracts since early 2025 according to NPR reporting. Austin ended its contract outright, Denver's council rejected a proposed one unanimously, and Tempe turned off its cameras despite a live $80,000 contract while Cave Creek ended its own the same week ([[Tempe and Cave Creek Cancel Flock ALPR Contracts Amid Nationwide Pushback]]). Axon, a competing vendor, is now redesigning its own ALPR camera specifically so it looks less like a Flock camera and draws less vandalism, evidence that the backlash is having a market effect, though Axon's system raises the same warrantless-access concerns Flock's does ([[Axon ALPR Redesign Amid Flock Backlash]]).

## Frederick status

**confidence: high.** César confirmed on 2026-09-27 that Frederick does not have a Flock contract. The March 2026 note (Frederick and Frederick County actively evaluating or deploying Flock cameras) and the April 2026 note (Flock operates in Frederick) are superseded by that confirmation, and the July 2026 note (Frederick does not currently contract with Flock and is being asked to consider one) matches it. Everything in the framework section below is therefore prospective, written for a contract Frederick could be asked to sign, not one it holds.

## What a Frederick governance framework would need to cover

Based on documented failures above, not hypotheticals:

1. Close the non-renewal-versus-cancellation ambiguity Flock used in Verona; require a short, mandatory removal window regardless of how a contract ends.
2. Require remote-disable capability tested and confirmed in writing before signature, not taken as a vendor claim.
3. Prohibit Bluetooth tracking and FreeForm person-search data capture and retention explicitly; neither was part of the original plate-reader pitch.
4. Require active, independently reviewed audit log review, not just the existence of logs.
5. Require public disclosure before any vendor use of city camera feeds outside the stated law enforcement purpose.
6. Map which Frederick County agencies beyond FPD and FCSO might hold a Flock contract, since ICE access can route through any of them.
