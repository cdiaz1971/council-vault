# Flock Safety -- Corporate Playbook for Selling Surveillance to Local Governments

## Summary

Flock Safety uses a systematic playbook to penetrate local government markets -- state grant subsidies, former elected officials as lobbyists, and police training on how to argue for surveillance at city council meetings -- making it a template to understand for any municipality considering tech vendor relationships.

## Key Points

The Flock Safety model has several distinct components that together constitute a replicable vendor strategy:

**State grant subsidies as the entry point.** Flock cameras are frequently installed using state public safety grants, which lowers the apparent cost to cities and reduces the political friction of adoption. When communities later want to exit the contract, they face the argument that they're "giving up free money" (in Bandera's case, plus $15,000 in local exit costs). The grant structure creates lock-in.

**Former elected officials as public affairs representatives.** Flock sent a former Cleveland city council member to a town hall in Bandera, Texas -- a town of 900 people. Deploying people with local government credibility to argue for the technology at community meetings is a deliberate legitimacy strategy.

**Police training on council argumentation.** Flock is running training sessions for police officers specifically on "how to speak with city councils: meeting the moment with confidence" -- coaching the arguments that law enforcement should make to local elected officials. This is a vendor actively shaping the political case for its own product.

**Surveillance use in sales demos without disclosure.** In Dunwoody, Georgia, residents discovered Flock was using live camera feeds from their city in product demonstrations to other potential clients -- without community knowledge or consent.

The pattern across cities (Bandera TX, Troy NY, Dunwoody GA) shows that vendor persistence outlasts community opposition in many cases. Even when councils vote to end contracts, executives can override the vote (Troy: mayor declared state of emergency).

## Relevance

This note functions as a reference for technology vendor evaluation. The Flock playbook generalizes: any surveillance or data-collection vendor approaching Frederick may use similar tactics -- grant subsidies as the entry vehicle, law enforcement as the internal advocate, and community safety as the primary framing.

The question for Frederick is whether the council has a procurement framework that would surface these dynamics before adoption rather than after. The GovTech / [[Municipal Technology Strategy]] work is the right home for that conversation.
