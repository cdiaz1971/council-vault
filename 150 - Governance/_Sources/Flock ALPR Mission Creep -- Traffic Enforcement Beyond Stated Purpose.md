## Summary

Georgia State Patrol used a Flock automated license plate reader (ALPR) camera to issue a traffic ticket to a motorcyclist seen holding a phone -- despite Flock cameras being marketed exclusively as investigative tools for serious crimes, not traffic enforcement. Many municipalities explicitly promise residents that Flock cameras will not be used for traffic violations. This incident shows that promise is effectively unenforceable.

## Key Points

The December 2026 incident in Coffee County, Georgia involved a ticket for "Holding/supporting wireless telecommunications device." The citation notes "CAPTURED ON FLOCK CAMERA 31 MM 1 HOLDING PHONE IN LEFT HAND." Georgia State Patrol called it a "rare and unique circumstance" -- but the citation itself did not mention the expired registration that was the stated original basis for the stop.

Multiple jurisdictions formally promise residents Flock cameras will not be used for traffic enforcement. Examples from 404 Media's reporting: Glenwood Springs, Colorado; El Paso, Texas; Lynwood, Washington. Lynwood specifically states the cameras will not be used for "immigration enforcement, or monitoring First Amendment-protected expressive activity" -- 404 Media reports Flock cameras have now been used for all three prohibited purposes across different jurisdictions.

There are no meaningful legal restrictions on what police can use Flock data for. Police do not obtain warrants to search Flock's camera network. Network audit spreadsheets obtained via public records requests show officers frequently search license plates with no stated reason.

The motorcyclist appeared in court and the ticket was dropped.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick and Frederick County have been considering or actively evaluating Flock ALPR deployment. This story is a direct warning: whatever policy promises accompany any Flock adoption are not legally enforceable, and mission creep is documented and widespread. If the city or county adopts Flock, the council should be prepared for the full range of uses -- traffic enforcement, immigration enforcement, political surveillance of protests -- not just the investigative crime-solving framing in the vendor pitch. Any community benefit agreement or policy ordinance around surveillance technology must account for the gap between stated use policy and actual use.
