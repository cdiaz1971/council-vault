# Maryland Special Police Work Group First Meeting

## Summary

A Maryland legislative work group established under HB 1152 held its first meeting to study special police officers, surfacing a dramatic training gap: special officers can arrest and detain but receive as few as 80 hours of training versus 1,000+ for traditional officers; report due December 1.

## Key Points

The work group was established by House Bill 1152 (sponsored by Del. Nicole Williams, D-Prince George's) after a veto override saga: Gov. Moore vetoed SB 455 in 2025 (which would have expanded special police eligibility), the legislature overrode unanimously in December, then also passed HB 1152 requiring the study.

Special police officers have the same core powers as traditional officers -- they can detain and arrest people and enforce general criminal laws -- but are limited to a specific property or area designated by their employer. They are hired by entities like Johns Hopkins University and operate under arrangements like the JHU-Baltimore City Police MOU (December 2022). Court bailiffs are also a major category.

**The training gap is the headline finding from the first meeting:**
- State law requires special police to receive at least 80 hours of instruction
- Average actual classroom hours across four academies visited: 250 (ranging from 139 to 360)
- Traditional entry-level officers: minimum 850 hours required; no Maryland academy is under 1,000 hours in practice
- Special police have no required field training component

The study will cover: commissioning process, operations, training and equipment, regulatory oversight, best practices, officer counts in Maryland and surrounding states, complaint records, and public safety impact. Report to governor and General Assembly due December 1.

## Relevance

Frederick uses special police arrangements in limited contexts and has an interim police chief. The training gap finding -- officers with arrest authority receiving a fraction of the training required for traditional officers -- is directly relevant to any public safety governance questions that arise at the council level. The December 1 report should be on the watch list.

The JHU model (private university police with an MOU with city police) is a governance template that other institutions sometimes seek to replicate, and the study may produce recommendations that affect how such arrangements are structured statewide.
