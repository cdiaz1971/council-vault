# New Orleans Police Drone Manual Weaponization Language and the Gap Between Policy and Intent

## Summary

New Orleans police briefly published a drone operations manual allowing weaponized drones with superintendent approval, then quietly replaced it after a civic group noticed -- revealing that what police circulate internally may not match public policy.

## Key Points

NOPD's original drone operations manual (February 2024) said "the sUAS shall not be equipped with weapons of any kind." A revised version dated June 21, 2026 -- published during the council review of a $250,000 budget request to expand Skydio's drone coverage in the French Quarter -- included language allowing weapons with written superintendent approval. After a Louisiana activist group called Eye on Surveillance noticed and started calling council members, the weaponization language disappeared and a third version appeared on July 1 with prohibitory language restored.

NOPD says the June version was a draft presented for review, not final policy. They did not answer follow-up questions about why it was publicly posted.

The Skydio angle matters. Skydio's CEO Adam Bry walked back a 2020 pledge that the company would not weaponize its drones. He said "it's not our place to tell them what they can and can't do" and that "terrorists don't follow terms of service." The Trump administration's ban on foreign-made drones has been good for Skydio's market position.

Matthew Guariglia at EFF made the key point: police are preparing for the day when law enforcement gets whatever they want on drones. Line-of-sight requirements have already been waived. The bar keeps moving. And the argument that armed drones lower the emotional threshold for deploying force -- "pressing a button" rather than confronting a person -- is a direct civil liberties concern.

A pilot project in Georgia is already testing "less lethal" armed drones for school shooting response, illustrating how normalization happens.

## Relevance

This connects to the surveillance procurement governance track in two ways. First, the NOPD pattern -- quiet document revision after civic vigilance, combined with a history of stonewalling public records requests and operating a secret facial recognition network in violation of local law -- is exactly the vendor capture and accountability evasion pattern that Frederick needs policy infrastructure to prevent.

Second, the Skydio CEO's reversal on weaponization is a vendor-side signal worth tracking. If the primary drone supplier to police departments nationally has explicitly dropped its anti-weaponization commitment, any city contracting for drones needs explicit contractual prohibitions.
