# Hybrid Government Meetings as Civic Access Infrastructure -- NY Open Meetings Lessons

## Summary

Reinvent Albany and 38 co-signing organizations are pushing New York's legislature to pass hybrid meeting requirements before COVID-era provisions sunset July 1, 2026. The Frederick question: has the city's meeting accessibility regressed from its COVID peak, and what would a council resolution restoring hybrid public comment look like?

## Key Points

New York's COVID-era videoconferencing provisions for public bodies sunset on July 1, 2026. Without new legislation, government bodies revert to in-person-only requirements. Bills A3615 (Simone) and S1027 (May) would permanently require hybrid meetings -- simultaneous in-person and remote participation -- and close loopholes that allow bodies to technically comply while practically excluding public participation.

The coalition argument: hybrid access is the clearest low-cost way to expand public accessibility to government. The COVID period demonstrated that remote participation works -- people who could not attend in person due to disability, childcare, work schedules, or transportation could participate in public meetings for the first time. Allowing that access to expire is a regression.

Beyond the Open Meetings Law, Reinvent Albany's transparency package includes directing state agencies to fully use existing FOIL software (only 2 of 20 New York agencies accept electronic appeals despite having the software), passing bills to reduce FOIL response times, and requiring the State Senate to publish its confirmation votes publicly.

## Relevance

Frederick holds council meetings in person with streaming and recording available, but no formal remote participation option for public comment. For District 2's immigrant community -- residents who may face barriers to in-person attendance due to language, work schedules, transportation, or childcare -- a hybrid meeting policy would directly improve civic access.

The July 1, 2026 New York sunset is a visible policy moment Frederick can use as a prompt. Maryland does not have the same deadline, but the underlying question is the same: if Frederick offered more remote participation options in 2020-2022 than it does today, that is a documented regression in access that the council could address by resolution.

Maryland's Public Information Act (MPIA) is the local analog to Reinvent Albany's FOIL reform agenda. Reviewing Frederick's MPIA response practices -- electronic submission, response timelines, public disclosure of already-released records -- could identify specific improvements the council could advocate for at the city level without state legislation.
