# Bandera Texas Cancels Flock Contract After Community Resistance

## Summary

Residents of a small Texas town successfully pressured their city council to cancel a Flock Safety license plate reader contract through sustained public opposition, including vandalism of camera poles -- a case study in community veto power over surveillance technology adoption.

## Key Points

Bandera, Texas (population ~900) had eight Flock Safety AI license plate reader cameras installed using a state public safety grant. Over months of sustained community opposition -- including residents repeatedly cutting down the camera poles -- the city council voted 3-2 to terminate the contract. The town had one of the lowest crime rates in the state, which residents used to undercut the safety justification.

The dissenting councilmember Jeff Flowers reacted by proposing a mock ordinance banning all cell phones, GPS devices, internet services, and electronic record-keeping in the city, framing it as a logical extension of the community's stated privacy values. He called this the "Bandera Declaration of Digital Independence." The framing was broadly mocked but illustrates a political strategy surveillance advocates use: accusing privacy advocates of hypocrisy for using technology themselves.

Flock Safety's response is notable as an institutional pattern: the company sent a representative (a former Cleveland city council member now on Flock's public affairs team) to a town hall in a town of 900 people, demonstrating aggressive deployment tactics even at the smallest municipal scale. Flock is also training police officers on how to speak with city councils -- effectively coaching the pro-surveillance argument at the local government level.

The pattern is national: Troy, New York's city council voted to end its Flock contract, but the mayor declared a state of emergency to continue using the cameras. In Dunwoody, Georgia, residents discovered Flock was using their city in sales demos without disclosure.

## Relevance

This case is directly relevant to any Frederick conversation about surveillance technology adoption -- license plate readers, predictive policing tools, camera networks. The Bandera case shows: (1) community opposition can work when sustained, (2) vendors actively lobby local governments below the radar, and (3) the political argument from surveillance advocates conflates public accountability with privacy invasion.

As Council President, the procedural question is: does Frederick have a technology procurement policy that requires community input before surveillance tools are adopted? If not, the Bandera case is a useful data point for why such a policy matters. The Flock model of going city by city, council by council, using state grants to subsidize adoption, is a replicable playbook that other vendors use too.
