# AI for Participatory Governance -- Listening at Scale as Democratic Infrastructure

## Summary

Governments in Hamburg, Bogota, and New Jersey are using AI to synthesize mass public input in ways that make genuine participatory governance feasible for the first time -- turning a logistical impossibility into a scalable practice.

## Key Points

Noveck's argument is that one of the most durable excuses governments use for closed-door decision-making is logistical: processing large volumes of public comment at the pace of governing is genuinely hard. AI changes that constraint.

The cases she cites:

- **Hamburg, Germany** -- using AI tools to synthesize public input for all city planning decisions; has shared those tools free and open source with nine other cities.
- **Bogota, Colombia** -- expanded participatory budgeting to 50,000 participants in two weeks using AI to process and act on input at that scale.
- **New Jersey** -- asked workers and the public "what should be our AI policy?" and used AI to synthesize the responses into actionable guidance. Turned a process that would have produced paralysis into one that produced a statewide training program.
- **Massachusetts / GrantWell** -- AI for Impact Fellowship students built a free, open-source grant application tool for small cities and towns that is now rolling out across multiple states, with zero taxpayer cost.

The pattern across these cases: AI doesn't replace the public engagement; it removes the bottleneck that made genuine engagement unaffordable. The chief forester example from Noveck's NJ work is the cleanest -- a forestry expert, not a data scientist, was able to run real public input processes because AI handled synthesis.

Noveck's warning: this only works if the tools are designed for public purpose. The same AI that enables Hamburg to do participatory planning could, in the wrong hands, enable a government to perform engagement while ignoring its outputs. The question is always who controls the tool and what it optimizes for.

## Relevance

For Frederick, this framing has two immediate applications:

1. The data center ZTA process has a public engagement deficit -- community opposition has been vocal but the formal record is thin. A model like Bogota's participatory synthesis would give that opposition structural weight. The Planning Commission record is the right venue; this note is background for building that argument.
2. The Gov Ops Technology work plan is the right vehicle for establishing that Frederick's AI procurement standards include a public-purpose requirement. Noveck's open-source, publicly governed model framing should inform the contract language thread in [[Municipal Tech Consent Architecture -- Contract Language, Audit Standards, and Resident Notification]].

The GrantWell model is directly relevant to Frederick's capacity gaps in grant writing and federal program access -- worth surfacing to Gayon Sampson or whoever handles grants administration.
