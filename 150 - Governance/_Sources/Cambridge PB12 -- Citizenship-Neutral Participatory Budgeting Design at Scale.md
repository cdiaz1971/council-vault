# Cambridge PB12 -- Citizenship-Neutral Participatory Budgeting Design at Scale

## Summary

Cambridge's 12th PB cycle (2026) is open to all residents 12 and older regardless of citizenship, available in ten languages, and uses a multi-stage delegate research model to filter ideas through feasibility review before balloting. Cambridge has allocated over $11 million to 80+ projects since 2014.

## Key Points

Cambridge PB12 opened for voting March 5-15, 2026. Eligibility: all residents 12 and older, including non-citizens, undocumented residents, and college students living in Cambridge. Voters selected up to five of 20 ballot projects without ranking.

The ballot was available in ten languages: English, Amharic, Arabic, Bengali, Chinese, Haitian Creole, Hindi, Japanese, Portuguese, and Spanish. Voting options included online, by phone in English, and in person at multiple city locations. Phone voting in English provides a fallback for residents not comfortable with digital platforms -- a design detail that matters for older or less digitally connected residents.

The 20 ballot projects went through a multi-stage process: community idea submissions from October 2025 through February 2026, volunteer delegate research and prioritization, and city staff feasibility review (need, impact, cost) before balloting. This filters out unfeasible projects while keeping the core funding decision in residents' hands.

Cambridge's track record: 12 cycles, $11+ million allocated, 80+ projects completed. The program survived multiple budget cycles, political transitions, and the COVID-19 pandemic. That durability demonstrates both fiscal sustainability and community capacity-building -- the volunteer delegate system trains residents in civic deliberation skills that persist across cycles.

The citizenship-neutral design is a deliberate policy choice, not an oversight: immigration status is explicitly not a criterion for participation.

## Relevance

Cambridge PB12 is the clearest available model for a Frederick pilot. The ten-language ballot is aspirationally ambitious at Frederick's scale, but Spanish, Arabic, and possibly Amharic or Haitian Creole would cover the dominant non-English language communities in District 2.

The eligibility framework directly addresses the participation gap in District 2. Standard civic processes exclude non-citizens by design. A PB program that explicitly welcomes them is the council's most direct available tool for giving District 2's immigrant residents a genuine voice in resource allocation decisions.

The delegate research model is worth replicating. It prevents the ballot from being dominated by unfeasible projects, which protects residents (from voting for things that will not happen) and the city (from making commitments it cannot keep). At Frederick's scale, a pilot would likely involve a small capital allocation, a volunteer delegate cohort drawn from RISE Coalition partners, and a bilingual ballot at minimum. Cambridge has 12 cycles of documentation to draw from -- Frederick does not need to design this from scratch.
