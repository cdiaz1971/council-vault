# Flock ALPR Cameras in 6,000 US Communities -- Centralized Database Without Court Approval

## Summary

Flock Safety's automated license plate reader (ALPR) cameras have reached saturation scale: 6,000 communities and 5,000+ law enforcement agencies as of mid-2026. The system captures not just license plates but vehicle make, model, color, bumper stickers, dents, precise location, and timestamp -- all stored in a centralized, searchable national database accessible to law enforcement on demand, without a court order.

## Key Points

The ACLU and other critics identify two core problems. First, the cameras photograph every vehicle that passes, regardless of any suspicion of wrongdoing -- not targeted surveillance but mass collection. Second, the centralized database is accessible nationally by law enforcement without judicial oversight, meaning data collected in one city can be queried by agencies in another without a warrant.

Flock's own claims frame this as a crime-solving tool: the company estimates roughly 10% of reported US crimes are solved using its technology, amounting to approximately 700,000 crimes annually. This framing is the political counterweight to privacy concerns -- the company can point to concrete public safety outcomes while opponents argue about the surveillance architecture.

The community backlash documented in Route Fifty's coverage is widespread. The cameras have generated organized opposition in many of the communities where they have been deployed.

## Relevance

The immigration protection angle is direct and was flagged during the City Values Act debates: Flock camera data, including vehicle movement patterns, can be accessed by federal immigration enforcement. A Frederick resident driving between home, work, and Centro Hispano creates a trackable pattern in the Flock database that ICE can query without a warrant. This is the scenario that advocates like Christian Benford raised explicitly during CVA discussions -- prohibiting use of traffic cameras made by companies known to share data with immigration enforcement.

This note belongs in the governance/technology surveillance file but has strong connections to the immigration protection thread. If Frederick ever considers expanding ALPR deployment or enters a contract with Flock or a comparable vendor, this is the briefing material for a council intervention.

Worth cross-referencing with the Data Privacy Act (HB 711), which prohibits selling personal data for immigration enforcement -- the question is whether Flock's law enforcement access model falls within or outside that prohibition.
