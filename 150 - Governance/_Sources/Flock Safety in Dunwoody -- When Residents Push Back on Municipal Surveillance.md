# Flock Safety in Dunwoody -- When Residents Push Back on Municipal Surveillance

## Summary

A Dunwoody, Georgia resident obtained Flock Safety access logs via public records request and discovered that Flock employees had been remotely accessing the city's surveillance cameras -- including cameras in a children's gymnastics room, playground, school, and Jewish community center -- as live product demonstrations for other police departments. The city was an official "demo partner." Flock confirmed the access, apologized, and agreed to stop using sensitive locations for demos. The city renewed the contract anyway.

## Key Points

The mechanism matters: Dunwoody had a dense network of Flock cameras encompassing not just city-purchased hardware but also privately owned cameras from businesses that had voluntarily joined the network. Flock employees with access to the system could view any of those feeds. The "demo partner" arrangement meant Flock had blanket authorization to access those feeds for sales purposes -- something the city's own residents had no idea about until public records revealed it.

The resident who surfaced this, Jason Hunyar, had never filed a public records request before. He found the access logs, wrote them up, and sent them to the mayor and city council before publishing. Mayor Lynn Deutsch's response was to suggest it was irresponsible of him to make the information public because it revealed the city's security setup to potential bad actors.

At a subsequent three-hour city council meeting, residents made detailed, accurate arguments about Flock's broader history: its data sharing with ICE, its cameras being used by officers to stalk women, its security scandals. The mayor said she was "concerned and perplexed" when she learned about the demo access, said she had found a solution (Flock agreed to stop using sensitive locations), and then indicated she had no interest in ending the contract. Dunwoody voted to renew.

Flock's defense relied heavily on the transparency argument: that it generates access logs at all, making it more transparent than competitors, and that the logs can be obtained via public records -- which is what surfaced the problem in the first place.

Multiple cities across the country have ended Flock contracts following organized resident pressure. Dunwoody chose not to. Georgia's Attorney General publicly praised the city for standing by Flock.

## Relevance

This is a detailed case study in what happens when a municipality treats a surveillance vendor contract as a technical decision rather than a civic one. The residents were informed, organized, accurate, and persistent -- and they lost anyway. For Frederick, this is a useful reference point for any discussion of LPR technology, Flock Safety specifically, or surveillance contracting generally. The public records angle is the key tool: access logs exist, and FOIA requests work.

The ICE data-sharing dimension connects to Frederick's immigration policy work. Flock data has been shared with ICE in other jurisdictions. That's a direct conflict with any City Values Act framework.
