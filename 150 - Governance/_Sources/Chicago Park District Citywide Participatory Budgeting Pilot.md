# Chicago Park District Citywide Participatory Budgeting Pilot

## Summary
The Chicago Park District ran its first citywide participatory budgeting cycle in 2026, a $500,000 pilot drawn from its capital budget in which residents submitted 1,082 park improvement ideas and then voted on project categories rather than individual projects.

## Key Points
- **Scale and funding.** An inaugural $500,000 set aside from the district's capital infrastructure budget, with individual projects sized around $50,000. Big-ticket capital items (new fieldhouses, athletic field lighting) were excluded up front.
- **Cycle.** Idea collection January 17 to March 31, 2026, online and at in-person park events. Staff feasibility review (cost, safety, viability) completed in June. Voting July 27 to August 27, online and by ballot box at fieldhouses citywide, plus printed forms. Awards announced later in 2026, construction fall 2026 into 2027.
- **Design choice.** Staff grouped the 1,082 submitted ideas into broader categories (for example, permanent fixtures and park amenities), and residents voted on categories. That keeps the vote manageable but shifts real discretion back to staff in choosing specific projects.
- **Park-specific tracks.** Separate steering committees run PB at Douglass Park and Grant Park, funded by a share of large-event revenue at those parks. At Douglass Park, 30% of Riot Fest permit fees are earmarked for PB, with about $241,000 allocated.
- **Leadership framing.** General Superintendent Carlos Ramirez-Rosa pitches it as deepening engagement, increasing transparency in spending, and building trust in park planning.

## Relevance
This is the small, administratively owned end of the PB spectrum, the opposite of Portland's charter-mandated 2% set-aside. A Frederick version would look more like this: a modest pilot carved from the capital budget or an existing revenue stream, run by a department, with no charter change. The event-revenue model is especially portable. Frederick could direct a share of special event permit fees from the forthcoming Special Events Commission (G-26-9) or Gateway facility revenue to neighborhood park improvements chosen by residents. It also offers a concrete counterweight to SPARK, which collects priorities from Council members, not residents.
