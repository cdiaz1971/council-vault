# Police AI Procurement Outpaces Legal Frameworks -- No National Standard Exists

## Summary
Hundreds of law enforcement agencies across the US have adopted AI tools for report drafting, body camera analysis, license plate reading, facial recognition, and evidence management. The legal and regulatory infrastructure to govern those tools has not kept pace. The result is a landscape where accountability depends almost entirely on whether individual vendors or departments choose to self-regulate.

## Key Points
Major vendors in the police AI space include Axon, Motorola Solutions, Mark43, TRULEO, Flock Safety, and Clearview AI. Their tools span report writing (using dispatch audio and body camera footage), case file summarization, digital evidence search, and facial recognition for suspect identification.

The regulatory picture is thin. California and Utah have passed laws requiring disclosure and human verification when AI assists in writing police reports. More than a dozen states have passed laws on related technologies (facial recognition, drones, ALPR), but the coverage is uneven and enforcement is weak. There is no federal framework.

The Council on Criminal Justice released a framework calling for rigorous independent validation of AI systems, enforceable procurement standards, ongoing performance monitoring, and clear human override capability. None of those elements are required by law anywhere in the United States.

The speed problem is structural, as a Santa Fe Institute computer scientist noted: "The speed at which technologically created evidence has been adopted, and the aggression with which it's being pushed makes it hard for the legal community to keep up." Courts are receiving AI-assisted evidence before they have developed standards for evaluating it.

The misuse pattern is already documented. Individual officers across at least nine states have been found using ALPR and law enforcement databases for personal surveillance of spouses, romantic rivals, and others. Vendor audit logs help identify misuse after the fact but do not prevent it.

## Relevance
This is the governance gap that César's surveillance technology procurement work is trying to address at the local level. Frederick cannot wait for Congress or the Maryland legislature to set standards -- by the time they do, contracts will already be embedded. The actionable question raised by this note is whether Frederick has existing procurement policy language that applies to AI systems used in law enforcement, and whether that language requires independent validation, audit logging, use-policy disclosure, and human override documentation before a contract is signed.
