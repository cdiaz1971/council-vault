# 'I Saw a Shiny Thing': Cop Explains Why He Used License Plate Reader to Stalk Woman

## Summary
404 Media obtained body camera footage of the internal investigation into Florida officer Lamar Roman, who met a woman on a TV show set, illegally researched her through DMV and police databases, put her plate on an ALPR hot list, and stalked and pulled her over. The footage shows how casually the misuse was treated internally and how light the consequences were.

## Key Points
Roman used the DAVID (Department of Motor Vehicles) database and a separate police records system to pull the woman's license, vehicle, address, and date of birth after meeting her once. He then entered her plate into an ALPR hot list on Guardian, a system made by a company called Turing, so he would be alerted whenever her car passed a camera. He used that alert to intercept and pull her over without legal cause, passing multiple cars at speed to catch up to her.

The investigating detective told the victim, on camera, that misuse of these databases is a recurring problem: "we've had deputies misuse databases, we've told them over and over again, that's not what it's for. You see a hot chick, you don't look them up in a database." Roman was fired and lost database access, then pleaded to stalking and improper database access charges without jail time. Throughout the footage, both the arresting detective and a colleague treat the episode as a minor career setback for Roman rather than a serious abuse of power against the victim.

The story lands amid a broader pattern: Flock's CEO now acknowledges "a lot" of officer misuse cases, after the company initially claimed to know of only 15, and after independent reporting (Institute for Justice, Washington Post) counted at least 50.
