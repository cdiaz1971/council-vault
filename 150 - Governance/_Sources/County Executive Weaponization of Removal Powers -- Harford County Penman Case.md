# County Executive Weaponization of Removal Powers -- Harford County Penman Case

## Summary

After 600 days off the council following a wrongful removal orchestrated by Harford County Executive Bob Cassilly, Republican Councilmember Aaron Penman was ordered reinstated by Circuit Court Judge W. Michel Pierson in June 2026. The case is a documented example of a county executive using legal procedures to block elected legislators from serving, and of the judiciary ultimately functioning as a check -- but only after nearly two years of delay.

## Key Points

The removal originated from Cassilly's challenge to Penman's eligibility to serve while employed as a Harford County Sheriff's deputy. Cassilly made the same argument against a second councilmember (a county teacher). Both challenges succeeded at the circuit court level under the same judge; both were eventually overturned on appeal. The pattern suggests coordinated legal strategy rather than good-faith eligibility questions.

The practical effects of the removal were significant: Penman was forced to withdraw from his reelection bid because the appeal was still unresolved. The seat was filled by Alison Imhoff, who was placed in an awkward position but ultimately filed an affidavit saying she accepted Penman was wrongfully removed and would not block his return.

Even after the Appellate Court of Maryland vacated the original order, Cassilly's attorney continued to obstruct: challenging whether Penman had "resigned" rather than been removed (a distinction the judge flatly rejected based on the written record), filing a late motion to add the Republican Central Committee as a party, and asking for a stay of the reinstatement order. Judge Pierson became visibly impatient with the procedural delays.

The county said it plans to appeal and seek a stay -- meaning Penman may still be blocked from returning despite the court order, depending on whether a stay is granted.

## Relevance

This case belongs in the democratic resilience thread. It documents a specific mechanism -- executive challenge to legislator eligibility -- used to remove an intraparty opponent from office for nearly two years without permanent success but with significant practical consequences (lost election opportunity, disrupted representation for constituents). The pattern is relevant to understanding how elected officials at the local level can be targeted and what institutional protections exist (appellate courts) and don't exist (no fast-track reinstatement mechanism, no penalty for the executive who brought the bad-faith challenge).

It is also a reminder that the relationship between county executive and county council (or in Frederick's context, mayor and council) can be adversarial in ways that are not always visible until a conflict surfaces. Worth keeping as reference for any future discussion of council independence and procedural protections.
