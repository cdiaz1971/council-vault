# Extractive Research Harms the Communities It Claims to Serve

## Summary

The dominant model of institutional research treats communities of color as objects to study rather than as partners with expertise and self-determination. This extractive pattern -- institutions set the agenda, researchers parachute in, data is analyzed elsewhere, and conclusions are announced without community input -- produces mistrust, disillusionment, and policy failures. The Newark school reform case illustrates the pattern at scale: a well-funded, expert-driven plan announced on Oprah sparked massive public outcry instead of community buy-in.

## Key Points

Dr. Brittany Lewis traces a consistent pattern across institutions -- government agencies, nonprofits, universities, and philanthropies -- that claim to serve communities while structurally excluding them from defining the problem or shaping the solution. The standard research process she describes: funders set the questions, hire professional researchers, those researchers extract data from the community through surveys or brief focus groups, analyze it externally, and announce findings. The community may be invited to respond to documents already written.

The result is a system that substitutes a "limited data set of how they interacted with one specific service at some specific point in time" for genuine understanding. Paula Wolff of the Illinois Justice Project is quoted to this effect: institutions claim to be "evidence-based" while having no actual understanding of the context of people's lives.

Lewis names the ideological root: these organizations, despite their intentions, operate within frameworks shaped by "dominator culture" -- patriarchal, profit-centered, white supremacy norms that treat professional credentials as more authoritative than lived experience. The go-to community representative, the tokenized review, the post-hoc feedback invitation -- these are symptoms of an institutional culture that has not genuinely redistributed power.

The Tuskegee experiment is the extreme case. The Newark school reform is the contemporary one: $100 million from the Zuckerberg Foundation, designed by outside experts, announced publicly before community engagement had occurred. The outcome was polarization, not improvement.

## Relevance

This maps directly onto how César's council work intersects with community organizations like Centro Hispano, SSCM, and AACF. The research patterns Lewis describes are not confined to academia -- they show up whenever a city agency, planning consultant, or outside funder designs a policy intervention for a community without genuine partnership in defining the problem. Frederick's housing and immigration work is vulnerable to exactly this dynamic: well-intentioned city planning that treats community members as input-providers rather than co-designers.

It also connects to the broader tension in César's policy track between data-driven governance (Philadelphia Philly Stat model) and community-centered governance. These are not automatically in conflict, but the framing matters enormously. Who defines what the data is measuring, and for whose benefit?
