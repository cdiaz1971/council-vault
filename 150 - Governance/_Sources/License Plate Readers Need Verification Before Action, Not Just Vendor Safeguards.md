# License Plate Readers Need Verification Before Action, Not Just Vendor Safeguards

## Summary
A former Minneapolis police chief argues that license plate reader and police AI alerts must require human verification before any stop, detention, arrest, or use of force, citing LA and San Francisco cases where unverified alerts led to wrongful stops and excessive force.

## Key Points
- Author Brian O'Hara served as the 54th Chief of the Minneapolis Police Department and previously as Deputy Mayor and Public Safety Director in Newark, NJ, where he led implementation of a federal consent decree.
- More than 50 agencies or communities have canceled or suspended Flock/ALPR contracts, rejected proposals, or deactivated cameras in 2026, with lawmakers in both parties pursuing new restrictions.
- An independent audit of Minneapolis PD's 2023-2024 license plate reader activity (covering O'Hara's tenure as chief) found compliance with state statutory requirements, but auditors still recommended formal procedures for access, retention, deletion, and periodic review -- his framing: "Compliance is the floor, not the finish line."
- Los Angeles data: during a two-month review, in-car plate reader alerts led to 337 stolen-vehicle recoveries and 68 stops resulting in 74 arrests, but officers also acknowledged 161 alerts as "correct plate matches" even though later investigation found the vehicles were not actually stolen.
- San Francisco's police watchdog found officers used excessive force in a wrongful arrest after an officer manually entered the wrong plate and failed to verify before breaking a driver's window -- a human data-entry failure, not an AI or automated-reader failure.
- Flock has recommended shorter retention, case-code requirements, audit monitoring, and automatic suspensions for abnormal searches pending review; O'Hara argues these vendor-side controls "cannot substitute for law, effective supervision or independent review."
- Proposed five safeguards for any jurisdiction deploying ALPR/police AI: (1) define the use and expressly prohibit others, (2) test independently under real-world conditions before deployment and after updates, (3) control the data -- minimize retention/sharing/access and put vendor-assurance and deletion requirements into contracts, (4) verify before action -- corroborate any alert before a stop, detention, arrest, or use of force, (5) audit and enforce -- log every search, review independently, report publicly, and impose consequences for misuse.
- Core argument: because ALPR networks cross jurisdictional lines, state law should set a governance floor while local governments retain authority to adopt stricter limits and write them into contracts.

## Relevance
Directly useful to the [[Police Surveillance Technology]] page and any future Frederick council discussion of a Flock/ALPR contract, oversight ordinance, or data-sharing policy.
