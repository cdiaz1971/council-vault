# Portland Measure 26-267 Participatory Budgeting Charter Set-Aside and Opposition

## Summary
Portland's November 2026 Measure 26-267 would write a permanent 2% participatory budgeting set-aside into the city charter. The Oregonian editorial board, the mayor, and city labor unions oppose it, arguing it diverts about $16 million a year from core services during a deficit and leaves the process undefined.

## Key Points
- **What it does.** A charter amendment requiring Council to dedicate at least 2% of adopted General Fund discretionary ongoing expenses to a participatory budgeting program, though Council may draw the money from anywhere in the city budget, including dedicated funds like the Parks Levy or the Clean Energy Fund. Estimated $16.4 million in FY2027-28 and $17.0 million in FY2028-29. An oversight committee of residents from every district helps design it. Any resident can propose citywide or district projects; staff and budget delegates narrow them; a binding resident vote picks winners. Operational July 2027. The vote is exempt from city and state election law.
- **How it qualified.** Backed by Participatory Budgeting Oregon, Next Up Action Fund, and East County Rising, which raised about $554,000. Submitted 78,743 signatures, the most ever for a Portland initiative, with 41,860 verified against 40,437 needed.
- **The opposition case** (Oregonian editorial endorsement and letters, Mayor Keith Wilson, Northwest Oregon Labor Council's Laurie Wimmer chairing the no PAC): Portland faces roughly a $160 million shortfall, and a permanent set-aside pulls money from shelter beds, parks, Portland Street Response, firefighting, and investigators, with likely layoffs. Wilson argues no American city has hard-coded a PB set-aside into its charter. Critics note the measure leaves voter eligibility, age cutoff, voting method, and funding source to be decided after passage, and that Seattle's 2023 PB drew about 4,200 voters out of nearly 500,000 registered, raising capture concerns by organized groups. A further open question is whether funded projects create recurring maintenance costs the general fund then absorbs.
- **The supporters' case.** Portland is the last major West Coast city without PB. The flexibility is intentional so Council can set themes and funding sources. PB brings in younger, immigrant, and historically excluded residents and builds trust. Councilor Candace Avalos argued for it.

## Relevance
The Portland fight is the clearest current argument against the maximal version of PB, and labor's opposition is the instructive part: a mandated set-aside reads to public sector unions as a claim on the budget that funds their members' jobs. For Frederick, where collective bargaining is a live priority alongside Nash, any PB proposal needs to be designed so it does not pit resident decision-making against city workers. The Chicago model in [[Chicago Park District Citywide Participatory Budgeting Pilot]] (a capital-budget pilot, no charter mandate) avoids most of these objections. The low-turnout and capture critique is the one to answer with design: eligibility rules like those in [[Cambridge PB12 -- Citizenship-Neutral Participatory Budgeting Design at Scale]] and real outreach budgets. Result to watch on November 3.
