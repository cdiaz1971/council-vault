# Facial Recognition Technology -- Reform Frameworks and Policy Safeguards

## Summary

Following a growing pattern of wrongful arrests tied to facial recognition technology, the ACLU has articulated a set of concrete policy reforms for law enforcement agencies. These proposals address both the reliability problem (FRT produces false matches at a meaningful rate) and the accountability problem (agencies have concealed their reliance on the technology from courts). As of April 2026, at least fourteen people in the United States have been wrongfully arrested due to erroneous FRT results.

## Key Points

**Why FRT produces wrongful arrests:** Facial recognition systems match a query image against a database and return a ranked list of probable candidates -- they do not confirm identity. When the underlying image is low-quality (e.g., surveillance camera footage), match accuracy degrades significantly. Officers who treat a returned candidate as a confirmed suspect, especially without independent investigation, create serious wrongful arrest risk. The Williams case shows that this problem compounds when the FRT scan is conducted by an unknown third party with no chain of custody documentation.

**Proposed reforms (ACLU):**
- Ban arrests based solely on a facial recognition result, even when followed by a human visual confirmation (since the human is anchoring to the FRT result, not conducting an independent ID)
- Prohibit reliance on FRT searches conducted by outside entities -- police departments should only use results from vetted systems with known methodology and chain of custody
- Require disclosure to courts when FRT was used in building probable cause for a warrant
- Mandate internal investigation when FRT contributes to a wrongful arrest

**The concealment problem:** The Williams case is notable not just for the false match but for the active concealment of FRT use from the court. If agencies routinely obscure surveillance tool use in warrant applications, judicial oversight is effectively bypassed. This is a structural accountability failure, not just a technology accuracy problem.

**Legislative context:** Several states are moving toward FRT regulation. Maryland itself has not enacted comprehensive FRT restrictions at the state level. Local governments have broader latitude to regulate municipal agency use of surveillance technology, including FRT procurement and deployment policies.
