# Flock Camera Removal in Verona Wisconsin Municipal Pushback and Offboarding Barriers

## Summary

After voting to stop using Flock ALPR cameras, Verona Wisconsin faced weeks of delays, corporate resistance, and legal ambiguity before covering the cameras in black trash bags -- a case study in how surveillance vendor contracts constrain municipal authority even after democratic exit.

## Key Points

In November 2025, Verona's Common Council voted to not renew its Flock contract, citing the company's practice of sharing surveillance data with federal agencies including ICE. What followed was a months-long ordeal documented through public records obtained by 404 Media.

The core problems:

Flock told city employees not to remove the cameras themselves. When asked whether the cameras could be remotely disabled, a Flock representative said he wasn't sure and would look into it. A work order Flock sent automatically listed the cameras for "maintenance" rather than removal. The mayor eventually directed staff to consider removing the cameras themselves, noting the contract had expired and he saw nothing preventing it. A police officer asked the city to hold off while he negotiated with Flock, who formally "objected" to the city removing its own equipment. After all that, public works bagged the cameras in black plastic trash bags on January 23 while awaiting physical removal. Flock eventually came and removed them on February 4.

The council president at the November vote articulated the underlying distrust plainly: Flock had given assurances about data governance, but the company's actual behavior with federal agencies made those assurances worthless. One alderman said "we can't trust that" even with guarantees in writing.

Flock's post-hoc explanation was that Verona had voted not to renew rather than canceling mid-contract, which triggered a different removal process. From Verona's perspective, that distinction was invisible and irrelevant -- they had made multiple explicit requests and the cameras kept running.

Notable detail: a Flock automated email during the process stated the work order had been resolved and that "your device should not be functioning normally" -- an apparent typo for "normally" that also captured the absurdity of the situation.

## Relevance

This is the most detailed documented account of what "deFlocking" actually looks like operationally. For Frederick's Flock procurement governance work, this surfaced three specific vulnerabilities:

1. Contract language around termination vs. non-renewal creates ambiguity about when a city can enforce removal.
2. Vendors can claim equipment ownership as grounds to resist city removal even after the contract ends.
3. Remote disable capability, which Flock markets as a feature, may be operationally unavailable when needed.

The Verona case also confirms the ICE data-sharing concern that has driven other cities to terminate -- council members explicitly cited it as the reason for their vote. This aligns directly with the City Values Act rationale and the surveillance procurement governance framework.

The "bag the cameras" outcome is worth noting as a practical interim measure cities have used while waiting for vendor compliance. It has now happened in multiple jurisdictions.
