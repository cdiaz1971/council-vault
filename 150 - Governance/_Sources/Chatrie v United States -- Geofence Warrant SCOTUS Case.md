# Chatrie v United States -- Geofence Warrant SCOTUS Case

## Summary

SCOTUS heard oral arguments in Chatrie v. United States, a Fourth Amendment challenge to geofence warrants -- orders requiring Google to hand over location data on all phones within a defined geographic area. The Court appeared likely to allow such warrants with limits on time and space, not prohibit them outright.

## Key Points

**The case**

Okello Chatrie was convicted of armed robbery of a federal credit union in Midlothian, Virginia in 2019. A detective obtained a geofence warrant directing Google to produce location data for all devices within 150 meters of the credit union during a one-hour window. Google provided anonymized data, then additional data on nine users, then de-anonymized data on three -- all without a second warrant at each step.

**What the Court heard**

The Trump administration argued location history is not protected by the Fourth Amendment because users voluntarily share it with Google when they enable location tracking -- a consent-based theory. Chatrie's lawyers argued a geofence warrant is a general warrant prohibited by the Constitution: it sweeps in everyone in a geographic area without individualized suspicion.

Justice Sotomayor probed the consent theory: if voluntary sharing with Google means no warrant is required, then the government could seek photos, calendar entries, and all other Google data without a warrant for any reason. The administration's counsel confirmed that would follow from the same logic.

Justice Barrett noted both sides risked being "maximalist" -- the government in claiming broad data access without individualized suspicion, Chatrie's side in seeking a categorical prohibition on geofence warrants.

**The likely outcome**

Stanford law professor Orin Kerr, a leading Fourth Amendment scholar, predicted the Court would hold that geofence warrants are constitutionally permissible when appropriately limited in time and space -- not a categorical ruling either way on whether the Chatrie warrant itself was a "search."

**The coalition**

31 states and DC filed an amicus brief supporting geofence warrants, signed by attorneys general across the partisan spectrum. Maryland signed. They argued geofence warrants, when tailored and supported by probable cause, are among the narrowest available tools for identifying unknown suspects tied to a specific location and time.

Google filed its own amicus opposing the broad use of geofence warrants, arguing they are structurally overbroad -- even small geographic areas can encompass hundreds of thousands of users, and the parameters routinely include private homes, houses of worship, and sensitive locations.

**The 2018 precedent**

The Court's last major digital privacy ruling (Carpenter v. United States, 2018) required warrants for location data generated by cell tower connections. Chatrie extends the analysis to voluntary location tracking -- a meaningfully different data type because it requires active user opt-in, which the government argues breaks the privacy expectation.

## Relevance

Directly relevant to [[ALPR Policy and Surveillance Governance]] and the broader surveillance accountability work relevant to Frederick. The geofence warrant doctrine determines how much control local governments actually have over their residents' location data when federal law enforcement comes calling. If the Court holds that voluntary-disclosure kills the privacy expectation, it has implications far beyond geofence warrants -- it touches any location data held by apps, platforms, or commercial data brokers.

For Frederick specifically: the same Fourth Amendment questions that apply to Google location data apply to ALPR networks and Flock Safety data. The analytical framework the Court develops in Chatrie will shape the legal landscape for any city trying to build meaningful data governance constraints on surveillance technology.
