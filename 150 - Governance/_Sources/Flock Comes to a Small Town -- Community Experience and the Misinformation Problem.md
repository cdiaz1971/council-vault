# Flock Comes to a Small Town -- Community Experience and the Misinformation Problem

## Summary

404 Media podcast episode documents a community member's experience when Flock came to his small town, the growing no-phone Luddite event movement, and the problem of anti-Flock social media influencers who fabricate claims -- which ultimately makes accurate opposition harder to sustain.

## Key Points

**Community experience:** "Matthew" describes what happened when Flock came to his small town -- a microcosm of what is playing out across the country as cities and towns navigate whether they want ALPR cameras in their communities. The episode frames this as a genuinely national conversation happening at a near-constant pace across local government.

**The Luddite moment:** Rising "no phone" events and social gatherings are reframing technology refusal as a positive community practice rather than a privacy-activist stance. This is culturally significant -- it normalizes opting out of surveillance-adjacent technologies in everyday social contexts.

**The misinformation problem:** Anti-Flock influencers on social media are making up claims and spreading false information about the company and the cameras. This is a real problem for accurate opposition: when false claims circulate alongside true ones, Flock can credibly challenge the whole set. It also gives Flock's government affairs staff a tool to dismiss opposition as misinformation-driven rather than evidence-based.

## Relevance

The misinformation problem is the most practically useful piece for Frederick's surveillance governance work. Any council discussion of Flock or ALPR procurement should be grounded in documented, verifiable claims from credible sources -- the 404 Media reporting, the ACLU, IREHR, and the whistleblower account in [[Flock Safety Whistleblower Jonathan Paz Reveals ICE Deception and Council Manipulation]] -- rather than social media claims. The community experience dimension confirms the pattern: Flock's sales process and the community opposition to it are both happening on a fast, repeating cycle across American municipalities. The longer Frederick goes without a formal procurement policy, the more vulnerable it is to the same process playing out locally with less community preparedness.
