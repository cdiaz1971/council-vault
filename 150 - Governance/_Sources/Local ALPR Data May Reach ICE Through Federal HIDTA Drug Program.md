# Local ALPR Data May Reach ICE Through Federal HIDTA Drug Program

## Summary
The ACLU, citing reporting by 404Media and the creator of HaveIBeenFlocked, says local police automated license plate reader (ALPR) data -- from vendors like Flock, Axon, and Motorola/Vigilant -- is being aggregated onto federal servers through the High Intensity Drug Trafficking Area (HIDTA) program, where it becomes accessible to ICE and other federal agencies, often without clear local awareness or control.

## Key Points
HIDTA sits under the White House Office of National Drug Control Policy ("the Drug Czar") and comprises 33 regional programs covering all 50 states. 404Media's reporting, which the ACLU piece relies on without independent verification, found that the White House and several states have moved to feed local ALPR systems into HIDTA. One documented example: Georgia law requires cities to sign a memorandum of understanding promising to send ALPR data to a HIDTA before they may operate cameras on state rights of way; the ACLU links an MOU for the Atlanta-Carolinas HIDTA obtained via a MuckRock request tied to the Randolph County Sheriff's Office. The article describes the HIDTA data channel as "virtually automatic" -- a route into federal access that does not depend on a direct vendor-to-ICE contract.

Transparency is a documented problem: 404Media calls the program "complex" and "convoluted," with confusion even among participating agencies about who controls HIDTA-collected data. The DEA has argued in court that HIDTA records are not subject to open-records law, and a North Carolina sheriff's office refused a records request on the grounds that "all data is kept on the Federal Houston-HIDTA database," outside local control. With dozens of HIDTAs nationally, neither 404Media nor the ACLU could say how many jurisdictions are actively sending data in.

Context: ICE's access to local ALPR data became a national flashpoint as the Trump administration expanded mass deportation efforts, prompting a wave of state laws restricting local-to-federal data sharing. Flock subsequently canceled its direct contract with ICE, but Motorola/Vigilant reportedly continues to share data directly with federal agencies -- making the HIDTA channel a potential backdoor around vendor-level restrictions. A related item noted in the piece: a Senate Judiciary Subcommittee on Crime and Counterterrorism hearing on Flock's nationwide AI surveillance network was held September 23, 2026, with ACLU counsel Chad Marlow testifying; separately, the Institute for Justice has cataloged over 200 documented ALPR abuse incidents, and more than 200 cities have ended Flock or other ALPR contracts since the start of 2026.

The ACLU's bottom-line recommendation for local governments and communities: beyond the standard questions (how many ALPR cameras, how long data is retained), explicitly ask whether the local police department is funneling plate data into a HIDTA. Its stated policy position is that the only reliable protection is ending ALPR and other mass-surveillance programs altogether, rather than regulating their federal data flows after the fact.

## Relevance
This bears directly on the Police Surveillance Technology page and on any Frederick Police Department ALPR/Flock usage: if Frederick PD participates in a Maryland HIDTA (likely the Washington/Baltimore HIDTA region) or has signed any state-mandated data-sharing MOU, local ALPR data could reach ICE regardless of whatever direct vendor-level restrictions the City negotiates or whatever stance Frederick takes toward 287(g) and the Community Trust Act. This is a concrete oversight question worth raising with Police Chief Kevin Meyer or City Attorney Rachel Gray: whether Frederick PD's ALPR data (if any) flows into a HIDTA, and whether that flow is disclosed anywhere in existing surveillance-technology reporting to Council. It also intersects with the City Values Act's local non-cooperation goals -- a HIDTA pathway could undermine CVA intent even without any direct City-ICE agreement.
