# ICE's Full Body-Camera Rollout Exposes the Gap Between Capture and Accountable Release

## Summary

As ICE completes agency-wide body camera deployment under a policy giving leadership broad release discretion, a policing-technology op-ed argues programs should be judged not by footage volume but by whether agencies can responsibly review and release it, citing London's Metropolitan Police same-day release reform as a contrasting model.

## Key Points

- ICE promised earlier in 2026 that all its agents would wear body cameras. ICE's policy gives leadership broad discretion over whether footage is released, framed around serving the agency's "best interests" -- legitimate reasons cited include active investigations, operational sensitivity, and protecting the identities of victims, witnesses, and bystanders.
- Author Simon Randall (CEO of body-camera redaction/review vendor Pimloc) argues deployment is "only the first part of the equation"; the harder, less visible work is processing footage so it can be safely and quickly reviewed and released.
- Contrast case: London's Metropolitan Police revised its body-worn video policy in 2026 to allow more proactive release when there's a legitimate policing purpose. After a knife attack in Golders Green, the Met reviewed and released responding officers' footage within hours -- a sharp change from the prior norm of withholding footage until criminal proceedings concluded, sometimes years later. The Met's policy change didn't mean releasing everything; release still requires legal, policy, and operational review, but the starting question shifted from "should this be released?" to "can we make this safe and appropriate to release while it still matters?"
- Adoption context: a 2016 federal census found 47% of US law enforcement agencies had acquired body cameras; by 2020, 68% of sheriffs' offices alone had them. No current single nationwide adoption rate exists, but body cameras have moved from emerging technology to mainstream infrastructure. The National Institute of Justice describes research on body camera impact (complaints, use-of-force reduction) as mixed -- a camera is not inherently an accountability tool; the policy and process around it determine whether it becomes one.
- The practical difficulty: footage is rarely clean -- moving cameras, poor light, crowded frames, people moving in and out of view, faces needing protection. Before release, agencies must determine what's relevant, what needs protecting, and what can stay visible without losing the context that makes a recording meaningful.
- The author's framing: next-generation body-camera programs should be judged on whether an agency can find relevant material quickly under scrutiny and process footage volume without an impossible operational burden -- not solely on officer-equipped counts or hours captured.
