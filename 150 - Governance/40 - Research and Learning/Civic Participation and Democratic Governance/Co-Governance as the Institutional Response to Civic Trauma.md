# Co-Governance as the Institutional Response to Civic Trauma

## Summary

Co-governance -- participatory budgeting, civic assemblies, and co-designed policy solutions -- is the structural mechanism for rebuilding civic trust by making government visibly responsive to the people it harms. The goal is not better outreach; it is shared ownership over decisions.

## Key Points

Co-governance, sometimes called collaborative governance, operates through three main mechanisms: public participatory budgeting processes, civic assemblies, and co-designed policy solutions where people directly harmed by a problem are central to solving it. The premise is that the trust deficit is not a communication problem -- it is a power problem. People need to experience government responding to them before they will invest in it again.

Evidence from practice:
- After community assemblies in Washington state, 90% of participants reported feeling more confident they could influence policies affecting their communities.
- Mamdani's rise in NYC is cited not primarily as a digital organizing story but as a co-governance story: he governed in a way that made people feel the city was working for them. The rent freeze celebration with tenants was a symbol of that orientation.
- Niketa Brar's work in Chicago built the Vote Equity Project, People's Budget Chicago, and introduced public Racial Equity Impact Assessments -- all methods of repairing civic distrust through direct, collaborative governance.

The distinction the article draws is sharp: electoral politics can change who holds power, but co-governance changes how power is exercised. Without the latter, winning elections only defers the trust crisis.

The author chairs Chicago's Equity Advisory Council and leads national co-governance work through For the Many Strategies.

## Relevance

This is the institutional architecture César has been working toward without always naming it. Priority-driven budgeting (co-championed with Nash) is a co-governance tool. The community engagement work around the CVA was co-governance in practice. The FRRN rapid response network was co-governance applied to immigrant protection. The next development opportunity is making these mechanisms more explicit and durable -- a participatory budgeting process, a standing community assembly on housing policy, or a Racial Equity Impact Assessment requirement wired into ordinance review. The Mamdani model is directly applicable to how César can frame his governing approach publicly heading into 2026 midterm cycles and beyond.
