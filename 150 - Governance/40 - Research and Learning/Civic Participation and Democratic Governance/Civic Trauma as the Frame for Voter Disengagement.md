# Civic Trauma as the Frame for Voter Disengagement

## Summary

Voter disengagement is not apathy -- it is civic trauma, the learned hopelessness that accumulates from repeatedly facing a system that actively harms and excludes people. Treating it as apathy produces the wrong diagnosis and the wrong response.

## Key Points

The concept of civic trauma, coined by author Niketa Brar, describes the hopelessness that builds when people repeatedly encounter a system that fails them. It is a learned adaptive response, not indifference. People experiencing civic trauma do not withdraw from all civic life -- research shows non-voters are often deeply engaged in faith communities, protests, and neighborhood organizing. They go where they believe their voice has impact. The ballot is where they have stopped believing.

Key data points:
- Trust in U.S. civic institutions has fallen from its 1958 baseline to just 17% as of 2025 (Pew Research).
- Less than 40% of Americans trust civic institutions even when their preferred party is in power.
- Nearly one-third of the country sat out the 2024 election.
- Non-voters are frequently active in other civic spaces -- they are not disengaged from community, they are disengaged from elections specifically.

The framing has direct political implications: if the problem is trauma, not apathy, then the solution is not a better message or a more charismatic candidate. It requires demonstrating that government actually responds to what people say -- which means governing differently, not campaigning differently. Kamala Harris reaching out to Mamdani after his rise in NYC is cited as a warning: borrowing popularity without adopting the governing philosophy that produced it misses the point entirely.

## Relevance

This frame is essential to César's work. Frederick's District 2 -- majority-minority, 14.9% annual residential mobility, poverty rate 1.5x the county rate -- is exactly the kind of community where civic trauma accumulates. When César shows up to community meetings, passes the City Values Act, or makes the WAVE Fines Act move through committee, he is doing civic repair. Understanding voter disengagement as trauma rather than apathy also shapes how he approaches community engagement: meeting people where trust has already been established (faith communities, cultural organizations, neighborhood networks) rather than defaulting to formal public comment processes that reproduce the systems people have already stopped trusting.
