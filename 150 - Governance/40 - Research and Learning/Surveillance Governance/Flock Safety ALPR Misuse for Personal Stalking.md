# Flock Safety ALPR Misuse for Personal Stalking -- Officer Abuse Cases

## Summary
A former Costa Mesa, California police officer pleaded guilty in April 2026 to using Flock Safety cameras and law enforcement databases to surveil his wife, a mistress, and romantic rivals. Similar cases have surfaced in Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Illinois, Kansas, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, and Wisconsin. Flock Safety acknowledges misuse occurs but argues that permanent audit logs make it detectable. At least 30 cities have ended or canceled Flock contracts since early 2025.

## Key Points
The Costa Mesa case is notable because it involved both Flock ALPR cameras and confidential law enforcement databases -- the officer was charged with both illegal database access and possession of child pornography. The misuse of Flock cameras was not incidental; it was the mechanism for conducting ongoing personal surveillance of specific individuals.

Flock Safety's public response, in a May 2026 blog post, acknowledged that misuse occurs but characterized it as rare and emphasized that permanent audit logs can identify and investigate improper access. The vendor's accountability model is detection after the fact, not prevention.

Thirty cities have ended or canceled Flock contracts since early 2025, per NPR reporting from February 2026. The Stateline article connects those cancellations explicitly to growing concerns about surveillance scope and data sharing, particularly around immigration enforcement.

The pattern of individual misuse is different in kind from the ICE data-sharing concerns -- it demonstrates that the risk surface extends beyond institutional data requests to individual actors using the system for private purposes. Audit logs only help if someone is checking them.

## Relevance
This adds a second documented harm vector to the Flock thread alongside the immigration enforcement and civic chilling effect vectors already in the vault. The stalking cases demonstrate that ALPR misuse is not hypothetical or edge-case -- it is a documented pattern across at least nine states and is severe enough to result in criminal prosecution. For Frederick's procurement governance work, the question is not just whether the City has a use policy, but whether anyone is actively auditing the logs and what the consequence structure is for violations. Together they make the case for active ongoing oversight, not just contract-level disclosure.
