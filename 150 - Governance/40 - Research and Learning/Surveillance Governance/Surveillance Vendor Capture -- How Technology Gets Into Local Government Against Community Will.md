# Surveillance Vendor Capture -- How Technology Gets Into Local Government Against Community Will

## Thesis

Surveillance technology doesn't enter local government through deliberate community choice -- it enters through a vendor playbook designed to minimize democratic friction and create lock-in before public opposition can organize. The Flock Safety case makes this explicit, but the pattern is not unique to Flock.

## The Pattern

The vault already has an extensive Flock file: mission creep from stated traffic purposes to immigration enforcement feed (see [[Flock ALPR Mission Creep -- Traffic Enforcement Beyond Stated Purpose]]), sales demo access to children's spaces without disclosure (see [[Flock ALPR Sales Demo Camera Access -- Children's Spaces Breach]]), and the ACLU tracking national pushback (see [[ACLU -- Growing Municipal Pushback Against Flock ALPR]]). The Bandera, Texas reporting from 404 Media adds the complete lifecycle:

**Entry:** State public safety grants cover installation cost, lowering perceived risk to city. The community doesn't choose the technology; a grant creates the opportunity and the vendor closes it.

**Entrenchment:** Contracts are signed before community opposition can organize. By the time residents show up to meetings, the cameras are already installed.

**Lock-in:** Exit has costs. When Bandera wanted to end its contract, it faced the argument that the city was "giving up free money" plus $15,000 in actual exit costs. The grant that eased entry becomes a moral and financial argument against exit.

**Vendor persistence:** Flock sends former elected officials (a former Cleveland city councilmember) to town halls in towns of 900 people. Flock runs police training on how to argue for the technology at city council meetings. Even when the council votes to end the contract, sympathetic executives can override the vote (Troy, NY: mayor declared state of emergency to continue using cameras after council vote to terminate).

**Scope creep:** [[Flock ALPR Mission Creep -- Traffic Enforcement Beyond Stated Purpose]] documents how ALPR cameras sold for traffic safety end up feeding immigration enforcement pipelines. The stated purpose doesn't survive contact with the institutional demand for surveillance data.

## Why Community Resistance Is Hard

Bandera succeeded -- a rare outcome -- because the community was unusually cohesive, persistent, and willing to take direct action (cutting down camera poles). Even then, it took months of repeated city council meetings, physical destruction of equipment, and a 3-2 vote rather than a mandate.

The Troy, NY case is probably the more common outcome: council votes to end contract, executive overrides. The [[Institutional Deployment Without Consent -- A Cross-Domain Pattern]] note documents this as a cross-domain problem -- institutions deploy on top of communities without consent across surveillance, AI, housing, and urban planning.

## The Frederick Angle

The [[Surveillance Technology Governance -- Frederick Policy Brief]] and [[Municipal Technology Strategy]] notes already flag this as a live issue. The question is whether Frederick has a procurement framework that intercepts the vendor playbook at the entry point rather than after installation.

Specific gaps to address:

**Grant-funded technology triggers the same democratic review as city-funded technology.** The fact that a camera was paid for by a state grant doesn't change its community impact. Procurement policy should require the same community input process regardless of funding source.

**Technology audits before contract signing, not after.** [[Flock Safety in Dunwoody -- When Residents Push Back on Municipal Surveillance]] documents residents discovering their city was used in sales demos without their knowledge. Pre-contract audits and disclosure requirements close this gap.

**Sunset and exit provisions in surveillance contracts.** The Bandera exit cost was the financial argument against termination. Contracts should include clear exit terms, community review triggers, and automatic sunset clauses.

**Scope limitation language.** Every surveillance contract should define its permitted use cases and prohibit data sharing for immigration enforcement or purposes beyond the stated scope.

The [[Municipal Tech Consent Architecture -- Contract Language, Audit Standards, and Resident Notification]] note already proposes concrete contract language. The Bandera case is the empirical case for why that language matters.

## Synthesis

The Flock cases form a complete picture: [[Bandera Texas Cancels Flock Contract After Community Resistance]] shows what successful community resistance looks like and how hard it is. [[Flock Safety -- Corporate Playbook for Selling Surveillance to Local Governments]] names the vendor strategy that makes resistance necessary. The existing vault cluster documents what that strategy looks like at the national scale.

The underlying dynamic is [[Private Infrastructure Dependency and the Accountability Gap in Public Institutions]]: when public services run on private vendor infrastructure, the vendor's interests become embedded in the public institution's operations. Ending a surveillance contract isn't just a policy choice; it's an extraction from a relationship the vendor has made expensive to leave.

The council's technology governance work -- whether on Flock specifically or municipal technology procurement broadly -- needs to start from this structural reality rather than treating each vendor relationship as a neutral technical decision.
