# Agentic Policing and the Answer-First Problem -- AI Inverts the Investigative Process

## Summary
Emerging AI systems described as "agentic policing" platforms would combine body-camera footage, license plate readers, sensor data, case files, and social media records into a unified model capable of generating investigative leads and suspect identifications. The concern from legal scholars is structural: these systems produce an answer first, then require officers and prosecutors to work backward to establish whether the answer is accurate, inverting the traditional investigative model in constitutionally troubling ways.

## Key Points
The traditional investigative process moves from evidence to hypothesis to conclusion. Agentic policing systems invert that: the AI analyzes aggregated data and outputs a probable suspect or connection, and human actors then audit the output. As GWU law professor Andrew Guthrie Ferguson framed it, "We've never started with an answer and made people work backwards."

The legal problems are significant. Evidence produced by AI systems may be difficult to challenge in court if the model's reasoning is opaque. AI errors -- including context misreads, bias in training data, and hallucinated connections -- could propagate into criminal records, arrest decisions, and prosecution. Even where humans retain final authority, the cognitive weight of an AI-generated conclusion shapes how they evaluate alternatives.

This is not a distant future scenario. Current platforms from Axon, Motorola Solutions, Mark43, Flock Safety, Clearview AI, and others already aggregate body camera footage, dispatch records, license plate data, and case files. The agentic layer is the next integration step above tools already deployed in hundreds of agencies. There is no consistent national framework governing any of it; California and Utah have passed laws narrowly regulating generative AI in police report writing, but nothing governs the broader analytical integration.

The report also documents individual misuse: a former Costa Mesa officer pleaded guilty to using Flock Safety cameras and law enforcement databases to surveil his wife, a mistress, and romantic rivals. Similar cases have surfaced in Florida, Georgia, Idaho, Illinois, Kansas, Pennsylvania, Tennessee, and Wisconsin.
