# Surveillance Vendor Resistance to Democratic Exit -- Flock Verona and NOPD as Pattern

## Summary

Two stories from this week reveal a shared pattern: surveillance vendors actively resist municipal democratic exit, whether by asserting equipment ownership rights to block camera removal (Flock/Verona) or by quietly testing weaponization language in policy documents (NOPD/Skydio).

## The Pattern

Both stories this week from 404 Media point at the same underlying dynamic: surveillance technology vendors treat democratic decisions by local governments as obstacles to manage rather than authority to respect.

**In Verona:** After a council vote to end Flock service, the company objected to the city removing its own cameras, claimed uncertainty about whether remote disable was even possible, and sent work orders for "maintenance" rather than removal. The city ended up bagging the cameras in trash bags while waiting for corporate compliance. The democratic vote happened in November; the cameras were finally removed in February.

**In New Orleans:** During the council review of a $250,000 budget expansion for Skydio drones, NOPD briefly published a drone manual allowing weaponized drones. When a civic group noticed and called council members, the manual disappeared and was replaced. The sequence -- testing expanded authority in a document during a budget request, then retreating when noticed -- mirrors the pattern of institutional deployment without consent documented in [[Institutional Deployment Without Consent -- A Cross-Domain Pattern]].

Both cases share:
- Vendors or police departments treating public policy as a ceiling to push against rather than a constraint to accept
- Democratic decisions (a council vote to exit, a public operations manual) overridden or quietly undermined
- Civic vigilance as the mechanism that forced accountability (residents noticing cameras still on; Eye on Surveillance calling council members)
- No consequence for the vendor or department for the evasion attempt

## Implications for Frederick

The surveillance procurement governance work [[Surveillance Technology Governance -- Frederick Policy Brief]] needs to account for these exit and accountability failure modes explicitly:

1. Contract language must specify that the city retains the right to remove vendor equipment immediately upon contract expiration or termination, with vendor cooperation required within a short window.
2. Remote disable capability should be tested and confirmed in writing before any contract is signed -- not asserted by the vendor as a marketing claim.
3. Any internal policy documents related to contracted surveillance technology should be subject to public records request and require council notification if changed.
4. The distinction between non-renewal and cancellation (which Flock used to justify delay) should be explicitly neutralized in contract language.

The civic vigilance point is also worth lifting: in both cases, organized residents and advocacy groups spotted the problem before elected officials did. That argues for investing in a local surveillance watchdog function, whether through the Frederick Rapid Response Network or a technology governance advisory body.
