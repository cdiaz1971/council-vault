# Open Invocation Policies and the Neutrality Trap

## Summary
Councils adopt invocation policies expecting Christian prayer, but the law requires them to be open to everyone, so non-Christian and Satanic speakers follow. Whether that becomes a crisis depends on the chair's procedure, not the speaker's words.

## The Pattern
Two councils adopted invocation policies in 2026 and both had a Satanic Temple member deliver an invocation in early October. In each case the policy was drafted to survive an Establishment Clause challenge, which means it had to be first come, first served and blind to content. The sponsors assumed in practice only Christian clergy would sign up. Both speeches were short, civil, and about reason and autonomy, with "Hail Satan" at the end.

The difference was in how the meeting was run. In Battle Ground the mayor followed the policy, greeted the speaker, and moved on, and nothing happened in the room. The backlash came days later from outside. In Ottumwa the mayor let a pastor interrupt the agenda and then granted a moment of silence as a corrective ritual. That took a side on viewpoint, extended the controversy, and pulled the city into Fox News coverage and angry calls.

## The Trap
An open invocation policy turns the council chair into the referee of religious pluralism at every meeting. The chair can either treat every invocation identically, which angers the constituency that asked for the policy, or respond to some invocations and not others, which creates the legal exposure the policy was designed to avoid. Ottumwa's lone dissenter named this before the vote and was proven right. There is no version where the council gets only the prayers it wanted.

## Implications for Frederick
If an invocation proposal ever comes before the Frederick council, these two cases are the argument. The honest options are no invocation, or a fully open one that the chair will enforce neutrally even when the speaker is unpopular. Either way, the chair's rule should be fixed in advance: objections go to public comment, and no corrective prayer, moment of silence, or rebuttal is offered to one speaker that would not be offered to all. A moment of silence for everyone at every meeting is a neutral alternative that avoids the referee role entirely.

Open questions: whether Frederick has ever used invocations or a moment of silence, and what the city's rules of procedure say about out-of-order interruptions.
