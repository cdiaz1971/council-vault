# Civic Participation and Co-Governance

## Purpose

This page supports Council decisions about how residents take part in City government beyond voting for officials: public comment, Neighborhood Advisory Councils, participatory budgeting, civic assemblies, and the use of AI to process public input. It also covers the City's own election rules, which set who has a formal voice. It separates three things:

- **Outreach:** the City informs residents.
- **Consultation:** the City asks for input and decides.
- **Co-governance:** residents share a defined decision, such as how to spend a set amount of money.

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Civic trauma | Learned distrust from repeated experience of a system that fails or harms people, a term from Niketa Brar | Treats low participation as a response to experience, not apathy; the remedy is visible responsiveness, not better messaging ([[Civic Trauma as the Frame for Voter Disengagement]]) |
| Co-governance | Arrangements where residents share real decisions with government | Distinct from consultation, where government keeps the decision |
| Participatory budgeting (PB) | Residents propose and vote on how to spend a set portion of a budget | The most studied co-governance tool |
| Civic assembly | A randomly selected, demographically balanced group of residents that deliberates and recommends | Reaches people who do not attend public meetings |
| Neighborhood Advisory Council (NAC) | City-recognized neighborhood forums for raising local issues with the City | Frederick's standing neighborhood channel |
| Racial Equity Impact Assessment (REIA) | A structured review of a proposal's effects by race before a vote | Used in Chicago and elsewhere as part of co-governance practice |

## Current baseline

### Trust and participation

- Trust in U.S. civic institutions stood at 17% in 2025, and nearly one-third of eligible Americans did not vote in 2024. People who skip elections are often active in faith communities, protests, and neighborhood organizing ([[Civic Trauma as the Frame for Voter Disengagement]], citing Pew Research and Next City).
- In the City's Council District 2, residential mobility is about 14.9% a year and poverty runs about 1.5 times the County rate, conditions associated with lower participation in formal processes (same source; verify against current ACS data before citing).

### Who can vote in City elections

- Any resident of the City who is at least 18 by the next general election and does not claim a vote elsewhere may register (Charter Art. V, § 1.1(a)). People serving a prison sentence for a felony, people under guardianship with a specific court finding, and people convicted of buying or selling votes cannot (§ 1.1(b)).
- Residents who meet those rules may register with the City Clerk "regardless of citizenship status," and the Clerk keeps a supplemental voter registry separate from the State list (§ 1.2(b), (c); Res. 24-24, September 19, 2024). The Board of Aldermen approved the change 4-1 to take effect with the 2025 City election ([PolitiFact, Oct. 2024](http://politifact.com/article/2024/oct/21/a-maryland-city-has-let-noncitizens-vote-in-local/)). Takoma Park has allowed noncitizen voting in its elections since 1993 (same source).
- Registration and turnout figures for noncitizen voters in 2025: verify with the City Clerk.

### Direct democracy in the Charter

- Any ordinance except the budget or a tax can be petitioned to referendum within 20 days of taking effect, with signatures from 10% of registered voters; the ordinance is suspended until the vote (Charter Art. V, § 18(a)-(c)).
- Council may, by resolution, send any proposed ordinance to the voters (§ 18(e)).

### Neighborhood Advisory Councils

- NACs were created after 2002 community forums as "a forum for residents to become involved in identifying and recommending positive changes to improve their neighborhoods" ([City of Frederick](https://www.cityoffrederickmd.gov/316/Neighborhood-Advisory-Council-NAC)). Planning and Police staff support meetings.
- In 2021 there were 12 NACs with uneven participation: some met monthly, some quarterly, some rarely. An ad hoc committee proposed volunteer liaisons, revised boundaries, City-paid meeting advertising, and online posting of agendas and minutes ([Frederick News-Post, Feb. 2021](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/politics_and_government/health_and_welfare/ideas-offered-for-boundary-structure-changes-for-frederick-neighborhood-advisory-councils/article_71cae960-9c8e-53f0-838c-70190b0545cc.html)). The Mayor has since announced further NAC changes ([Frederick News-Post](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/economy_and_business/real_estate_and_development/mayor-announces-neighborhood-council-changes-city-council-to-consider-more/article_bd05c872-fb0c-530c-bb85-9e5ce9602cd9.html)); current number, boundaries, and structure: verify.
- NAC boundaries are a layer on the City GIS server ([[Frederick City GIS Server -- Query Reference]]).

### Budget participation

- Frederick has no participatory budgeting program. Priority-driven budgeting, which scores programs against community priorities, has been proposed as a reform to the current incremental process ([[Priority-Driven Budgeting -- Frederick Application]]).

## Evidence on co-governance tools

- **Participatory budgeting and turnout.** A 2023 peer-reviewed study of New York City PB found participation raised voter turnout by an average of 8.4 percentage points, with larger effects for young, lower-income, less-educated, and Black voters and for residents who are the minority race in their neighborhood (Johnson, Carlson, and Reynolds, *Political Behavior* 45(1); [summary](https://researchers.kean.edu/en/publications/testing-the-participation-hypothesis-evidence-from-participatory-/)).
- **Scale.** In 2026 more than 93,000 New York residents voted on $30 million through the citywide People's Money program; Cambridge, Massachusetts finished its 12th PB cycle with ballots in ten languages ([[Civic Brief - 051926]]).
- **Civic assemblies.** After community assemblies in Washington State, 90% of participants said they felt more able to influence policy affecting their communities ([[Co-Governance as the Institutional Response to Civic Trauma]]). Self-reported confidence is not the same as policy change.
- **AI for public input.** Hamburg synthesizes public input on planning with AI and shares its tools with other cities; Bogotá ran PB with 50,000 participants in two weeks using AI to process input ([[AI for Participatory Governance -- Listening at Scale as Democratic Infrastructure]]). MIT and New York City Planning used AI to map and group comments for two neighborhood plans and published the results so residents could check them ([[Voice to Vision AI for Participatory Planning Public Comment Analysis]]). California uses AI to supplement, not replace, public comment ([[California AI for Public Comment Analysis -- Supplementing Traditional Civic Engagement]]). In each case the AI removes the logistical bottleneck that makes processing large volumes of input at governing speed infeasible; it does not substitute for the decision itself. The safeguard is that people make the decision, the tool is built for public purpose, and the synthesis is published; the same technology used without those conditions can let a government perform engagement while ignoring what it produces ([[AI for Participatory Governance -- Listening at Scale as Democratic Infrastructure]]).

## Authority map

| Lever | Who controls it | Council role |
|---|---|---|
| Voter qualifications and City elections | City Charter, amended by Council resolution | Direct |
| Referendum rules | Charter Art. V, § 18 | Direct, by Charter amendment |
| Council meeting format and public comment rules | Council rules, within the Open Meetings Act | Direct |
| NAC structure and staffing | Administration; Council by ordinance or budget | Shared |
| Participatory budgeting | Council appropriation and process design | Direct for a set-aside; needs administration to run it |
| Civic assemblies | Council by resolution, with funding | Direct |
| REIA or equity review of legislation | Council by ordinance or rule | Direct |

## Policy options

| Option | Addresses | Cost and effort | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Pilot participatory budgeting with a fixed set-aside, multilingual ballots, and eligibility matching City voter rules | Low participation, distrust | Funds set aside plus staff or a contracted facilitator | Small amounts read as symbolic; projects must be implementable |
| Civic assembly on a defined question, such as housing | Narrow public meeting attendance | Moderate: recruitment, facilitation, stipends | Recommendations ignored deepen distrust |
| NAC reform: published agendas and minutes, City support for outreach, liaisons, updated boundaries | Uneven NAC participation | Low to moderate | Duplication with existing efforts |
| Equity impact review for major legislation | Unseen distributional effects | Low to moderate staff time | Becomes a checkbox without data |
| AI-assisted synthesis of public comment, published for residents to check | Volume of input on major items | Low to moderate | Errors in summary; must not replace reading |
| Keep and publicize hybrid participation in Council meetings | Access for working residents and people with disabilities | Low | Depends on equipment and State rules |
| Outreach for City voter registration, including the supplemental registry | Use of the Charter's voting rules | Low | Political controversy |

## Decision framework

- Is the City informing, consulting, or sharing a decision, and is that clear to residents?
- Who is missing from the current process, and what would reach them?
- What will the City commit to doing with the result?
- Is there money or authority attached, or only input?
- How will residents see what happened to their input?

## Metrics

- Participants in each process, compared with City demographics by age, race, income, language, and Council district.
- City election turnout, including supplemental registry voters.
- Share of PB or assembly recommendations implemented, and time to implementation.
- NAC meetings held per year, by NAC, with agendas and minutes posted.
- Public comment volume and the share of comments addressed in staff reports.

## Council checklist

- [ ] Does the item change who can participate or how?
- [ ] Does the process reach residents who do not attend Council meetings?
- [ ] Is information available in the languages residents use?
- [ ] Is the commitment to act on input stated in advance?
- [ ] Will results be published?
