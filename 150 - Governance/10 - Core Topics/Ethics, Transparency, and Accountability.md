# Ethics, Transparency, and Accountability

## Purpose

This page supports Council decisions about how the City of Frederick polices the conduct of its officials and keeps its own records open. It covers the Ethics Commission and Chapter 21 of the City Code, the Maryland Public Information Act (MPIA) as the City applies it, and Open Meetings Act records. It separates three questions that often get blurred together:

- whether a specific official acted improperly, which is the Commission's job and not Council's;
- whether the process that answers that question is sound, which Council controls through the ordinance, appointments, and budget;
- whether residents and Council can see the City's records, which is governed by state law and City practice.

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Ethics Commission | City body that issues advisory opinions, hears complaints, and administers disclosure and lobbying filings ([City of Frederick](https://www.cityoffrederickmd.gov/130/Ethics-Commission)) | Its findings can carry fines, cease-and-desist orders, and court referral |
| Advisory opinion | Guidance requested by an official before acting | Can later be cited as a defense, so timing and the facts it relied on matter |
| Complaint | Allegation that an official violated Chapter 21 | Moves through intake, preliminary review, investigation, hearing, and decision |
| Prestige of office | Use of a public position for private gain of oneself or another | The provision most often at issue in recent Frederick cases |
| GP 5-807 annual report | State-required yearly report from the Commission to the governing body | Council's main statutory window into how the Commission operates |
| MPIA custodian | The office that holds and must produce a requested record | Determines who controls access to Council's own records |

## Current baseline

### State framework

- Maryland requires every county and municipality to adopt ethics provisions on conflicts of interest, financial disclosure, and lobbying, and to keep conflict and disclosure rules at least equivalent to State law ([GP 5-807](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Laws/StatuteText?article=ggp&section=5-807&enactments=false); [State Ethics Commission](https://ethics.maryland.gov/local-government/)).
- Each local ethics commission must certify compliance to the State Ethics Commission by October 1, meet at least once a year, and send its governing body an annual report with the number of meetings, agendas and minutes, and signed attendance sheets ([GP 5-807](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Laws/StatuteText?article=ggp&section=5-807&enactments=false)).
- Under the MPIA, a custodian must grant or deny a request promptly and within 30 days, and must notify the requester within 10 working days if production will take longer ([GP 4-203](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Laws/StatuteText?article=ggp&section=4-203&enactments=false)).
- Under the Open Meetings Act, agendas must be available before meetings, minutes prepared as soon as practicable, and records kept at least five years ([DLS fiscal note, HB 973 (2026), current-law summary](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/fnotes/bil_0003/hb0973.pdf)).

### How Frederick's ethics system is built

All City Code citations are to Chapter 21 as enacted by Ordinance G-23-21 on December 7, 2023, with no later amendments ([Code of Ordinances](https://library.municode.com/md/frederick/codes/code_of_ordinances)).

- Every Council member and the Mayor is subject to the Ethics Ordinance (Charter § 5.1 and the Mayor's Charter provisions).
- The Commission has five members, all City residents, appointed by the Mayor with the consent of Council for three-year terms; members may serve until a successor is appointed (§ 21-2(a)). Council may remove a member after a hearing for neglect of duty, misconduct, disability, or a violation of the chapter (§ 21-2(f)).
- Council acted on two reappointments in 2026: Kevin Lollar at the March 19 hearing and Phil Bowers, for a term through October 2029, on the October 1 agenda ([[Hearing Brief 2026-03-19]]; [[Hearing Brief 2026-10-01]]). The roster on the City's Commission page is out of date.
- The Commission "shall be advised by the City Attorney, or other legal counsel if deemed appropriate by the Commission or the Mayor" (§ 21-2(a)(5)). The Legal Department answers to the Mayor under the Charter. City Attorney Rachel Gray has said the Mayor is not typically informed of ethics complaints and that outside counsel can be hired when deemed appropriate ([Frederick News-Post, March 19, 2026](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/continuing_coverage/data_centers/nash-files-complaint-alleging-mayor-has-weaponized-frederick-ethics-commission/article_38ceb612-0656-522a-990b-0d3d19d2b0aa.html)).
- The Commission adopts its own Rules of Procedure; the ordinance does not require Council approval of them (§ 21-2(e)).
- **Complaints.** A complaint must be written, under oath, and filed within one year (§ 21-3.1(a)). Staff acknowledge it within 7 days; within 30 days the Commission may refer it to the City Attorney or another investigator for a preliminary investigation; the Commission itself makes the initial determination on jurisdiction and frivolousness within 45 days after that investigation (§ 21-3.1(b), (c)). The primary investigation, by the City Attorney or another investigator, has no deadline; the report is due "within a reasonable time" (§ 21-3.1(e)). All actions are confidential until a final determination (§ 21-3.1(i)).
- **Hearings.** The subject may give statements to the investigator (§ 21-3.1(d)) and is entitled to a hearing "in accordance with any applicable rules of procedure," with findings of fact and conclusions of law (§ 21-3.1(g)). The ordinance does not itself guarantee the subject representation, cross-examination, or access to the evidence. Harassment hearings under the same chapter do: the accused may be represented by any person, testify, present evidence, and cross-examine witnesses (§ 21-10(c)(2)).
- **Penalties.** The Commission may order a violator to cease and desist and impose a fine up to $1,000; lobbying violations carry up to $5,000 each (§ 21-9(a), (b)). At the Commission's request, the City Attorney or other counsel may go to Circuit Court, which may void a conflicted official action challenged within 90 days (not appropriations, taxes, or bonds) and fine up to $5,000 per violation (§ 21-9(c)). A finding of a violation is public (§ 21-9(d)).
- **No appeal.** Chapter 21 gives the subject of a complaint no appeal or judicial review; court action runs only at the Commission's request (§ 21-9(c)).
- **Conflicts.** The conflict rules cover the official's and qualified relatives' interests, with "qualified relative" limited to a spouse, parent, child, or sibling (§ 21-4(a), (b)). They do not address staff whose position reports to the subject of a complaint. Prestige of office is defined in § 21-4(f).
- **Advisory opinions.** The Commission must respond "promptly," based on "the facts provided or reasonably available to it," and must publish opinions with the subject's identity deleted (§ 21-3(b), (c)). Cathy Anderson reports that Rule 4.3 of the Rules of Procedure limits opinions to facts the requester provides and that anonymization has been inconsistent ([[Ethics Commission Rules Narrow What the Ordinance Requires]]; verify against the current Rules).
- **Records.** Financial disclosure statements and lobbying filings are kept by the City Attorney's office for four years as public records (§ 21-5(i), § 21-6(g), § 21-7(e)). Chapter 21 does not restate the State requirement of an annual report to Council; that duty comes from GP 5-807.
- **Practice beyond the text.** Anderson reports that in practice the Legal Department also handles intake, notices, agendas, minutes, and case files; that complaints have been screened in an informal threshold conversation between the chair and the City Attorney rather than by the Commission; and that confidentiality has not prevented the City Attorney from briefing the Mayor ([[Frederick Ethics Process Concentrates Every Function in the Legal Department]]). These describe practice, not the ordinance, and remain her account.

### Recent caseload

- **Complaint against Council Member Nash (data centers).** Nash was notified in December 2025; the Commission held a closed hearing on March 25, 2026, and on May 11, 2026 found violations tied to undisclosed lobbying work, imposing a $1,000 fine and a cease-and-desist order on data center and energy matters ([Frederick News-Post, May 14, 2026](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/politics_and_government/ethics/ethics-report-finds-nash-violated-rules-for-disclosure-on-data-centers-energy-policy/article_999db262-e310-5005-97b6-4ebc4067897d.html)). Outside attorney Leslie Powell investigated. Anderson reports process concerns including a respondent who did not see the complaint before the hearing, exclusion of non-attorney representatives, no recording, and late evidence ([[Nash Ethics Hearing Due Process Gaps]]; her account, based on the case file; verify before public use).
- **Complaints against Mayor O'Connor.** Nash filed a complaint in March 2026 alleging misuse of the Commission against her ([Frederick News-Post, March 19, 2026](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/continuing_coverage/data_centers/nash-files-complaint-alleging-mayor-has-weaponized-frederick-ethics-commission/article_38ceb612-0656-522a-990b-0d3d19d2b0aa.html)) and a second complaint on July 22, 2026 concerning his spouse's nonprofit. Both are pending.
- **Earlier finding.** In 2022 the Commission found a violation by then-Alderwoman Nash on a complaint filed by County Executive Jan Gardner in a personal capacity ([Frederick News-Post](https://news.yahoo.com/nash-seeking-clarity-lobbying-rules-065200447.html)).
- **Advisory opinions.** Five 2026 opinions were posted by September 23. Anderson reports that the two heard first (26-4 and 26-5) were signed months after their hearings, and that the attorney engaged to investigate the complaint against the Mayor is listed as advising on 26-5, an opinion requested by the Mayor ([[Ethics Commission Rules Narrow What the Ordinance Requires]]; checkable against the posted opinions).

### Outside counsel and cost

- Three outside firms have been engaged to advise the Commission at uncapped hourly rates of $250 to $400, and Anderson found no record of Council approving any of them; she reports $81,743.87 paid from a legal services line in FY2026, including a $37,973.84 payment with no vendor listed ([[Ethics Commission Outside Counsel Spending Lacks Council Approval]]; verify with Finance).
- The Legal Department's FY2027 budget is $1,281,615, with $95,000 for outside counsel ([[FY2027 Budget -- Legal Services (1121)]]).

### Records and transparency

- The Legal Department handles MPIA requests for the City and budgets $6,800 for PIA software in FY2027 ([[FY2027 Budget -- Legal Services (1121)]]).
- At the April 8, 2026 budget hearing, a rise in PIA requests was described in the room as reflecting constituent difficulty rather than a systemic issue ([[Budget Notes 2026-04-08]]).
- Anderson reports that the GP 5-807 annual reports for 2022 to 2025 reached her only on the 30-day MPIA deadline, with file metadata showing creation after the request was closed, and that the City's PIA portal does not list Council as a records custodian ([[City Records Surfaced Only Under Public Information Act Pressure]]; verify whether Council received each year's report).
- Anderson also reports that the June 18, 2026 Council meeting download on the City website contained only public comment until the evening she filed a PIA request ([[City Records Surfaced Only Under Public Information Act Pressure]]).

## Authority map

| Lever | Who controls it | Council role |
|---|---|---|
| Content of Chapter 21 (process, deadlines, counsel, recusal, appeal) | City, by ordinance, within State minimums | Direct. Council can amend the ordinance. |
| Commission appointments and removal | Mayor appoints with Council consent; Council may remove after a hearing (§ 21-2(a), (f)) | Direct on consent and removal |
| Rules of Procedure | Commission adopts its own (§ 21-2(e)) | Can require Council approval by ordinance |
| Choice of Commission counsel | City Attorney by default; Commission or Mayor may choose other counsel (§ 21-2(a)(5)) | Can change by ordinance |
| Outside counsel contracts and spending | Mayor's office and Legal; budget appropriation by Council | Budget authority, procurement rules |
| Findings on individual complaints | Commission | None. Council should not weigh in on pending matters. |
| MPIA obligations and deadlines | State law | City sets custodians, staffing, and portal practice |
| Open Meetings Act minimums | State law | City can exceed (recording, posting deadlines) |
| Removal of elected officials | City Charter | Charter amendment by resolution |

## Policy options

| Option | Addresses | Implementing entity | Cost and effort | Risks |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Public work session with the Ethics Commission on process, counsel, and records | Visibility, Council's standing as approving body | Council | Low | Must avoid discussing pending complaints |
| Independent review of the complaint process by someone outside the Mayor's reporting line, with findings returned to Council for a public vote | Structural conflict | Council, contracted reviewer | Moderate (contract) | Reviewer selection becomes its own dispute |
| Independent counsel for the Commission, selected by the Commission and funded through a dedicated line | Advice and investigation by staff who report to a possible subject | Council by ordinance and budget | Moderate, ongoing | Cost; small caseload may not justify a staff position |
| Recusal rule for staff who report to a complaint's subject | Conflict when the Mayor or City Attorney is named | Council by ordinance | Low | Requires a fallback adviser |
| Deadlines for each complaint stage and for signing advisory opinions | Delay, opinions issued after related complaints | Council by ordinance | Low | Deadlines without capacity create missed-deadline findings |
| Written threshold-review rule with a recorded decision | Complaints closed with no record | Council by ordinance | Low | None significant |
| Respondent rights: see the complaint and evidence, present evidence, choose a representative, cross-examine, record the hearing, appeal. § 21-10(c)(2) already grants most of these in harassment hearings | Fairness for any accused official | Council by ordinance | Low to moderate | Longer proceedings |
| Rules of Procedure require Council approval and may not narrow the ordinance | Rules that diverge from Chapter 21 | Council by ordinance | Low | None significant |
| Caps, scopes, and procurement rules for outside ethics counsel; quarterly spending report to Council | Uncapped, unbid engagements | Council by ordinance or budget language | Low | None significant |
| Require the GP 5-807 annual report to be delivered to Council on the record and posted publicly | Council not receiving its statutory report | Council by ordinance or resolution | Low | None |
| Complete-recording policy for official meetings, and Council listed as a custodian in the PIA portal | Control of Council's own record | Council direction to Clerk and Legal | Low | None |
| Removal-from-office Charter amendment | Accountability of elected officials | Council by Charter resolution | Moderate | Inherits every weakness of the complaint process it relies on |

## Decision framework

- Does the proposal change the process for everyone, or does it read as a response to one pending case?
- Who advises the Commission when the Mayor, the City Attorney, or a Council member is the subject?
- What deadline applies at each stage, and what happens when it is missed?
- What rights does an accused official have to see the evidence and respond?
- Is there a path to appeal?
- Who approves outside counsel, under what cap and scope, and how is spending reported?
- Will Council and the public receive the GP 5-807 report every year without asking?
- If a removal mechanism is proposed, which complaint process does it rely on, and has that process been fixed first?

## Metrics

- Days from complaint filing to final disposition, by stage.
- Days from advisory-opinion hearing to signed opinion.
- Outside counsel spending per matter and per year.
- Share of Commission meetings with agendas posted in advance and minutes posted after approval.
- GP 5-807 report delivered to Council by October 1, each year.
- MPIA median response time and share answered within 30 days.
- Official meeting recordings published complete, as a share of all meetings.

## Council checklist

- [ ] Is the matter pending? If so, limit discussion to process.
- [ ] Has the Chapter 21 section been checked against the current code text?
- [ ] Is the recusal and counsel arrangement clear for the subject involved?
- [ ] Are deadlines, respondent rights, and appeal addressed?
- [ ] Are outside counsel costs capped, scoped, and reported?
- [ ] Has Council received this year's GP 5-807 report?
- [ ] Does any removal proposal rest on a reformed process?

## Comparable models

- **Frederick County.** Seven members and one alternate, appointed by the County Executive and confirmed by the County Council; the County Attorney's Office is the Commission's legal adviser by law; complaints are expected to be decided within 90 days ([Frederick County Ethics Commission](https://frederickcountymd.gov/1722/Ethics-Commission-Information)).
- **Montgomery County.** The Ethics Commission employs its own Chief Counsel, selected by the Commission through competitive recruitment, and manages its own budget and staffing apart from the County Attorney ([2024 Annual Report](https://www.montgomerycountymd.gov/Ethics/Resources/Files/pdfs/Annual%20Report%20for%20Calendar%20Year%202024.pdf)).
