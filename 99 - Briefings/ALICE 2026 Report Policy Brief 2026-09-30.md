# ALICE 2026 Report Policy Brief 2026-09-30

## Executive Summary

The 2026 ALICE report, built on 2024 Census data, shows financial hardship in Maryland holding flat at 39% of households, and Frederick County easing from 33.1% to 31.8%. The City of Frederick did not improve. It held at 43% below the ALICE Threshold while the number of struggling City households grew by about 500. The cost of basics rose far faster than the hardship rate suggests: the Frederick County family survival budget rose $9,492 in one year to $121,080, driven by a $698 a month (39%) jump in child care to $2,500, which now exceeds housing ($2,250) as the single largest cost for a young family. The agenda's direction holds up. Five adjustments are warranted, the most important being child care priority and program eligibility set at the ALICE line instead of the poverty line. The vault's "45% of City households" figure is the Frederick County Election District 2 number (a county subdivision, not City Council District 2), not the City's, and should be corrected to 43%.

One caution frames everything below. The data stops at December 2024. It does not capture the 2025 federal SNAP and Medicaid changes, the Maryland Child Care Scholarship freeze, or 2025 and 2026 rent increases. A flat 2024 is a baseline, not a trend toward recovery.

---

## 1. What the report measures and what changed in method

ALICE (Asset Limited, Income Constrained, Employed) counts households above the Federal Poverty Level but below a county-specific Household Survival Budget covering housing, child care, food, transportation, health care, technology, and taxes. "Below the ALICE Threshold" means ALICE plus poverty combined.

Method notes that affect year-to-year comparison:

- **County figures are one-year ACS estimates** (Frederick County has more than 65,000 people). The report itself warns that single-year changes should be read with caution and that multi-year trends are more reliable.
- **City, election district, and ZIP figures are five-year ACS estimates.** The "2024" City figure is the 2020 to 2024 average, and the "2023" figure is 2019 to 2023. Four of five years overlap, so the City number moves slowly by design.
- **Child care** now counts school-age children as 5 to 17 (previously 5 to 12). This does not affect the headline family of four, which has an infant and a preschooler, so the child care jump in that budget is a real price change in MSDE rate data.
- **Technology** now averages three major carriers plus three low-cost carriers, sourced to Consumer Reports and the FCC. Part of the technology increase is a source change.
- **Occupation data** now comes from ACS microdata instead of BLS. The report says the occupation table is not directly comparable to prior years.

**Which "District 2" this is.** The row above is Frederick County Election District 2 (GEOID 2402190132), a county subdivision that contains the City and surrounding areas. It is not City Council District 2, which César represents. United For ALICE does not publish data for City Council districts. Council District 2 figures would have to be built from census tract data, which United For ALICE publishes through its mapping tool.

What it means:

1. **The City-County gap persists at about 11 points** (42.7% vs. 31.8%). The County's improvement is happening outside the City.
2. **More City households are struggling, not fewer.** The rate held because households grew, but 506 more City households were below the threshold, and the City's poverty count rose while the County's fell.
3. **City poverty is rising against the county trend.** Poverty households in the City rose 227 while countywide they fell 356. That means poverty outside the City fell by roughly 583.

Frederick-specific points:

- **Child care rose 39% ($698 a month) in one year**, and at $2,500 now exceeds housing by $250 a month. In 2023 housing ($2,107) still exceeded child care ($1,802).
- **Housing rose about 7% locally**, less than the 14 to 15% statewide average, but from a much higher base. A single adult's Frederick housing cost ($1,949) is 35% above the state average ($1,440).
- **The ALICE gap against 200% of FPL** is $20,748 for a single adult, $58,680 for a family of four, and $24,660 for a single senior. This is the amount by which a program capped at 200% FPL falls short of the cost of living here.
- County median household income rose from $114,360 to $122,059. Rising median income alongside a flat City hardship rate is the distributional story: gains are not reaching the City's lower-income households.

| Housing | $1,251 | $1,440 | +15% | $1,615 | $1,845 | +14% |
| Child care | $0 | $0 | | $1,538 | **$2,164** | **+41%** |
| Food | $514 | $515 | 0% | $1,394 | $1,397 | 0% |
| Transportation | $414 | $373 | −10% | $910 | $800 | −12% |

| Taxes | $565 | $605 | +7% | $1,113 | $1,263 | +13% |
| **Annual total** | **$39,552** | **$42,120** | **+6.5%** | **$98,136** | **$108,192** | **+10.2%** |
| Hourly wage needed | $19.78 | $21.06 | | $49.07 | $54.10 | |

The 65+ single adult budget rose from $43,872 to $46,164 (+5.2%), with housing up the same 15% and health care down from $580 to $537.

Three things stand out:

- **Child care overtook housing.** In 2023 housing ($1,615) was still larger than child care ($1,538) for a young family. In 2024 child care ($2,164) is $319 a month more than rent. This reverses the vault's current statement, which says child care already exceeded housing in 2023. It did not then. It does now.
- **Housing rose 14 to 15% in one year.** This is HUD Fair Market Rent data, so it tracks market rents directly.
- **Transportation and health care fell.** Gas prices and insurance premium inputs dropped. These declines partially mask the housing and child care increases in the total.

The ALICE Essentials Index, which tracks only the cost of basics, rose 5.5% a year in Maryland from 2021 to 2024, nearly double its 2.8% pre-pandemic pace.

### 3.2 Housing burden

| Measure (Maryland, households below threshold) | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Renters paying 30% or more on housing | 73% | 73% |
| Renters paying 50% or more | 45% | 44% |
| Homeowners paying 30% or more | 59% | 60% |

Burden did not ease. Nearly half of struggling renters give up half their income to rent.

### 3.3 Who is struggling (Maryland)

| Group | 2023 | 2024 |
|---|---|---|
| Householder under 25 | 73% | 71% |
| Householder 65 and over | 48% | 47% |
| Black households | 49% | 49% |
| Hispanic households | 47% | 45% |
| American Indian/Alaska Native | 53% | 55% |
| White, non-Hispanic | 33% | 32% |
| Single mothers with children | 70% | 69% |
| Single fathers with children | 56% | 57% |
| Single or cohabiting under 65, no children | 38% | 37% |

Rates were mostly flat. Two points matter for Frederick:

- **Seniors are the growth story.** Households headed by someone 65 or older grew 55% from 2010 to 2024, faster than any age group, and saw the largest increase in the number of ALICE households. Nearly half are below the threshold.
- **Black hardship grew in absolute terms.** The report says Black households below the threshold increased from 2021 to 2024 while White households held flat. The Hispanic count rose through 2023 and dipped in 2024. The Hispanic dip should be read cautiously given ACS response rates among immigrant households, and it predates 2025 enforcement activity.

### 3.4 Work and wages

- 44% of Maryland full-time workers did not earn enough to support one adult and one school-age child.
- $20 an hour was not enough to support that budget in any Maryland county.
- Part-time workers were twice as likely as full-time workers to be below the threshold (38% vs. 18%).
- Only about 40% of employed Marylanders had a salaried full-time job.
- Among the 20 most common occupations, 26% of workers were below the threshold. Construction laborers (56%), cashiers (50%), nursing assistants (50%), cooks (43%), and material movers (42%) led the list. Accommodation and Food Services was the hardest-hit sector at 41%.
- Maryland's minimum wage reached $15.00 in 2024, and the ALICE rate still did not move.

### 3.5 Savings and the benefits gap

- 35% of households below the threshold had three months of emergency savings (up from 32%), against 71% above it. 40% had retirement assets (up from 38%), against 88%.
- The family of four needed $108,192 but lost federal EITC eligibility at $62,688. The 2024 FPL was $31,200. Any City program keyed to the poverty line or to 200% of it misses most ALICE families.

## 4. Implications for the Affordability Agenda

The six items were selected against the 2023 data. Measured against 2024, the agenda's direction holds: housing costs rose sharply, burden did not ease, and the City's hardship concentration is unchanged. The changes below adjust priority, eligibility, and framing. None removes an item.

### Change 1. Raise the priority of Item 6, Child Care Supply LMC Amendment

**Why.** Child care in Frederick County jumped 39% in a single year to $2,500 a month (41% statewide) and is now the largest cost in a young family's budget, ahead of housing. Item 6 is currently the last-funded item: the ordinance votes in June 2027 and the reserved-slot pilot waits for the FY2029 budget (June 2028). That sequencing was set when child care was the second-largest cost.

**Recommendation.** Keep the LMC amendment on its Planning Commission track, since that timeline is procedural, but move pilot design into FY2028. Options:

| Option | What it does | Tradeoff |
|---|---|---|
| A. Keep FY2029 pilot | No change | Two more budget cycles of 40%-scale cost growth before any price relief |
| B. FY2028 planning appropriation (recommended) | Fund pilot design, provider outreach, and a small first cohort (about 25 slots) in FY2028 | Competes with the housing fund and water credit for the same May 15 to June 20, 2027 window |
| C. Full FY2028 pilot (50 to 100 slots) | Price relief a year earlier | Estimated $600,000 to $1.8 million a year; risks crowding out Items 3 and 4 in a veto fight |

Option B keeps the FY2028 budget defensible while signaling that the Council has read the new data. The October 2026 data request to Frederick County on scholarship waitlist and slot capacity within City limits becomes more urgent under any option.

**Also correct** the Item 6 note, which says the $1,538 child care cost exceeds housing. Update to the 2024 Frederick figures ($2,500 vs. $2,250).

### Change 2. Key program eligibility to the ALICE line, not the poverty line (Item 4, and any new program)

**Why.** Item 4's low-income water credit covers households on SNAP, Medicaid, or energy assistance, or at or below 200% FPL, which is $62,400 for a family of four and $30,120 for a single adult. The Frederick County family survival budget is $121,080, a gap of $58,680. The credit as drafted reaches poverty households and the lowest-income ALICE households, but not most of the 11,136 ALICE households in the City.

**Recommendation.** Keep the categorical eligibility (single application through HHS and Frederick Community Action Agency), and consider a second, smaller tier for households up to the Frederick County ALICE Threshold. Cost scales with enrollment, so this is a budget question for the FY2028 window, and Finance's water account data (already requested in October) should include an estimate of households in each tier. The same principle should carry into the housing fund's authorized uses under Item 3 and the child care pilot under Change 1, which already names "ALICE-threshold families."

This is the report's core policy lesson for local government: federal eligibility lines stop well short of the cost of living, and the City is one of the few actors that can set its own line.

### Change 3. Add seniors explicitly to the agenda's frame (Items 4 and 5)

**Why.** Seniors are the fastest-growing household group statewide and saw the largest increase in ALICE households. In Frederick County, 44% of senior households are below the threshold, the only age group whose rate rose, and at 12,230 households they are now the largest age group below it. The agenda currently has little aimed at them, and the only senior reference is the APFO exemption for age-restricted housing, which Item 3 argues is inequitable.

**Recommendation.** No new item. Two targeted edits:

- **Item 4 (water).** Env. § 9-202 speaks of homeowners. That limitation, flagged as a problem for tenants, is an opportunity for senior homeowners on fixed incomes. Name 65+ homeowners as a priority enrollment group for the credit.
- **Item 5 (missing middle).** Frame duplexes and Nash's ADU ordinance in part as aging-in-place tools, which broadens the coalition for the slowest and most contested item.

A NOAH or senior property tax relief measure remains a phase 2 candidate under Charter Art. VII, § 10(d).

### Change 4. Sharpen the housing items with the new cost data (Items 1, 2, 3)

**Why.** Housing cost rose about 7% in Frederick in one year (14 to 15% statewide), from a base already 35% above the state average, and 44% of struggling Maryland renters pay half their income on housing. Nothing in the data suggests changing these items. The data strengthens them.

**Recommendation.** Update the talking points and item notes:

- **Item 1 (Real Rent).** A single adult now needs $1,949 a month for housing in Frederick, up $132 in one year. That is the argument for the algorithmic rent-setting ban and for making advertised rent the real rent.
- **Item 2 (production accountability).** Require the annual October 1 report to include the City's ALICE rate and count from the most recent data sheet, alongside production figures. It costs nothing and ties production to outcomes.
- **Item 3 (MPDU and fund).** The rental MPDU tiers (60, 70, 80% FCAMI) sit near the ALICE line. Use the new data to support the deeper-affordability option (Option C in the item note).

### Change 5. Add one administrative action to Section 8 of the agenda

**Why.** The report identifies a low-cost gap the City can address without legislation.

- **EITC and VITA outreach.** About 20% of eligible taxpayers do not claim the EITC, and Maryland's state EITC is refundable at 50% of federal. A City push through HHS, Frederick Community Action Agency, and the utility billing insert before the 2027 tax season is nearly free.

The vault's 72% of children in rental units and the veteran gap are 2023 figures. The 2026 county report does not update them, and the City is not commissioning additional data from United Way, so cite them as 2023 figures.

### What does not change

- **Transportation.** Costs fell 10 to 12%. The agenda's decision to leave transit to the County and use parking reform (Item 5) stands.
- **Technology.** The 27 to 31% increase is partly a method change. Broadband is not strong enough to displace an existing item, but the tiering note's franchise and right-of-way lever remains a phase 2 candidate.
- **Wages.** 44% of full-time workers cannot support one child, and a $15 minimum wage did not move the rate. Wage policy is primarily a State and County lever and stays outside this agenda. The collective bargaining work for City employees is the Council's own wage lever and is tracked separately.
- **Excluded items.** Nothing in the new data changes the reasoning for leaving out rent stabilization, good cause eviction, source-of-income, municipal ID, or the rental licensing rewrite.

## 5. Corrections to the vault record

1. **City ALICE rate is 43%, not 45%.** The data sheet reports the City of Frederick (Census place) at 43.0% for the 2019 to 2023 period and 42.7% for 2020 to 2024. The 45% figure matches Frederick County Election District 2, a county subdivision that is not City Council District 2 and is a larger geography (about 36,000 households vs. 33,600 in the City). The 45% appears across the vault, including the agenda hub, Cesar Context, the ALICE reference note, and talking points. United Way of Frederick County's 2026 county report also shows the City at 43%. The City was never at 45% in either data year.
2. **County rate is now 32% (31.8%), not 33%.** Single mothers are 75%, not 76%.
3. **Child care did not exceed housing in 2023.** Several notes say it did, using the $1,538 state figure. In 2023 housing exceeded child care both statewide ($1,615 vs. $1,538) and in Frederick ($2,107 vs. $1,802). With 2024 data, child care now exceeds housing in Frederick ($2,500 vs. $2,250) and statewide ($2,164 vs. $1,845).
4. **Maryland savings figures.** The 2024 SHED figures are 35% with emergency savings and 40% with retirement assets for households below the threshold (previously 32% and 38%).

Per house rules, these should be corrected in place in each note, not appended.
