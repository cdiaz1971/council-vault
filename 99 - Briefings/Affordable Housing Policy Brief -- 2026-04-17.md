# Affordable Housing Policy Brief -- City of Frederick
**Prepared for:** Council President César Díaz
**Date:** April 17, 2026
**Sources:** 28 vault notes across 10 - Council (root, 100 - Housing, 195 - Political People), 50 - Research, plus 7 recent daily notes
**Scope:** What Frederick knows about affordable housing -- production, preservation, financing, political dynamics, and peer city models -- and what the vault reveals collectively about gaps and next moves.

---

## Executive Summary

Frederick faces a documented shortage of 8,133 housing units, a production target resolution that has been deferred twice, and a budget cycle that ends May 14 as the last leverage point for FY2027. The vault contains a sophisticated body of knowledge on supply-side tools, financing mechanisms, peer city models, and state legislative dynamics -- but the gap between what Frederick knows and what Frederick has actually enacted is wide. The central tension running through every housing note is the same: the tools exist, the research is solid, the political will is the variable. On the supply side, LMC reform and parking minimum elimination are ready for action but have no formal timeline. On the financing side, the MPDU payment-in-lieu fee is the clearest near-term legislative lever and it remains uncalibrated and underused. On the preservation side, the vault argues that durability is affordability policy -- and Frederick has no preservation strategy. State preemption via the Maryland Housing Certainty Act is now headed to the Governor's desk, which resets Frederick's window for proactive action.

---

## I. The Production Problem -- What the Numbers Actually Say

The vault's baseline document for this analysis is [[Housing Production Target Memo]], which establishes the core math: Frederick needs 1,400 units per year through 2035 to meet its Housing Needs Assessment projection of 13,774 units by 2035, with 8,133 cost-burdened households earning below $75,000 annually as the affordability pressure point. The 15% affordability baseline in the production resolution -- with 33% of affordable units at or below 60% AMI -- is well-researched and aligns with regional inclusionary housing norms from Montgomery County and Fairfax County.

The [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] documents that this resolution has been deferred twice as of March 20, 2026. The housing study is being used as a potential justification for further delay, but the vault flags the critical question: is the housing study release a genuine prerequisite or a deferral mechanism? The April 9 H&HS budget session and May 14 budget deadline are the remaining leverage points in FY2027 to push the production conversation forward.

What [[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]] establishes, and what the broader housing note corpus confirms, is that Frederick's missing middle zoning gap is structural and unaddressed. R4 requires an 8,000 sq ft minimum lot; R6 requires 7,000 sq ft. Both permit only single-family detached. No ordinance extending duplex, townhouse, or triplex permissions to these zones has been introduced. The [[Maryland Housing Certainty Act -- Developer Vesting Rights 2026]] passed the House 124-7 and the Senate 34-12 and is now headed to Governor Moore's signature -- it will lock in approved applications under current zoning for at least five years. If Frederick's LMC reform produces upzoning in R4 and R6 after the Act takes effect in October 2026, that reform cannot reach projects that filed complete applications before the new code is in place. The window for proactive LMC action ahead of state vesting rights is now.

[[Housing -- Five Simple Zoning Changes for Affordability]] reinforces the specific interventions that research supports: removing minimum lot size requirements, allowing missing middle typologies by right, reducing parking minimums, streamlining discretionary review, and using density bonuses as the primary affordability lever. These are not speculative -- they are the documented supply-side toolkit and each one has a direct LMC analog.

## II. Parking Minimums -- The Clearest Unforced Error

[[Parking Minimums as a Housing Cost Driver]] is the most technically complete note in the housing corpus and deserves direct attention. A single surface parking space costs roughly $10,000 to construct; a structured garage space runs $40,000 or more. These costs are passed through to tenants and buyers. For affordable housing, childcare centers, and small commercial tenants, mandatory parking requirements can make otherwise viable projects infeasible.

Washington State's 2025 parking reform (SB 5184) is the vault's recommended model because it sidesteps the transit-proximity debate -- which would be politically complicated in Frederick given MARC service limitations -- and instead exempts specific use cases: homes under 1,200 sq ft, commercial spaces under 3,000 sq ft, childcare centers, subsidized affordable housing, and senior housing. These are politically defensible cases that frame the issue correctly. Developers retain the freedom to build as much parking as the market demands; what changes is who decides.

This matters directly for the Himes Ave project documented in [[Himes Ave -- Conifer LIHTC Development Meeting Notes (2026-03-16)]]. Conifer is seeking a reduction from 1.5 to 1.25 spaces per unit to maximize unit count at 145 units. That variance request is the visible edge of a code problem that affects every affordable project in the pipeline. A proactive parking minimum reform would eliminate the need for variances on projects like Himes Ave and improve financial feasibility across the board.

## III. The LIHTC Problem -- Units That Don't Serve Who They're Meant To

[[LIHTC -- Structural Tension Between Tax Credit Program and Tenant Housing Security]] is a critical analytical note that the vault treats well. LIHTC is the primary federal affordable housing production tool at $13.5 billion annually, but its design produces an internal contradiction: rents are calculated as a percentage of AMI (typically 50-60% AMI), not based on tenants' actual incomes. Nearly half of all LIHTC tenants have incomes at or below 30% AMI. This means roughly 37.5% of LIHTC tenants are rent-burdened even in units that count as "affordable."

The implications for Frederick are direct. The Himes Ave project (145 units, Conifer, LIHTC financing, 2028 timeline) is a LIHTC project. Units will be counted as affordable for reporting purposes. Whether they are affordable for the lowest-income residents in District 2 -- the ones most in need -- depends entirely on whether the financing includes any deeper subsidy to serve households below 30% AMI. That question is not addressed in the meeting notes and belongs on the list of follow-up items for when the LIHTC application moves forward.

The California study cited in the note found that only 15% of LIHTC units were priced affordable to extremely low-income households, despite ELI households comprising the majority of LIHTC tenants. Frederick should not assume that "LIHTC affordable" means "affordable to our most cost-burdened residents."

## IV. The Financing Gap -- What Frederick's MPDU System Actually Produces

[[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]] provides the most direct analysis of Frederick's financing gap. The $2.00 per square foot payment-in-lieu fee is structurally low, not calibrated to actual affordability gaps, and not scheduled for recalculation until late 2026 at the earliest under the three-year review trigger. The fee pool has reportedly grown to approximately $6M -- but that figure came from a primary campaign context and should be independently confirmed.

Baltimore City's funded inclusionary zoning program (Ordinance 24-308, operational July 21, 2024) is the direct peer comparison. Baltimore's innovation is a property tax credit equal to the actual forgone rent revenue for each inclusionary unit, calculated annually against the difference between market rent and the affordable rent charged. The Sightline Institute analysis identifies Baltimore as one of only three jurisdictions in the country with a genuinely funded IZ program -- meaning developers are not being asked to absorb below-market rent as an unfunded cost. Frederick's current payment-in-lieu model is functionally the opposite: it is an escape valve that generates nominal revenue and produces minimal units. The MPDU baseline rate of 12.5% is also below the 15% production target in the Housing Production Target Resolution -- closing that gap requires a Chapter 19 amendment that has not been initiated.

The [[FY2027 Budget -- Housing and Human Services April 9 Session]] note is the right pressure point for these questions. The April 9 session is the opportunity to press on what $12.6M in H&HS spending actually delivers against the 8,133-unit shortage, whether Nash's $250k affordable housing RFP is oriented toward production or preservation, and what the City Housing Fund actually contains in FY2027.

## V. Preservation -- The Side of Housing Policy Frederick Hasn't Started

[[Housing Preservation -- Durability as Affordability Policy]] makes an argument the vault has not yet integrated into Frederick's active policy work: in older cities, most affordable housing already exists in the existing building stock, not in new construction pipelines. Keeping that stock structurally sound is a housing policy decision. The note specifically connects this to the April 9 budget session and asks whether the $250k affordable housing RFP has a preservation component -- or whether it is oriented toward new production only.

The Golden Mile corridor is the direct Frederick application. Older housing stock along the Golden Mile and adjacent corridors represents naturally occurring affordable housing (NOAH) that is at risk if not actively preserved. [[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]] identifies a CLT partnership as the strongest long-term preservation tool -- the College Park Community Preservation Trust ($14.9M, nine homes acquired and sold under permanent affordability covenants) is the closest peer model -- but acknowledges that Frederick has no CLT relationship, no city-CLT partnership, and no public land disposition policy. The vault is clear that this is a gap, not a work in progress.

## VI. Peer City Models Worth Tracking

The vault contains several peer city analyses worth synthesizing:

[[Portland Housing Production Strategy -- 35-Action Six-Year Plan]] is the most complete legislative model in the vault. Oregon law requires cities to complete a Housing Needs Analysis every six years and adopt a Housing Production Strategy within one year. Portland's 35-action framework organized across eight categories -- with explicit cross-bureau coordination directives, equity and anti-displacement goals integrated alongside production targets, and a separate social housing study as a parallel track -- is the direct structural template for what Frederick's deferred production resolution could eventually become.

[[Camden County Shared Housing -- Tackling Homelessness Without New Funding]] offers the clearest no-new-money model for Frederick's homelessness response. Camden County used a shared housing pilot -- funded from its existing Homeless Trust Fund -- to build interagency capacity without waiting for new federal or state dollars. The model: government as catalyst, nonprofits as implementers, master leasing as the mechanism for highest-acuity populations. The question for Frederick is whether the city's housing trust fund has flexible enough terms to seed a landlord incentive pool, and which local nonprofits have master lessee capacity.

[[Housing -- Faith-Owned Land as Infill Housing Opportunity]] identifies a supply source Frederick has not addressed. Faith institutions hold significant underutilized land in Frederick -- excess parking, vacant side lots, unused fields -- that could accommodate infill housing without requiring LMC reform or subsidy. A 0.15-acre lot can accommodate 5 units; a 0.31-acre double lot can accommodate 11 units with a shared courtyard. A targeted outreach strategy to Frederick congregations, framed around their own mission, could unlock supply that bypasses the LMC debate entirely.

[[Housing -- School-to-Housing Adaptive Reuse]] documents that school conversions are now the fastest-growing sector of adaptive reuse nationally, driven by enrollment declines. Atlanta's model -- district as developer, teacher-priority affordable units -- is directly applicable if Frederick City Schools closes or consolidates buildings. The city has standing to advocate for housing reuse over commercial sale on surplus school sites.

## VII. ICE Enforcement as a Housing Crisis

[[ICE Enforcement Chilling Effect -- Economic and Housing Disruption]] belongs in any honest affordable housing brief for District 2. In Minnesota, Operation Metro Surge drove tens of thousands of immigrant households to stop working, triggering over 2,400 eviction filings in Minneapolis and St. Paul in the first weeks of 2026 and an estimated $47 million in lost wages. The same dynamic is documented in Memphis under the National Guard-backed enforcement operation. Frederick County's ICE-related fear is documented in WYPR reporting from March 2026. Housing instability from enforcement fear falls on low-income renters regardless of immigration status -- it is a housing policy issue, not only an immigration policy issue. The City Values Act and the 287(g) question are not separate from the housing production agenda; they are part of it.

## VIII. Political Context

The political landscape on housing is complicated and the vault documents it in detail. [[Michael O'Connor]] is active on housing rhetoric but the vault identifies a structural accountability gap: the MPDU fee pool has reportedly grown to $6M with limited production to show. The production target resolution has been deferred twice. His FY26 budget veto restored $2M in housing subsidies but the vault does not document what those subsidies produced.

[[Katie Nash]] has proposed targeted development moratoria for areas undergoing rezoning -- framed as responsible growth -- and championed pedestrian safety and housing in her prior council role. The vault does not document her specific position on the production target resolution, the MPDU fee reform, or LMC parking minimum changes. This is an intelligence gap worth closing before the May 14 budget deadline.

The [[Maryland Housing Certainty Act -- Developer Vesting Rights 2026]] note flags that [[Libby Taylor]]'s proposed LMC moratorium on new building takes on different weight if state law simultaneously locks in vested rights on applications already submitted. A moratorium that freezes new applications while the old ones vest under current code could be the worst of both worlds -- no new supply under the new code, and no leverage over projects already in the pipeline under the old one.

## IX. Information Gaps and Research Priorities

### Critical Gaps

Frederick's DHCD-published housing production target has not been independently confirmed and calibrated against the 1,400-unit-per-year baseline. Until that number is confirmed, the production resolution is arguing to a target that may not match the state's published figure for Frederick. This is an urgent verification task.

The City Housing Fund balance is unconfirmed. The fund exists in §19-18 with broad authorized uses, but capitalized only through payment-in-lieu fees and MPDU foreclosure proceeds. Whether there is $1M or $6M in the fund matters for the April 9 budget session and for any preservation or CLT strategy.

The MPDU payment-in-lieu fee recalculation timeline is documented as "late 2026" under the three-year review trigger, but the specific trigger date and who controls the process is not in the vault. If the review can be accelerated, it is the clearest near-term legislative lever.

### Significant Gaps

No notes exist yet on faith-based housing partnerships in Frederick. The vault identifies faith-owned land as a significant untapped supply source but there is no documentation of outreach, interest, or relationships with local congregations.

The vault has no note on the current status of Frederick's Continuum of Care funding and what the HUD homelessness grant ruling (documented in [[Federal Housing Policy -- HUD Homelessness Grant Rules Struck Down]]) means for Frederick's service providers specifically. The sanctuary jurisdiction restriction in the original HUD NOFO was directly relevant to the City Values Act -- what is the current exposure?

### Contextual Gaps

No analysis exists of how the MARC Brunswick Line's service frequency compares to the Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act's qualifying threshold. If Frederick is within the bill's coverage zone, the bill eliminates local parking minimums for TOD projects and mandates mixed-use development near qualifying stations -- which affects the LMC reform sequence. Legal needs to confirm this analysis before October 1, 2026.

## X. Recommended Next Steps

**Immediate (before May 14 budget deadline):**
- Confirm Frederick's DHCD-published housing production target and align or adjust the production resolution accordingly
- Press at the April 9 H&HS session: City Housing Fund balance, $250k RFP scope (production vs. preservation), and what $12.6M delivers against the 8,133-unit shortage
- Confirm independently whether the MPDU fee pool is approximately $6M and when the §19-6.1 three-year recalculation trigger falls
- Get city attorney analysis on whether Libby Taylor's LMC moratorium proposal conflicts with the Housing Certainty Act vesting rights framework before taking a public position

**Short-term (before October 1, 2026 Housing Certainty Act effective date):**
- Direct staff or Legal to assess current development agreement language against the Housing Certainty Act's early vesting provisions
- Identify whether a proactive LMC amendment in R4 and R6 permitting townhouses by right can be introduced before October 1
- Initiate parking minimum reform using the Washington State use-case model (affordable housing, childcare, small commercial, homes under 1,200 sq ft) -- framed as a production tool, not a parking debate
- Confirm MARC frequency analysis under the Transit and Housing Opportunity Act

**Medium-term (FY2027 legislative agenda):**
- Initiate Chapter 19 MPDU amendment to raise the baseline from 12.5% to 15% and recalibrate the payment-in-lieu fee against actual affordability gaps, using Baltimore's tax credit model as the benchmark
- Develop a preservation track -- begin with a Golden Mile NOAH inventory and assess CLT partnership feasibility using the College Park model
- Develop a faith-based housing outreach strategy targeting congregations with underutilized land; frame around mission alignment, not rezoning
- Follow Himes Ave LIHTC application for ELI unit depth -- confirm whether any units serve households below 30% AMI
