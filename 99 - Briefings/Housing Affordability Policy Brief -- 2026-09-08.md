# Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08

## Executive Summary

Frederick's affordable housing problem is not primarily a shortage of political will or a shortage of developer interest. It is a rulebook that makes the cheapest housing types illegal or unbuildable, a permitting process that takes 18 to 24 months, and a housing fund capitalized by a fee set at roughly three percent of the actual subsidy gap. All three are within council authority to change.

The documented need is 13,774 units countywide by 2035 and an 8,133-unit shortage inside the city. The Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force projects 4,000 units over ten years if every one of its recommendations is implemented. That gap is the honest starting point. Nothing in this brief closes it alone, but the combination of the items in Tier 1 and Tier 2 is worth several thousand units of capacity, and the Tier 3 financing tools are what determine whether those units are affordable or merely permitted.

Two corrections to the existing baseline drive the sequencing. The Starter and Silver Homes Act failed in the 2026 session, along with the middle housing and ADU preemption bills, so no state backstop is coming and the argument that Frederick will be forced into these reforms anyway no longer holds. At the same time the Maryland Housing Certainty Act did pass, which means every month of delay locks more projects into current rules. The window for local reform is open and closing at the same time.

---

## Corrections to the Current Vault Baseline

**The Starter and Silver Homes Act did not pass.** HB 239 and SB 36 died in the 2026 session, as did the Generational Housing Act ADU mandate (HB 1538), Middle Housing Options (HB 778), and Good Cause Eviction (HB 774 / SB 462). The Frederick Housing Policy Baseline and the LMC Zoning Amendments strategy brief both treat the Starter Homes Act as pending. It is not. Two consequences. First, the political framing in the LMC brief ("getting there first on our own terms is better than being forced there later") needs to be replaced with a straight local-authority argument, because nobody is forcing Frederick anywhere. Second, R4 and R6 stay single-family-detached-only until the council changes it.

**Frederick almost certainly gets no parking relief from the Transit and Housing Opportunity Act.** HB 894 passed and bans parking minimums within a quarter mile of qualifying rail stations, but the qualifying list is WMATA heavy rail, the Purple Line, Baltimore light rail and metro, and the MARC Penn Line only. The Camden Line does not qualify and would need hourly weekday service between 8am and 6pm to do so. The Brunswick Line, which serves Frederick, is not on the list. The Housing Policy Baseline says the act "would eliminate minimums near qualifying transit stations if Frederick's MARC stations qualify." The working assumption should be that they do not. Confirm with Planning before anyone repeats it publicly.

**What did pass and matters.** The Housing Certainty Act (HB 548 / SB 325) gives developers five years of vested rights from complete application submission. Any LMC change adopted later does not reach applications already on file. HB 85 / SB 511 established a state process for converting entities into cooperative limited equity housing corporations, which removes one of the legal-structuring obstacles flagged as an open question in the Frederick CLT brief.

---

## The Diagnosis

Three separate bottlenecks, and they need different tools.

**The code bottleneck.** R4 and R6, Frederick's lowest-density zones and a large share of residential land, permit only single-family detached homes. Triplexes, fourplexes and sixplexes are not defined dwelling types anywhere in the LMC's dimensional tables. ADUs are permitted broadly but throttled by an owner-occupancy requirement and a 10 percent cap in planned developments. Parking minimums of 1.5 to 2 spaces per unit apply citywide outside the downtown overlay, at $5,000 to $10,000 per surface space and $25,000 to $50,000 per structured space, passed straight through to rents. Single-room occupancy, the cheapest market-rate housing form that exists, has no permission structure.

**The process bottleneck.** Review timelines of 18 to 24 months against an MAHTF target of 8 to 10 months. No pre-approved plan library, which means every ADU and small infill project carries full custom design cost. Minor subdivision review that may be closer to Raleigh's one-to-two years than Durham's one-to-two months. A possible building code mismatch where 2 to 8 unit buildings are reviewed under commercial standards. None of these are zoning problems, and none of them are fixed by upzoning.

**The capital bottleneck.** The affordability gap on a single unit at 80 percent AMI runs roughly $60,000 to $80,000 capitalized over a 40-year control period. The payment-in-lieu fee generates about $2,000 per unit. The City Housing Fund holds roughly $4.3 million, enough for about 450 units, and MPDU in-lieu revenue is projected to reach only $1.83 million by 2030. Frederick has no housing trust fund with a dedicated revenue stream, no preservation strategy for naturally occurring affordable housing, and no land disposition policy directing surplus parcels to housing.

---

## Tier 1 -- Low Effort, High Certainty

These require a president letter, a staff directive, a single-provision text amendment, or a reporting resolution. None of them require a coalition, a fiscal note of consequence, or a preemption fight.

**1. Trigger the payment-in-lieu recalculation on schedule.** Section 19-6.1 contemplates recalculation after three years of square footage and affordability gap data. Chapter 19 was re-enacted November 2, 2023, which puts the trigger at November 2026, roughly two months out. Direct Housing and Human Services and Planning now to produce the affordability gap analysis so the recalculation happens rather than lapsing. This is a letter, not an ordinance, and it converts the rate increase from a new imposition into an exercise of existing law. The ordinance follows in Tier 2.

**2. Repeal the ADU owner-occupancy requirement.** One provision in LMC Section 802. Every other ADU restriction stays. This is the single largest suppressor of ADU production in the code and the shortest housing ordinance available to the council. California's 2020 statewide repeal is the cleanest before-and-after evidence.

**3. Remove the 10 percent ADU cap in TNDs, PNDs, and MU zones.** No documented policy justification for the number, no sunset, and it applies to neighborhoods that already have the infrastructure. Another single-provision amendment. Move it with item 2 as one ordinance.

**4. Direct Planning to build a pre-approved plan library and confirm electronic plan review capacity.** A certified set of designs for ADUs, duplexes, and small multifamily that an owner can pull off the shelf, paying only for site-specific work. This is administrative, within the department's existing authority, and it is what determines whether ADU legalization actually produces ADUs. Eugene, Pasadena, Los Angeles, and Seattle all run versions. Ask Director Collard what exists today before assuming it does not.

**5. Require quarterly permit timeline reporting.** Median review time by project type, with deed-restricted affordable projects broken out separately. The MAHTF's 8-to-10-month target is an administrative aspiration with no accountability mechanism attached. A reporting requirement is the accountability mechanism, it costs nothing, and it produces the data needed for any later fast-track ordinance.

**6. Ask the building official one question.** Are 2-to-8-unit buildings in Frederick reviewed under commercial code or the International Residential Code? Dallas revised its code to apply the IRC to buildings up to 8 units and 3 stories, eliminating sprinkler requirements and cutting small multifamily costs substantially. If Frederick has the same mismatch, this is a large cost lever hiding in a code reference.

**7. Defer or waive fees on deed-restricted affordable units.** Permit fees and water and sewer connection fees charged at permit issuance rather than at certificate of occupancy are a carrying-cost problem for projects with the thinnest margins. Deferral costs the city timing, not revenue. Waiver costs revenue and should be limited to units at or below 60 percent AMI.

**8. Ask Planning where Frederick sits on minor subdivision review.** Raleigh's exempt plat ordinance (TC-4-26) exists because small-lot subdivision there takes one to two years while Durham does it in one to two months. If Frederick is on the slow end, an exempt plat process is a procedural reform with no ideological content and it directly enables small-lot and starter home production.

**9. Issue the Housing RFI.** Already on the FY2027 roadmap as a president letter. It is free, and it generates the developer-side pro forma evidence needed to defend a higher payment-in-lieu rate against the feasibility objection that is certain to come.

---

## Tier 2 -- Requires Coordination, Planning, or a Coalition

These are ordinances with real opposition, fiscal notes, or interdepartmental dependencies. They are also where most of the actual production capacity lives.

**1. The MPDU package, moved as one ordinance.** Payment-in-lieu from $2.00 to $12.00 per square foot. Tiered threshold replacing the flat 25-unit cutoff: full MPDU requirements at 15 units and above, payment-in-lieu only at 7 to 14 units, exempt below 7. Deeper affordability bonuses at 60 percent and 30 percent AMI. The 12.5 percent baseline held as a floor, not a negotiating position.

The MAHTF endorses lowering the threshold to 10 units but does not pair it with a rate increase or a depth requirement. Moving threshold reduction alone produces more projects subject to MPDU and weaker affordability per project. These three parameters move together or they should not move.

**2. Missing middle by right in R4 and R6, paired with parking reform.** Duplexes and townhouses permitted by right at R8's dimensional standards, 4,000 square feet for duplexes and 1,400 per townhouse unit. Parking minimums reduced or eliminated for missing middle and multifamily outside downtown, single-family requirements unchanged.

One design caution from Denver. Upzoning without form constraints tends to produce teardown-and-replace with larger, more expensive units rather than more units. Denver's Unlocking Housing Choices approach caps what a single unit can be while unlocking additional square footage for developers who build more, smaller units. Layering that size-for-units trade into the R4 and R6 amendment answers the "this will bring luxury teardowns to my street" objection with a code provision rather than a promise.

**3. APFO tiered AMI weighting.** Units at or below 40 percent AMI do not count toward the school capacity test, 40 to 60 percent count as half a unit, 60 to 80 percent count as three quarters, limited to developments that are entirely affordable at or below 80 percent AMI. This is the highest-urgency council-actionable item in the MAHTF report and it is technical enough to move without a culture fight. Adkins is reachable on planning grounds.

Be honest about the tension. Three city schools are above 120 percent capacity and six are above 100 percent. The argument for this is that affordable units generate fewer students per unit than large single-family homes and that excluding affordable housing from growth while permitting market-rate growth is a distributional choice, not a neutral capacity rule. Make that argument on the merits rather than treating the school question as a technicality.

**4. Faith-owned land by right.** A targeted LMC text amendment permitting housing on land owned by religious institutions and nonprofits without rezoning. Virginia's HB 1279 is the model, including its four-year sunset, which is a useful design precedent because it forces a reauthorization vote against actual production data. Preservation Maryland has identified over 37,000 acres of faith-owned land statewide. Pair the amendment with a designated city contact and a process map for congregations, because the barrier for a church with an underused parking lot is usually navigational rather than legal.

**5. SRO and co-living legalization.** The ten-section amendment package already exists in draft. The MAHTF's explicit endorsement is the political opening that did not exist before. SROs house the population with the least mobility and the highest proportion of immigrants and low-wage workers, which makes the post-revocation stewardship gap below a hard prerequisite rather than a refinement.

**6. Public land disposition policy.** An ordinance or standing policy requiring that surplus city land be offered first for affordable housing through a public RFP, with a community land trust ground lease as an available structure. Public land should not be traded without a public process, which is already the roadmap language. Pensacola is the live model: the city routed a demolished gym site to the Northwest Florida Community Land Trust rather than selling it, and nine homes there will stay affordable permanently rather than for a covenant period. Lucas Village is the local precedent.

**7. A Housing Trust Fund with a dedicated revenue stream.** Consolidate the $250,000 Housing Accelerator CIP, the MPDU fund, and a dedicated stream into one vehicle with an annual appropriation. The MAHTF makes this case without naming it.

Flag the internal conflict now. The rental licensing rewrite cuts the license fee from $240 to roughly $150 biennially. The MAHTF's own numbers say the housing fund is structurally undercapitalized and names rental licensing revenue as a source. Those two positions cannot both be the council's. Resolve it before both bills are in committee.

**8. Post-revocation stewardship.** The licensing rewrite, the SRO amendments, and the Real Rent Ordinance all end at punishment. None answers what happens to tenants and the building when a license is actually revoked. A receivership provision, or a right of first refusal for a nonprofit or CLT at revocation, converts enforcement from a displacement event into permanent affordability. This is the difference between deterrence and transformation, and it is the piece that makes the SRO ordinance defensible to the tenants it covers.

**9. Eviction diversion.** Alexandria's program lets a tenant behind on rent stay by paying 10 percent of the balance up front and the rest on installments. It targets one-time shocks rather than chronic inability to pay, it costs the landlord less than a filing, and it requires no new fee schedules or landlord obligations. Low friction, small-landlord friendly, and a credible early-phase component of the tenant protection package.

## Tier 3 -- New and Structurally Different

These change what kind of housing system Frederick has, not just how many units clear the code. They are harder, slower, and they are where the affordability, as opposed to the supply, actually comes from.

**1. A Frederick Housing Production Fund built on equity replacement.** This is the highest-leverage idea in the file. Montgomery County's Housing Opportunities Commission capitalized a $100 million fund through an HOC bond and a matching county appropriation, then used it to take an ownership position in market-rate developments in place of private equity. Private equity demands 15 to 20 percent returns. HOC charges 5 percent. That single cost change makes stalled but fully entitled projects pencil without deep subsidy. Projected output is 6,000 units over the fund's 20-year bond life, 1,800 of them permanently affordable, with loan interest flowing back to the county so the steady-state taxpayer cost approaches zero. Atlanta, Chicago, Michigan, Massachusetts, and New York have copied it.

The reframe matters as much as the mechanism. The question stops being "how do we subsidize affordable units" and becomes "how do we cut the cost of the capital that stops projects from penciling." Frederick cannot do this at $100 million, and the city's own bond capacity is not the obvious vehicle. The Housing Authority of the City of Frederick is. A joint city-HACF production fund, capitalized by an authority bond and a recurring city appropriation, is the version of this that fits Frederick's balance sheet. It is a two-to-three-year build and it should start with a resolution directing the CFO to evaluate feasibility, which the MAHTF already invites through its TIF and alternative finance recommendation.

**2. A preservation fund with a property tax rebate.** Frederick has no strategy for naturally occurring affordable housing, the older unsubsidized stock that disappears when an investor buys, renovates, and re-rents. Mecklenburg County lost more than 77 percent of its low-cost housing between 2015 and 2024 that way. Charlotte's response was a business-backed Housing Impact Fund that raised $225 million in impact capital at returns as low as 6 percent, bought nearly 2,000 units, and capped rents at 80 percent AMI with a third of units at or below 30 percent AMI for 20 years.

The transferable piece is not the fund size. It is the subsidy mechanism: the city and county each rebate an amount equal to a participating property's property taxes back into the fund, targeted at its deepest-affordability units. That costs the city forgone incremental revenue rather than an appropriation, which is a materially easier ask in a tight budget. Preservation is also cheaper per unit than production, and every preserved unit is a household that does not get displaced while waiting for new supply.

**3. Community land trust with cooperative ownership, satisfying MPDU.** Two specific moves rather than a general endorsement. First, amend Chapter 19 so a developer can satisfy MPDU obligations by contributing land or capital to a community land trust. That converts 40-year affordability into permanent affordability and gives a trust a development pipeline it would not otherwise have. Second, HB 85 / SB 511 passed this session and established a state process for cooperative limited equity housing corporations, which removes one of the open legal questions in the existing CLT brief. The CLT-plus-co-op structure reaches the homeownership-aspiration cohort that neither the supply frame nor the social housing frame currently reaches, and it produces residents with a permanent material stake in the neighborhood rather than tenants of a program.

**4. Municipal ADU sponsorship.** Charlotte's Queen City ADU Program offered up to $80,000 or half of eligible project costs as interest-free forgivable financing, with $10,000 forgiven for each year the unit stays rented at or below 80 percent AMI over an eight-year affordability period, open to both owner-occupants and non-occupant owners. The program is currently paused, so cite the design rather than the outcome, but the design is right.

A Frederick-scale version at $25,000 to $40,000 per unit from the Housing Fund, forgiven over eight to ten years against a recorded covenant, is probably the cheapest per-unit affordable production available to a city this size. It also does something politically useful. It puts homeowners and small landlords on the supply side of the housing fight instead of in the opposition, because the policy hands them an asset rather than asking them to accept a building next door. It only works if items 2, 3, and 4 in Tier 1 are already done. Legalization without financing and without pre-approved plans produces permits nobody pulls.

**5. Define mobile and modular dwelling units in the LMC.** Factory-built movable units sit in a cost tier below site-built ADUs, which is the tier where ADU legalization keeps failing on economics. Permit them wherever ADUs are permitted, subject to the same standards. Verify the state preemption position first, because Maryland's treatment of manufactured and modular housing is the threshold question and the vault does not answer it.

**6. Make a housing fund contribution a standard element of any data center community benefit agreement.** The vault already establishes that data center tax incentives and housing subsidies compete for the same dollars, and that a business personal property tax-exempt use displacing reliable tax base makes housing finance more fragile, not less. If the ZTA moves regardless, the correct posture is not only opposition. It is that a mandatory, formula-based housing fund contribution is a non-negotiable element of approval, not a discretionary goodwill item. That converts the fight the council may lose into a capitalization stream for the fund it needs.

**7. Shared housing with master leasing.** Camden County built shared housing pilots entirely from existing resources by paying for provider training and seeding a small landlord incentive pool, with a nonprofit rather than the individual tenant holding the lease and managing matching and conflict. It uses existing market-rate units, requires no construction, and shifts risk off the tenant with a record or an unstable history. What landlords actually respond to is guaranteed rent and help with turnover, which can be repairs and paint rather than cash. Government as catalyst, nonprofits as implementers. This is the cheapest homelessness intervention per household on this list and it needs a budget line in the tens of thousands, not the millions.

**8. Size-for-units zoning math.** Covered above as a design layer on the R4 and R6 amendment, but it belongs here too because it is a genuinely different way to think about zoning. Instead of asking what to permit, it asks what to make profitable. Cap maximum unit size, unlock additional floor area for more and smaller units, and the developer math shifts from one expensive unit to several affordable ones without a mandate or a subsidy.

**9. A single-stair resolution timed to the Maryland Department of Labor study.** Frederick cannot amend the state building code. It can be on record, in the company of Baltimore, and ready to adopt when enabling legislation moves. Single-stair designs cut costs 6 to 13 percent, dedicate up to 95 percent of floor area to housing rather than 70 to 87 percent, and open narrow infill lots that are undevelopable under the double-egress rule. Expect fire department friction and plan the engagement before the resolution, not after.

## State and County Asks

Flagged separately because none of these are within council authority. They belong in MML advocacy, state delegation coordination, and the county relationship.

**State.** Support reintroduction of the Starter and Silver Homes Act, Middle Housing Options, and the Generational Housing Act ADU mandate, with local-flexibility amendments so Frederick is not arguing against its own reforms. Support single-stair enabling legislation following the Department of Labor study. Note that Good Cause Eviction (HB 774 / SB 462) authorized counties rather than municipalities, so any municipal tenant protection authority needs its own path and should be a specific delegation ask rather than a general one. Separately, MARC Brunswick Line service frequency is a housing question: hourly weekday service between 8am and 6pm is the threshold that could bring Frederick under HB 894's transit-oriented development and parking provisions, which turns an MTA funding request into a housing production tool.

**County.** Recordation tax reduction or refund for affordable housing projects with the revenue redirected to housing, which the MAHTF recommends and which requires a city-county negotiation. School capacity coordination with FCPS alongside any APFO amendment, connected to the school mitigation fund review already on the roadmap. TransIT service on the Golden Mile, the corridor holding more than 30 underutilized commercial parcels and the most plausible site for the small-lot redevelopment several of these tools are designed to unlock.

## Sequencing

Move Tier 1 items 1 through 5 in the next 60 days. They are letters and staff directives except for the ADU ordinance, and they establish that the council is the body driving housing rather than the body responding to the Mayor's task force.

Move the ADU amendments and the MPDU package on parallel tracks in the same quarter. The MPDU recalculation trigger arrives in November 2026 and the analysis has to be requested now to meet it.

Move R4 and R6 plus parking reform after the Housing RFI returns, so the feasibility argument is answered with developer data rather than assertion. Do not wait for the comprehensive LMC review. The Housing Certainty Act vests every complete application on file for five years, so the cost of waiting is measured in projects permanently exempted from whatever the review produces.

Start the Housing Production Fund feasibility resolution now even though the fund is a two-to-three-year build, because the CFO evaluation is the long pole and it can run in parallel with everything above.

## Risks and Distributional Flags

Supply-side reform without tenant protection produces displacement before it produces affordability. Upzoning raises land values first and delivers units later, and the households displaced in the interval are exactly the ALICE renters this agenda exists for. The Real Rent Ordinance, the licensing rewrite, and eviction diversion are not optional companions to the production agenda. The MAHTF is supply-side in its dominant framing and does not name tenant protection as a parallel priority. Being publicly on record that production and protection are two tracks rather than substitutes is the position that keeps the council honest when the first displacement story runs.

The rental licensing fee reduction and the housing fund capitalization argument are in direct conflict and both are live.

The MAHTF is a mayoral instrument. Every recommendation in it that the council adopts as a council-sponsored ordinance is a recommendation the council owns. Every one that waits for administrative implementation is one the Mayor owns.

Finally, the arithmetic. The task force projects 4,000 units under favorable conditions against 13,774 units of documented need. Naming that gap publicly is what prevents the report from being treated as sufficient.

## Verify Before Acting

- Whether the Section 19-6.1 recalculation trigger has been activated and what date HHS considers it to fall on.
- Current City Housing Fund balance and FY2027 allocations, and whether Chapter 19 administrative regulations were ever adopted under Section 19-4.
- Whether any Frederick MARC station qualifies under HB 894. The working assumption here is no.
- Whether 2-to-8-unit buildings are reviewed under commercial code or the IRC.
- Whether Frederick already has any pre-approved plan library or electronic plan review capacity.
- Maryland's treatment of modular and mobile dwelling units, and what HB 85 / SB 511 actually enables for a Frederick CLT.
- Current inventory of city-owned parcels suitable for housing, and whether HACF has unused bond capacity.
- Whether any Maryland CLT could be partnered with rather than a new entity created.

## Drafting Flags

These need actual legislative or correspondence language and should be worked in chat rather than drafted here.

1. President letter to HHS and Planning directing the MPDU affordability gap analysis ahead of the November 2026 recalculation trigger.
2. LMC text amendment repealing ADU owner-occupancy and the 10 percent planned-development cap, as one ordinance.
3. President letter to Planning on pre-approved plans, electronic plan review, minor subdivision timelines, and the residential-versus-commercial building code question for 2-to-8-unit buildings.
4. Resolution directing the CFO to evaluate a city-HACF housing production fund on the Montgomery County HOC equity-replacement model.
5. Public statement establishing the council's housing agenda and the production-plus-protection frame, before the MAHTF implementation narrative sets.
