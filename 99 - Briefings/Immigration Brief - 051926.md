# Immigration Brief - 051926

## This Week

The federal pressure on local immigration policy has intensified in 2026 in ways that matter directly for cities like Frederick. Senator Lindsey Graham introduced two versions of the End Sanctuary Cities Act this winter and spring, with the updated S. 3805 imposing criminal penalties on local officials who decline to honor ICE detainer requests. The bill targets a specific category of government action: detainers are civil immigration requests, not criminal warrants, and federal courts have historically held that local governments cannot be compelled to execute them under the anti-commandeering doctrine of the 10th Amendment. The bill attempts to reframe noncompliance as willful obstruction of federal law enforcement, which would expose sheriffs, police chiefs, and city officials to personal criminal liability.

The constitutional challenge is real and is already being articulated by legal commentators. The Hill published an op-ed from a former federal official arguing the bill is flatly unconstitutional because it would coerce state and local law enforcement into implementing federal immigration policy, crossing the commandeering line that courts have enforced for decades in other contexts. Oregon has been a sanctuary state since 1987; more than 200 jurisdictions across the country currently have sanctuary policies. Courts have generally held that sanctuary policies do not interfere with federal enforcement, they merely decline to augment it with local resources. That distinction is the constitutional fulcrum.

On the city response side, San Francisco held a large public rally in January to reaffirm its sanctuary policy, with representatives from local unions, nonprofits, law enforcement, and city government. The Sheriff's statement there was notable because it articulated the narrow exception clearly: the city's policy permits ICE notification only when a person with a serious or violent felony conviction is leaving custody. That kind of precision is exactly what cities need in their policies, not broad silence but exact, defensible, documented limitations. The framing protects both immigrant communities and city officials from accusation that the policy shields violent criminals.

The NILC analysis of the broader funding threat remains the clearest statement of stakes for smaller cities. Because the Graham bill is broadly drafted, virtually any federal program touching state and local government could become a funding lever. While undocumented immigrants are ineligible for most major federal benefit programs, the cities and counties that serve them most heavily are also often the ones most dependent on federal grants for housing, health, and transportation infrastructure. The political arithmetic of this threat is designed to force capitulation from cities that cannot absorb the loss.

The Global Refuge explainer on sanctuary city mechanics is useful framing for public communication. Sanctuary policies do not mean cities conceal immigrants from federal authorities or prevent deportation. They limit local police from acting as immigration agents, declining to make arrests on civil immigration warrants or enter 287(g) agreements that deputize local officers. Federal authorities retain full independent enforcement authority. That distinction is something Frederick's public communications on the City Values Act should make explicit and repeat consistently.

## Frederick Implications

This is live territory. The City Values Act and its proposed amendment are the Frederick-level expression of the national sanctuary policy debate. The Maryland Community Trust Act passage and the 287(g) ban give the city state-level backing that strengthens the legal and political position. The Sheriff's implementation posture under the Community Trust Act is the variable to watch locally, and the vault note on judicial warrant requirements and sheriff resistance is the right thread to follow.

District 2 has the highest concentration of immigrants in Frederick. The chilling effect research in the vault on ICE enforcement and its economic and housing disruption consequences is directly applicable: when immigrant families stop using public institutions, the community-wide costs accumulate in housing instability, lost small business activity, and school attendance drops. Frederick's investment in the City Values Act is also an investment in keeping District 2's economic and civic fabric intact.

The criminal penalty provisions in Graham's bill are a signal worth watching in council communications. If the bill advances in any form, the council will need a clear public statement that the legal position of Frederick officials acting under the City Values Act is defensible, that the city has reviewed the constitutional arguments, and that it is not backing down. That statement should be prepared before the moment it is needed, not drafted under pressure.

Municipal ID is the most actionable near-term protective tool independent of federal legislation. Regardless of what happens with sanctuary policy at the federal level, a municipal ID program increases immigrant community members' ability to access city services and engage with institutions on terms that do not require immigration status disclosure. The vault materials on legal risks and federal preemption are the right starting point for a council briefing on next steps.
