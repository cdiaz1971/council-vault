# ADU Ordinance Brief -- PC25-614ZTA
**For August 13, 2026 Work Session, Item A**

## Bottom Line

The draft ordinance advances a real, defensible production goal: eliminating owner-occupancy, easing parking, and raising the size ceiling to 1,200 SF are the changes most likely to unlock ADU construction after four years of zero detached ADUs built under the 2021 code. Two pieces of public comment on this item, from Kevin Sellner and from Citizens for Responsible Growth, raise affordability and character concerns worth taking seriously, but the specific mechanisms they propose don't hold up against either the historical evidence or their own cited numbers. Position going in: realistically increase the number of ADUs in Frederick. Not opposed to affordability mandates, parking concessions, or size trade-offs in principle, but only where they're paired with something that actually offsets the cost to the property owner, the way every affordable housing tool that has ever worked in practice does it. A mandate with no offset doesn't produce more affordable units. It produces fewer units, full stop.

## What's Actually in the Draft

Eliminates the requirement that the owner live in the ADU or the primary residence. Reduces parking (transit-proximity exemption at 1,320 feet, smaller stall dimensions). Raises the detached ADU size ceiling from 800 to as much as 1,200 SF, only through a Planning Commission modification, not by right. Retains the existing 90-day minimum lease term. Strikes the current deed-restriction/recordation requirement entirely. Planning Commission voted 4-0 to recommend approval, but with the express condition that owner-occupancy be retained, on community character grounds.

Since 2021, zero detached ADUs have been successfully permitted and constructed under the current code. That's the production data point underlying tonight's changes.

Maryland HB 1466 (2025), effective October 1, 2025, requires a compliant local ADU ordinance by October 1, 2026, and separately prohibits deed restrictions that "unreasonably limit" ADU development, defined to include anything that significantly increases construction cost or effectively prohibits development. Not cited anywhere in the packet materials. Worth confirming with Rachel Gray whether this is why the deed-restriction requirement is being struck, and whether it creates exposure for both the Planning Commission's suggested deed-restriction fallback and for Sellner's covenant proposal below.

## Countering Kevin Sellner's Proposal

His letter asks for two things: the 1,200 SF exemption and the non-owner-occupied allowance should only be available if the owner signs a 15-year covenant renting to ALICE or sub-30%-AMI households at $903/month with capped escalation, subject to mandatory annual City inspection for the full term, with no ADU permitted without City enforcement.

**No offsetting incentive is attached.** Every affordable housing mechanism that has actually worked, LIHTC, MPDU bonus density, PILOT abatement, pairs an income restriction with something that compensates the owner for it: a density bonus, a fee waiver, gap financing. Sellner's proposal offers nothing beyond what the ordinance already grants for free. A rational owner facing that choice simply builds the smaller, unrestricted unit instead, or doesn't build at all.

**The inspection burden is roughly six to seven times the citywide standard.** Frederick's existing rental licensing ordinance, Chapter 12.5, requires at least 15% of licensed rental units to be inspected each year, on a license that renews every two years. That's the baseline every other rental unit in the city operates under. Sellner is asking for 100% inspection every year, for fifteen straight years, applied to this housing type alone.

**The math doesn't pencil, using CRG's own numbers.** Fifteen years of gross rent at $903/month with 2-3% annual escalation totals roughly $190,000, before subtracting taxes, insurance, maintenance, financing costs, or the burden of complying with annual inspections for fifteen years. CRG's own letter puts new detached ADU construction at $100,000 to $300,000. At the low end, the owner barely recovers construction cost with nothing left over. At the realistic end for a full-size new build, they never recover it at all.

**The real-world pattern this resembles is well documented, and it isn't affordable housing production, it's the design that suppresses it.** Where a similar type of below-market mandate has been imposed without an offset, San Francisco's inclusionary zoning program, which offers no offsetting density bonus, saw production fall an average of 31% the year after adoption in Bay Area municipalities studied by Powell and Stringham (2004). Even where a genuine incentive exists but isn't attractive enough, builders simply decline it: a Grounded Solutions Network study of Seattle's voluntary bonus program found more than half of eligible projects built to base height and skipped the affordable bonus entirely. San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion is the clearest causal evidence on what an uncompensated rent mandate does to supply: landlords treated by rent control reduced rental housing supplies by 15 percent by selling to owner-occupants and redeveloping buildings, and the lost rental housing supply likely drove up market rents in the long run, ultimately undermining the goals of the law (Diamond, McQuade, and Qian, American Economic Review, 2019).

## Countering the CRG Letter (Marjorie Rosensweig)

**Her letter contains an internal contradiction worth naming.** She argues both that the changes are unlikely to open the gates to significant ADU production, and that they'll produce enough investor activity to damage character and overwhelm parking. Both can't be true. If production stays minimal, there's nothing to worry about on character or parking. If there's enough production to threaten either, her production estimate is wrong.

**Her own cost data undercuts Sellner's mechanism specifically.** She cites $100,000 to $300,000 for new detached ADU construction, precisely the size-increase category Sellner's covenant would attach to. Read straight, her own letter shows the covenant can't work financially on the exact units it claims to unlock.

**The absentee-landlord "pencils at market rate" point is fair but not new to this ordinance.** No version of Section 802, before or after tonight's changes, has ever imposed a rent restriction on any ADU, owner-occupied or not. An owner-occupant renting their existing ADU today faces the same incentive to charge market rent. This is a critique of the affordability language in Section 802 generally, not a reason to oppose removing owner-occupancy specifically. Unrestricted new supply still puts downward pressure on citywide rents even when a given unit isn't itself below market.

**The rental licensing skepticism deserves partial credit, not a full concession.** Enforcement capacity is a fair, ongoing question for any city program. But Chapter 12.5 isn't hypothetical. It requires at least 15% of licensed units inspected annually, with fines up to $1,000 per day for noncompliance. That's a real codified floor whatever the execution record looks like.

**The parking objection is her most legitimate point and the easiest to answer plainly.** The transit exemption removes a requirement, it doesn't prohibit anyone from building a space if they want one. The 1,320-foot standard isn't new or ADU-specific, it's the same distance already used for parking reductions in the DB and DBO downtown districts.

## The Empirical Record on Investor-Driven "Degradation"

This is the part of both letters, and of the Planning Commission's own third recommendation, that concerns character and gentrification-style harm rather than affordability mechanics. The honest read: mostly not supported at ADU scale, though there are two narrower, real findings worth acknowledging rather than dismissing.

**Against the concern:** California's own data connecting ADU permitting to ownership records found the percent of ADUs built on properties with a corporate owner is similar to the percent of single-family homes with a corporate owner across the state. No disproportionate institutional takeover. A Urban Institute case study of a Portland neighborhood that specifically required owner-occupancy found that neighborhood had fewer owners actually living on the property, 35 percent, than the rest of the city without the requirement, 90 percent, because the policy was difficult to enforce and discouraged first-time homeowner-developers, and that speculative investment in ADUs did not materialize where owner-occupancy wasn't required. The same research found most ADUs don't create the parking problems commonly assumed. The mechanism the Commission's letter worries about, requiring owner-occupancy to prevent an investor problem, was tested directly in that Portland case and produced the opposite of its intended effect.

**Fair caveats worth having ready if pressed:** A 2024 peer-reviewed study of the Austin, TX ADU market (Jenna Davis, Housing Policy Debate) found no significant rent difference between investor-owned and owner-occupied ADUs on the open market, but flagged that owner-occupants often extend informal discounts to tenants they know personally, off the visible market, a gap the authors say could widen over time. Separately, research on rental housing generally, not ADUs specifically, has found signs of disinvestment increase when properties shift from individual to LLC ownership. Neither finding supports "rapid, character-altering development." Both are legitimate, narrower points about long-run affordability and maintenance worth conceding rather than overclaiming against.

The large-scale investor-purchase-and-eviction literature (e.g. Atlanta gentrification research) that does show real, documented displacement harm is about institutional acquisition of existing multifamily rental buildings at scale, a fundamentally different transaction than one homeowner adding a single small unit to a lot they already own. That mechanism doesn't map onto ADUs, and nothing in the ADU-specific literature shows a pattern of rapid, character-altering investor consolidation happening through this tool.

## Where There's Room for Common Ground

Not closed to an affordability-targeted mechanism for the ADU program. What would actually work is the same model the MPDU strategy already leans on: pair any income restriction with a genuine offset, a fee waiver, City Housing Fund gap dollars, or additional size or height allowance beyond what's already free in this draft. That's a real incentive structure with a track record. A mandate with no offset isn't affordability policy, it's a way to make sure the provision it's attached to never gets used.
