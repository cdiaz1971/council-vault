# Child Care Supply LMC Amendment

**Agenda Item 6 of 6.** Part of [[Frederick Affordability Agenda 2026-2029]]. Vehicle: **ordinance** (LMC text amendment), Planning Commission review. Budget follow-on in FY2029. Sponsor: Díaz.

## Summary

Child care is now the largest cost in an ALICE family's budget, ahead of housing, and the City does not set rates, license providers, or run subsidies. What it does control is where care can legally operate. Reading LMC § 811 against current state law turns up a concrete problem: the LMC's rules for homes caring for 8 to 12 children look like they limit capacity below what the State authorizes, which Land Use § 4-216 forbids. Fixing that is part legal compliance, part supply policy, and it costs nothing. It is the right first move in a domain where the City's tools are otherwise indirect.

## Why it belongs in an affordability agenda

- A Frederick County family with two young children pays $2,500 a month for care in the ALICE survival budget, up $698 (39%) in one year and now more than housing ($2,250) ([[ALICE 2026 Report Policy Brief 2026-09-30]]). 46% of county families spend over $1,200 a month; 80% rely on child care to work ([[Childcare and Care Economy -- Frederick Peer City Research and Council Options]]).
- 72% of children in rental units in the county lived in ALICE or poverty households as of the 2023 data, the most recent figure available ([[Cesar Context -- Council]]).
- The State's Child Care Scholarship program froze new enrollments on May 1, 2025, with about 5,000 families on the waitlist. The General Assembly added $20 million for FY2027 and expanded priority eligibility effective July 1, 2026, but some families remain frozen out (Maryland Matters, April 20, 2026). When subsidy is rationed, supply near home matters more.

## What the LMC says

- **§ 811(c).** Homes caring for eight or fewer children are exempt from approval, citing COMAR.
- **§ 811(d).** Homes caring for **8 to 12 children** are treated as "Small Child Care Centers," allowed in R districts only if:
  - the applicant gives "guarantees as may be deemed necessary by the Board" against nuisance, with the Board considering other day cares within 500 feet;
  - the center employs **no more than one nonresident** and provides a parking space for that person;
  - pickup and drop-off are shown "to the Board's satisfaction";
  - the lot has **at least 1,000 square feet per child**; and
  - the operator shows a state license.
- **§ 811(d)** cites "Maryland Code (Section 5-572)." Child care licensing moved from the Family Law Article to Education Article Title 9.5 years ago. The citation is stale.
- **Child day care centers** are conditional uses in most residential districts in Table 404-1, and § 811(a) permits them in RO, NC, GC, DB, MU, and MXE subject to site plan review. The table and § 811(a) should be reconciled; Planning should confirm the column readings.
- **Parking:** minimum 1 space and maximum 1.5 spaces per 375 square feet for child day care centers (Table 607-1).
- **§ 829(c)(1)E.** If a dwelling is used for child care, no other home occupation is allowed on the premises.

## The state-law problem

- **Education § 9.5-301:** a family child care home serves up to 8 children; a **large family child care home serves 9 to 12**.
- **Land Use § 4-216(b):** "A local jurisdiction may not, by local ordinance, resolution, law, or rule, limit the number of children for which a family child care home or large family child care home provides family child care to below the number authorized by the State Department of Education."

A state-licensed large family child care home on a typical R6 lot of 7,000 square feet could care for only 7 children under the 1,000-square-foot-per-child rule, and on an 8,000 square foot R4 lot, only 8. That is a local limit below the State's 12. The one-nonresident-employee cap can also operate as a capacity limit, since staffing ratios at 9 to 12 children generally require a second adult. **This appears to conflict with § 4-216 and should go to the City Attorney as a compliance question.** If she agrees, the amendment is not optional policy, it is conformance, which changes the politics.

A 2026 bill (HB 1259) would have gone further and required localities to treat licensed family child care homes as a permitted residential use. It reached conference committee and does not appear to have been enacted. § 4-216 is the operative law.

## The amendment

1. **Conform § 811(d) to state law.** Treat state-licensed family and large family child care homes as permitted residential uses at their licensed capacity. Remove the per-child lot minimum and the nonresident-employee cap as applied to licensed homes. Keep objective operating standards (licensed status, a safe drop-off area) and drop the open-ended "guarantees as may be deemed necessary by the Board."
2. **Update citations** to Education Article Title 9.5 and current COMAR 13A.
3. **Centers by right in mixed-use, commercial, and institutional settings.** Permit child day care centers by right (with site plan) in MU, MXE, NC, GC, and as an accessory use on the property of places of worship and schools, which already have parking and drop-off infrastructure. Reconcile Table 404-1 with § 811(a).
4. **Parking.** Allow shared or off-site parking to count toward the 1 per 375 square foot requirement and reduce it for sites on fixed-route transit.
5. **Remove the § 829(c)(1)E bar** on a provider's household running any other home occupation.
6. **Fee relief.** Waive City zoning and permit fees for new licensed child care providers for three years, by amendment to the fee schedule ordinance (Charter Art. VII, § 8).

## Budget follow-on (FY2029)

The vault's options note proposes a reserved-slot pilot: the City buys 50 to 100 slots from licensed providers for ALICE-threshold families, estimated at $600,000 to $1.8 million a year, with a provider wage standard as a contract condition ([[Childcare and Care Economy -- Frederick Peer City Research and Council Options]]). That is realistic only after two things happen: a data request to Frederick County on scholarship waitlist and slot capacity within City limits, to learn whether the gap is supply or price, and a year of the zoning change. Target FY2029 (budget adopted June 2028), not FY2028, so the FY2028 cycle can carry the housing fund and water credit.

## Obstacles

- **Neighborhood objections** to traffic and noise in residential zones. The answer for licensed homes is that the State has already decided capacity.
- **Planning Commission time.** Bundle with the Item 5 submission if Planning prefers one hearing, or run separately if Item 5 stalls. This item is less controversial and should not be held hostage to it.
- **Limited direct effect on price.** Zoning adds supply, it does not lower tuition. Be honest about that in public. The pilot is the price tool.
