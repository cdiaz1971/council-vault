# Council Legal Authority for Affordability

Part of [[Frederick Affordability Agenda 2026-2029]].

## Summary

This note is the legal floor under the agenda. It records what the Charter and City Code say, section by section, so every item in the plan can be traced to a specific grant of power and to the specific limits on it. Where the vault's earlier research drifts from the text, the text controls and the discrepancy is flagged.

**Version caution.** The City Code file reviewed is the March 22, 2026 export in `G:\My Drive\City council\09 - City Documents & Templates`. It does not include 2026 enactments such as G-26-1 (City Values Act), G-26-6 (Rodent Control, which now occupies Chapter 12.5, Article VI), or G-26-11 (Housing Certainty Act implementation). Pull the current Municode text before any drafting session.

## 1. Where the council's power comes from

### 1.1 The general grants

- **Charter Art. II, § 4 (General powers).** The Council "may pass any ordinance not contrary to the Constitution and laws of Maryland or this Charter" for good government and "for the protection of the health, safety, and welfare of the residents of the City." This is the police-power hook for tenant protections, fee rules, and utility protections.
- **Charter Art. IV, § 1 (Powers of the City).** The City has all powers granted to municipalities under Maryland law "together with any and all powers inherent in the creation of municipalities," and "may exercise any power or perform any function that is not now or hereafter denied to it by the Constitution of Maryland, this Charter, or any applicable law passed by the General Assembly." Read with Maryland Constitution Art. XI-E and Local Government Article § 5-202, this is broad home rule, bounded by preemption.
- **Charter Art. IV, § 2 (Enforcement).** Ordinances "may be enforced by civil remedy, or by fines and incarceration, or by other penalties or remedies as permitted by Maryland law." Civil remedy and municipal infraction fines are the safe enforcement tools. A new private cause of action is not (see 4.2).

### 1.2 Specific grants that matter for affordability

| Tool | Charter or Code grant | What it allows |
|---|---|---|
| Fees | Charter Art. VII, § 8 | Council sets "administrative or regulatory fees as allowed by law" by ordinance. Basis for fee waivers and income-sensitive fees. |
| Property tax credits | Charter Art. VII, § 10(d) | Council "may establish property tax credits as authorized by state law" by ordinance. The existing credits live in Code Ch. 8, Art. III. |
| Tax rate classes | Code § 8-14(b) | A higher rate on "habitually vacant property," rising to 5x the normal rate at 9 years. A revenue source that can be dedicated to housing. |
| Water and sewer rates | Charter Art. XI, § 2(d); Code § 25-1 | City "may charge and collect water rents and other charges as necessary." Council sets rates. |
| Utility revenue use | Code § 8-11 | Utility revenues go to a separate fund for operations, replacement, and debt. Surplus may go to general purposes; deficits come from the General Fund. This makes a rate-funded discount legally delicate and a General Fund or donated subsidy cleaner. |
| Bonds | Charter Art. VII, § 13 | General obligation debt capped at 3% of assessed value, water and sewer bonds excluded. Council approves every issue. |
| Land acquisition and disposal | Charter Art. XIII, §§ 1, 2, 3 | Acquire by purchase, gift, or condemnation for any public purpose; sell after 20 days' notice when "no longer needed for any public use." The legal basis for a housing-first land disposition policy. |
| Zoning | Charter Art. XIV, § 1 | Council divides the City into zones and amends regulations under the Land Use Article and the LMC (Appendix A of the Code). LMC amendments run through the Planning Commission first. |
| Adequate public facilities | Code Ch. 4 (under Land Use § 7-101) | Council controls the APFO tests, including school adequacy. City Code, not LMC. |
| Affordable housing (MPDU) | Code Ch. 19 (under Land Use § 7-401) | MPDU requirement, payment in lieu, City Housing Fund. City Code, not LMC, except the density bonus that points to LMC § 405(d). |
| Rental licensing | Code Ch. 12.5, Art. V | Licensing, inspections, revocation, tenant anti-retaliation, special funds. The hook for Real Rent. |
| Receivership | Code Ch. 12.5, Art. II | Court-appointed receiver for blighted structures, but only structures that are unoccupied (§ 12.5-3(b), (f)). |
| Purchasing | Charter Art. IV, § 4 | Council sets purchasing regulations by ordinance or resolution. Basis for contract standards such as wage floors for City vendors. |
| Collective bargaining | Charter Art. VIII, § 7 | Council may provide for bargaining by ordinance. Listed for completeness, not an agenda item here. |

### 1.3 What the council does not control

- **Administration.** Charter Art. VI(A), § 2 puts all departments "under the direct supervision of the Mayor." The pre-2024 language letting the Council "prescribe the functions" of departments (old Art. VI, § 1) was replaced. The Council can still require reports and create programs by ordinance, and the Code is full of precedent for that (§ 12.5-88 annual rental licensing report, § 19-6.1(g) housing fund report, § 33-11(b) quarterly City Values Act report). It cannot direct day-to-day staff work by resolution.
- **Budget execution.** Charter Art. VII, §§ 1 and 7 give the Mayor financial supervision and control of expenditures. The Council controls appropriations.
- **Contracts.** Charter Art. IV, § 3 lets the Mayor enter contracts, subject to City purchasing thresholds.

## 2. The vote math that governs every item

- **Passage.** "Every ordinance, resolution, and official action must be approved by a majority of the City Council members present and voting" (Art. II, § 10). The Council President votes on all questions (Art. II, § 9.1(b)).
- **Veto.** The Mayor may veto any ordinance, resolution, or official action within two weeks (Art. II, §§ 15.1(b), 16.1(c), 17.1(c)).
- **Override.** Two-thirds of the total membership, which on a seven-member council is **five votes**, within 30 days of return (Art. II, §§ 15.1(c), 16.1(d), 17.1(d)).
- **Budget.** Adopted by special ordinance on "a majority of the total membership," four votes (Art. VII, § 5(d)), before June 20 (§ 5(e)). The Council has "sole power to revise the budget" starting May 15 (§ 3(a)) and may insert or change items if it balances revenue (§ 5(c)). **If the Council fails to adopt, or the Mayor vetoes and the Council fails to override, "the Mayor's budget, as proposed, shall be deemed to have been finally adopted"** (§ 5(e)). Any agenda item that needs money lives or dies on either Mayor buy-in or five votes.

The practical rule for this agenda: design every ordinance to be defensible at five votes, and treat the Mayor as a likely veto on anything that reads as a new regulatory burden on landlords or developers.

## 3. The legislative pipeline

Per [[Frederick City Council Legislative Process]]: origination (committee, staff, member, or Planning Commission for zoning) → optional workshop → First Read (text locked) → Second Read (vote possible) → optional Third Read (vote mandatory). Hearings are the first and third Thursdays (Charter Art. II, § 7(a)); workshops have been running on second and fourth Thursdays. Legal review is advisory, not a gate, though César's practice is to hold First Read until legal has reviewed.

Minimum realistic clock for a City Code ordinance with no Planning Commission referral: one workshop plus First Read plus Second Read is about **six to eight weeks**. For an LMC amendment: Planning Commission hearing and recommendation first, then the same council sequence, realistically **four to six months**.

## 4. State-law limits that shape the agenda

### 4.1 Landlord-tenant preemption

The Real Property Article governs security deposits comprehensively (§ 8-203), and the City Attorney correctly removed deposit provisions from the Real Rent draft. Application fees are already regulated by **Real Property § 8-213**: fees over $25 must be returned within 15 days except the part "actually expended for a credit check or other expenses arising out of the application," with a double-damages penalty. That section exempts landlords offering four or fewer units at one location. Any local application-fee rule should cross-reference § 8-213 rather than restate it.

### 4.2 Private rights of action after Aleti

In *Aleti v. Metropolitan Baltimore, LLC*, 479 Md. 696 (2022), the Supreme Court of Maryland held that Baltimore's licensing ordinance created no implied private right of action to recover rent paid to an unlicensed landlord. The Court expressly declined to decide whether Baltimore had authority to create one, while noting that a home-rule jurisdiction "may establish a judicial cause of action ... only where the General Assembly gives a local government the authority to do so."

**Correction to the vault.** [[Real Rent Ordinance - Legal Review Prep]] reads Aleti as foreclosing only implied rights and treats the City Attorney's broader reading as an overstatement. The opinion's own language supports the City Attorney's caution. A tenant-enforced treble-damages provision is high risk. The better route is administrative restitution ordered by the City as a licensing condition, plus the tenant's existing remedy under the Maryland Consumer Protection Act for actual loss, which Aleti left intact. The Court also let stand claims to recover fees collected during unlicensed periods under money-had-and-received, which is worth noting in findings.

### 4.3 Utility shutoff notice law does not reach City water

Public Utilities Article § 7-309 requires 14-day "IMPORTANT NOTICE TO ALL OCCUPANTS" termination notices and lets tenants pay a landlord's utility bill and deduct it, but only for gas and electric service from PSC-regulated companies. It does not cover a municipal water system. Frederick's own Code § 25-2(a) sends shutoff notice only to "the owner of the property served." That gap is the City's to close, and nothing in state law prevents it.

### 4.4 Water affordability authority

Environment Article § 9-202, as amended by HB 923 (2018), lets a political subdivision "develop and implement service affordability programs to assist homeowners having difficulty making payments for water and sewer services," including payment plans and round-up programs. Frederick's Code § 25-12 already uses this for its round-up fund. The statute speaks of homeowners. Extending assistance to tenant households should rest on the general police power and a General Fund or donated funding source, and needs a City Attorney opinion.

### 4.5 Child care zoning preemption

Land Use Article § 4-216(b): "A local jurisdiction may not, by local ordinance, resolution, law, or rule, limit the number of children for which a family child care home or large family child care home provides family child care to below the number authorized by the State Department of Education." Education Article § 9.5-301 defines a family child care home as up to 8 children and a large family child care home as 9 to 12. LMC § 811(d) regulates homes caring for 8 to 12 children with a 1,000 square foot per child lot minimum, a one-nonresident-employee cap, and discretionary board approval. See [[Child Care Supply LMC Amendment]].

### 4.6 Housing Certainty Act vesting

HB 548 / SB 325 (2026), implemented locally by G-26-11 (passed 5-0 September 17, 2026), vests residential projects for five years from a complete application. Every LMC reform adopted later misses every project already filed. This is the clock behind the sequencing in [[Frederick Affordability Agenda 2026-2029]].

### 4.7 State bills that did not become law

Vault notes cite several state bills as if enacted. As of this research they are not.

- **HB 1257 (2025), residential lease fee disclosures.** Passed the House 135-4, stalled in Senate Judicial Proceedings. **HB 80 (2026)**, its successor, passed the House 94-35 on March 5, 2026 and was last recorded at a March 25 Senate hearing. [[Real Rent Ordinance Workshop Talking Points]] says HB 1257 "explicitly authorizes local jurisdictions to enact stronger protections." That is not an enacted authorization. Remove it from talking points.
- **HB 434 (2026), algorithmic rent-setting ban.** Heard in House committee in February. No enactment found.
- **HB 774 / SB 462 (Good Cause Eviction), HB 239 / SB 36 (Starter and Silver Homes), HB 778, HB 1538.** Failed, per MACo's end-of-session wrap-up and [[Housing Affordability Policy Brief -- 2026-09-08]].
- **HB 1259 (2026), family child care home zoning.** Went to conference committee. No enactment found. The existing § 4-216 is the operative law.

## 5. Relevant existing Code provisions, quoted or paraphrased

- **§ 19-6.1(b)(3)(A)-(D).** After three years of square-footage data, the Council "by ordinance or resolution will establish a formula" for the payment-in-lieu fee, based on an affordability gap (median attached home price vs. purchasing power at 70% FCAMI), multiplied by 12.5%, divided by average new-unit square footage. The $2.00 rate was set "as of November 2, 2023." The formula is mandatory language ("will establish"), which makes recalculation an obligation, not a new policy.
- **§ 19-18(b).** Ten authorized uses of the City Housing Fund, including rental assistance for households below MPDU incomes and LIHTC financing.
- **§ 12.5-76(a).** Rental licensing does not apply to HACF units, government-subsidized units inspected at least every five years, owner-occupied two-unit lots, **units with a certificate of occupancy no more than eight years old**, institutional facilities, and religious-order housing.
- **§ 12.5-83.** After revocation, tenants must vacate within 60 days. The City's only duty is to "provide tenants with information on available resources."
- **§ 12.5-85.** Anti-retaliation for actions taken under the licensing article.
- **§ 12.5-87.** Net rental licensing revenue splits 50% to a rental assistance fund and 50% to an affordable housing conservation fund, under HHS regulations subject to Council approval.
- **§ 25-1(d).** One percent per month penalty on water bills more than 30 days late.
- **§ 25-2.** Ten days' mailed notice to the owner, then discontinuance; unpaid charges become a first lien collectible like taxes; restoration only after the full balance, interest, penalties, and reconnection fee are paid.
- **§ 4-15(b)(1)(C).** Age-restricted "housing for older persons" is already exempt from the school APFO test. Precedent for treating deed-restricted affordable housing differently.
- **§ 8-6.** Homestead credit cap set at 105%.
