# Real Rent Ordinance Revisions

**Agenda Item 1 of 6.** Part of [[Frederick Affordability Agenda 2026-2029]]. Vehicle: **ordinance**, new Article of City Code Chapter 12.5. Sponsor: Díaz. Committee path: full council workshop already held July 9, 2026.

## Summary

Real Rent is the most advanced affordability ordinance César owns, and the closest to a vote. It is also carrying four problems that would weaken it on passage: it only covers licensed units, so it misses the newest corporate buildings where fee stacking is worst; its private enforcement clause rests on a reading of *Aleti* that the opinion itself does not support; its section numbers now collide with the Rodent Control ordinance; and its talking points cite state law that was never enacted. Each is fixable in a redraft. With the fixes, it is a strong five-vote ordinance that costs the City almost nothing to administer.

## Current status

- **Notion:** In Committee, Priority Critical, Legal Review Cleared unchecked. Blockers: private right of action (Aleti) and anti-circumvention.
- **Workshop:** July 9, 2026 full council workshop held ([[Workshop Brief 2026-07-09]]).
- **Draft on file:** [[Real Rent Ordinance]] (City Attorney's code-amendment format, §§ 12.5-89 to 12.5-99) and the earlier [[Rental Junk Fee Elimination Ordinance (Legally Hardened)]].
- **Scope boundary per César:** this item does not depend on Council Member Hempel Irani's rental licensing rewrite. It is drafted to stand on the licensing article as it exists today.

## Why it matters for affordability

Mandatory add-on fees (technology, "resident benefit packages," trash valet, pest, admin, payment processing, flat utility charges) function as unadvertised rent. For a household at Frederick's ALICE threshold, a $100 to $200 monthly add-on is the margin between on-time rent and a late fee. The ordinance does not cap rent. It makes the price the price, which is a consumer protection principle a majority of this council can support without a fight over rent regulation.

## The seven revisions

### Revision 1. Close the coverage hole

The draft defines "Covered Unit" as a unit "for which a rental housing license is required." Code § 12.5-76(a)(5) exempts from licensing any unit whose certificate of occupancy is **eight years old or less**. In practice that exempts the newest Class A buildings, which are the properties most likely to use third-party billing, amenity packages, and algorithmic pricing. It also exempts HACF units and subsidized units under § 12.5-76(a)(1)-(2), and owner-occupied two-unit lots under (a)(4).

**Fix.** Define Covered Unit as any rental housing unit in the City, using the § 12.5-72(e) definition, with narrow exceptions (owner-occupied single unit, government-owned units already governed by federal rent rules). Enforce against licensed units through the license (revocation, restitution as a condition) and against exempt units through municipal infraction fines and civil action under Charter Art. IV, § 2. This keeps the licensing-condition theory for the units it fits while reaching the units it misses.

### Revision 2. Replace the private right of action with City-ordered restitution

*Aleti* (Md. 2022) declined to decide whether a home-rule jurisdiction can create a judicial cause of action, and said such a cause may be created "only where the General Assembly gives a local government the authority to do so." The treble-damages private action in the original draft invites a threshold challenge that could take down enforcement entirely.

**Fix.** Three layers, none of which requires a new cause of action:

1. **Administrative restitution.** The Director may order refund of prohibited fees as a condition of license issuance, renewal, or continued licensure (already in draft § 12.5-96(b)), and for unlicensed units, as a term of any consent order in a municipal infraction case.
2. **Per-unit, per-month civil penalties** as drafted ($500 / $1,000 / $2,500), which the City Attorney did not object to.
3. **Findings that a prohibited fee is an actual loss** to the tenant. Tenants keep their existing remedy under the Maryland Consumer Protection Act, which *Aleti* left open on proof of actual injury, and the Attorney General's Consumer Protection Division can be the referral point. The ordinance can require landlords to post the Division's contact information with the Total Rent disclosure.

Takoma Park offers the adjudication model: its landlord-tenant chapter routes complaints to a Commission on Landlord-Tenant Affairs rather than to a private lawsuit. Frederick does not need a new commission. The Technical Code Appeals Board already hears licensing appeals under § 12.5-82.

### Revision 3. Restore the one-sentence anti-circumvention rule

The City Attorney removed the anti-circumvention section as unnecessary. The narrow, defensible ask from [[Real Rent Ordinance - Legal Review Prep]] stands: "The substance and effect of a charge, not its label or the identity of the person collecting it, determines whether it is a Mandatory Fee." Add one clause making the landlord responsible for fees charged by an affiliate or vendor the landlord requires the tenant to use. This closes the resident-benefit-package route through a third-party billing company.

### Revision 4. Add an algorithmic rent-setting prohibition

Rockville's Mayor and Council voted unanimously in July 2026 to rewrite its landlord-tenant code, including a ban on using an "algorithmic device" to set rent, fees, or lease terms (Rockville City Code § 18-148, effective January 1, 2027). The ban exempts landlords with one unit in the city, government affordable housing programs, and aggregated anonymized market reports. Montgomery County's Bill 8-26 proposes the same, with Class A violations at $500, rising to $750. The U.S. Department of Justice settled its antitrust case with RealPage on November 24, 2025, requiring RealPage to stop using competitors' nonpublic data to set prices, so the practice is already under federal scrutiny. Maryland's HB 434 (2026) would have banned it statewide and did not pass.

**Fix.** Add a section, modeled on Rockville § 18-148, prohibiting landlords of Covered Units from using a device that pools nonpublic rent data from two or more landlords to recommend rent or lease terms. Keep Rockville's three exemptions. Enforce through the same penalty schedule. This is the second Maryland city to do it, not the first, which is politically easier.

### Revision 5. Utility billing rules that match the water item

Draft § 12.5-95 already prohibits flat-rate utility fees not in Total Rent and bans admin fees on utility charges. Add two things:

- **Allocation disclosure.** Where a landlord bills water or sewer by ratio (RUBS) rather than submeter, the landlord must give the tenant the formula and, on request, the building's actual City water bill for the period. Takoma Park goes further and bars shifting utilities to tenants unless units are separately metered and rent is reduced to offset. That is a stronger model to hold in reserve.
- **Shutoff pass-through.** A landlord may not use a City water shutoff caused by the landlord's own nonpayment as grounds for any charge to the tenant. This pairs with the occupant-notice provision in [[Water and Sewer Affordability Ordinance]].

### Revision 6. Fix the application fee section

Draft § 12.5-94(a) invents its own application fee rule. Maryland already regulates it: Real Property § 8-213 requires return of fees over $25, less amounts "actually expended for a credit check or other expenses arising out of the application," with double damages, but exempts landlords with four or fewer units at a location. **Fix:** cross-reference § 8-213 for the landlords it covers, and apply the actual-cost rule by licensing condition to the small landlords § 8-213 exempts. That is additive rather than conflicting.

### Revision 7. Renumber and correct the record

- **Section numbers.** Rodent Control (G-26-6, passed 6-0 August 6, 2026) now occupies Chapter 12.5, Article VI, including §§ 12.5-91, 12.5-92, and 12.5-95. The Real Rent draft uses §§ 12.5-89 to 12.5-99 in the same article. Redesignate Real Rent as Article VII with new numbers. The Clerk and Rachel Gray should assign.
- **Talking points.** [[Real Rent Ordinance Workshop Talking Points]] states that HB 1257 authorizes stronger local protections and that Rachel Gray "has completed legal review." HB 1257 (2025) and its successor HB 80 (2026) both stalled in the Senate, and Notion shows Legal Review Cleared unchecked. Both lines should come out before the next public session.
- **Peer research.** [[Real Rent Ordinance - Maryland Peer Jurisdiction Research]] describes Montgomery County Bill 6-25 as a junk fee ordinance. It is not. Bill 6-25 (enacted April 2025) removed the landlord-tenant exemption from the county's consumer protection law so the county can sue landlords with patterns of deceptive practices and code neglect. It is a useful model for enforcement, not for fee structure. The in-state fee-structure models are Takoma Park (Chapter 6.16) and Rockville (Chapter 18).
- **Effective date.** The draft says 120 days after adoption for new leases and renewals. The talking points say January 1, 2027. Pick one. With the timeline below, 120 days after a March 2027 adoption lands around July 2027, which lines up with most lease renewal cycles.

### Optional companion: relocation assistance

The rental licensing article ends at "the City will provide tenants with information on available resources" (§ 12.5-83(d)) when a license is revoked and tenants must leave in 60 days. Rockville's rewrite expands relocation assistance for tenants displaced by code violations. A short section requiring the owner of a revoked or condemned unit to pay relocation assistance (for example, a fixed amount per unit indexed to Fair Market Rent), recoverable by the City as a lien if the owner does not pay, would stop enforcement from becoming a displacement event. It touches Article V, which is the subject of the separate licensing rewrite, so hold it as a Phase 2 amendment unless the rewrite stalls. See [[What Happens After License Revocation -- The Missing Piece in Frederick's Tenant Protection Stack]].

## Legal risk summary

| Provision | Risk | Why |
|---|---|---|
| All-in pricing, disclosure, prohibited fees | Low to moderate | Regulates advertising and licensing conditions, not rent levels. Takoma Park regulates permitted and prohibited fees in its Chapter 6.16 (amended by Ordinances 2013-25 and 2021-49). |
| Coverage of unlicensed units | Moderate | Relies on general police power rather than the licensing hook. Charter Art. II, § 4 and Art. IV, § 1 support it. |
| Algorithmic pricing ban | Moderate | Novel in Maryland outside Rockville. No state preemption found. |
| Private right of action | High | *Aleti*. Removed in this revision. |
| Security deposits | Preempted | Real Property § 8-203. Stays out. |

## Politics and votes

The ordinance has been framed from the start as "it does not tell landlords what to charge, it tells them to be honest about it," which is the right frame. Likely yes: Díaz, Nash, Hempel Irani, Taylor. Swing: Shackelford (the workshop prep flagged enforceability and landlord impact as his questions), Brehm (has argued licensing is already overburdened), Adkins. Five votes are needed to survive a veto. Shackelford plus one of Brehm or Adkins gets there. The coverage expansion (Revision 1) is the provision most likely to draw landlord-association opposition, because it reaches new buildings. The answer is that the newest buildings are where the fees are.

The September 17 public criticism of Nash by Hempel Irani and Shackelford is a coalition risk. Real Rent is César's bill, not Nash's, which insulates it somewhat.
