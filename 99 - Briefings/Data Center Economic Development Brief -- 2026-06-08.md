# Data Center Economic Development Brief -- 2026-06-08

## Summary

Briefing note prepared for the June 12 work session presentation by Richard Griffin, Director of Economic Development, on the economic impact of data centers. Griffin will make the broad fiscal case for the industry. This note surfaces the key gaps in that argument and prepares for informed engagement.

## Bottom Line

Richard Griffin will present the case for data centers as an economic development priority for Frederick. The presentation is likely to draw on the same arguments Trammell Crow has been making in support of the Nexus ZTA: high assessed values, significant real property tax revenue, construction jobs, and multiplier effects. The fiscal case is not fabricated, but it has meaningful gaps that the council should understand before it becomes the dominant frame for evaluating the ZTA. The most important gap is that the revenue projections are built almost entirely on real property tax from building shells, while the much larger equipment investment inside those buildings pays nothing to the city under current law.

## What the Fiscal Argument Actually Says

The core argument is that data centers assess at far higher values than warehouses or conventional industrial uses, generating substantially more real property tax revenue. The Sage Policy Group fiscal impact study commissioned by Trammell Crow estimates the city would collect approximately $13.7 million annually in real property tax at full buildout of the Nexus site under an all-data-center scenario, compared to approximately $2.7 million under an all-warehouse scenario. That $11 million annual delta is the heart of the fiscal case.

The argument is valid as far as it goes. Data centers do cost far more to build than warehouses -- Sage uses $900 per square foot versus $110 per square foot -- and Maryland's assessment methodology for commercial property is tied to construction and replacement cost. Higher cost means higher assessed value means higher tax revenue.

## Where the Argument Has Limits

**Personal property exemption.** The $13.7 million figure captures only real property tax on the building shell. The equipment inside a data center -- servers, cooling systems, networking infrastructure -- is personal property, not real property. All personal property is currently exempt from the city's personal property tax of $1.55 per $100 of assessed value. For a data center, the equipment investment dwarfs the building cost. The Sage report confirms this exemption in a footnote but does not model what revenue would look like if it were reversed. Under current law, the city captures only a fraction of the total investment value.

**Full buildout is a best-case projection.** The $13.7 million figure assumes all eight buildings come online. Data centers are built in phases tied to tenant commitments and power infrastructure. There is no public information about signed anchor tenants or confirmed utility capacity for the Nexus site. The revenue ramp-up could take a decade or more, and partial buildout would produce proportionally lower revenue.

**The comparison baseline.** Sage compares data centers to warehouses. That is the most favorable comparison available to the applicant. The honest comparison is data centers versus whatever would realistically get built on M1/M2 land in Frederick's current market. The city does not have independent data on the average assessed value of existing M1/M2 properties or the total real property tax those zones currently generate. Without that baseline, the delta is difficult to independently verify.

**Multiplier effects.** Griffin is likely to cite job multiplier figures similar to those in the PwC national report, which estimates each direct data center job supports 4.5 additional jobs in the broader economy. These figures use IMPLAN input-output modeling, which is the industry standard but is also widely criticized for producing inflated projections when used in developer-funded studies. The multiplier captures indirect and induced effects that may shift economic activity from elsewhere in the region rather than creating net new activity.

**Jobs tradeoff.** The all-data-center scenario supports the fewest ongoing jobs -- approximately 576 at full buildout -- compared to 1,143 for an all-warehouse scenario. Data center jobs pay significantly more on average (approaching $100,000 versus approximately $50,000), but fewer residents benefit. For a city where 43% of households are below the ALICE threshold, the distribution of employment opportunity matters alongside the aggregate fiscal picture.

## The Floating Zone Question

The ZTA as drafted makes data centers a by-right permitted use in all M1 and M2 zones meeting the 75-acre minimum. This means the council's role ends if and when the ZTA passes -- future applicants build without legislative review. The city's own planning staff recommended a floating zone framework instead, which would require a rezoning action for each project and keep the council in the decision. Griffin's presentation is unlikely to address this structural question, but it is the most consequential policy choice embedded in the application.

## Unresolved Policy Questions Flagged by Planning Staff

Planning staff identified four areas requiring further city evaluation before the ZTA is ready for council action. These remain unresolved.

Water consumption at full buildout and whether the city has authority to condition approval on usage limits. Clean energy requirements and whether state law preempts local conditioning of land use approval on energy source. Business personal property tax applicability and the extent to which the city can capture revenue from equipment. And the floating zone versus permitted use question addressed above.

## Political Context

Griffin presents to the council at the administration's direction. His presentation reflects the Mayor's office posture, which has been supportive of economic development projects of this scale. The presentation is not a legislative action -- no vote is taken -- but it shapes the frame for how the council receives the ZTA when it arrives, likely in August.

The Trammell Crow application is the implicit context for the presentation even if Griffin does not reference it directly. The arguments he makes will be the same arguments the applicant has been making at the Planning Commission. Understanding the gaps in those arguments before Thursday puts César in a stronger position to ask the questions that are not being asked.
