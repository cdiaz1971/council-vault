# Civic Brief - 051926

## This Week

Participatory budgeting is producing concrete evidence that it works, not just as a civic ritual but as a driver of broader democratic engagement. The New York City numbers this cycle are significant: over 93,000 residents cast ballots to decide how to spend $30 million of the city's 2026 budget. For context, NYC runs PB in two forms simultaneously. District-level PB lets residents direct their council member's discretionary funds toward specific neighborhood projects, while the citywide People's Money program gives voters power over community service funding including immigrant legal aid, youth enrichment, and women's programs. Together they constitute the largest participatory budgeting program in the country.

The research finding worth holding is that PB participation is associated with an 8.4 percentage point increase in regular election turnout among participants, with the effect strongest among the populations with the lowest baseline participation rates: young people, lower-income residents, and people with less formal education. This is not a small number. It suggests that PB is functioning as a gateway into sustained civic engagement, not just a one-off consultation event. For cities watching civic trust erode and voter participation stagnate, this matters.

Cambridge completed its 12th PB cycle in March 2026 with a ballot available in 10 languages including Amharic, Arabic, Bengali, Chinese, Haitian Creole, Hindi, Japanese, Portuguese, and Spanish. Eligibility extended to all residents 12 and older regardless of citizenship status. That language and eligibility framework is the design model that makes PB genuinely inclusive rather than a more elaborate version of the same civic engagement that already works for people who already engage. The Cambridge model is small enough to be replicable in a city Frederick's size.

The NYC Civic Engagement Commission launched the fourth cycle of People's Money in January 2026 with borough assemblies where randomly selected participants deliberate on proposals before a citywide ballot. That deliberation layer is what distinguishes PB from a simple online survey. Residents do not just vote on pre-determined options; they develop, refine, and evaluate proposals in structured conversation with city staff. The proposals that emerged from the Bronx this cycle included college prep, career readiness, and healthy eating knowledge programs, reflecting community priorities that would be invisible in a traditional budget process.

The Canadian experience, written up in Policy Options, offers a useful scaling argument. Vancouver, Victoria, and Montreal each ran PB programs in 2025 and 2026 at different scales. Montreal's third year showed that when the dollar amount increases, civic energy scales proportionally. The recommended starting point for cities new to PB: at least one percent of the capital budget, with a multi-year runway so projects can move from proposal to construction to celebration within a community's memory. That timeline matters for sustaining participation.

The National Civic League's All-America City Award for 2026 is themed around the nation's 250th anniversary and explicitly spotlights communities that have advanced participatory budgeting, community-building programs like neighborhood mini-grants, and storytelling projects. The award criteria are worth reading because they reflect where the national field is placing its attention: communities that are rebuilding trust between residents and government through repeated, meaningful co-governance rather than periodic consultation.

## Frederick Implications

Frederick has not implemented formal participatory budgeting, and this week's research makes the case that it should at least be piloted. The City Clerk budget note in the vault flags civic participation as a priority area for FY2027. A modest pilot using a small slice of discretionary council funds with an open voting process that includes non-citizen residents could test the model and begin building the civic infrastructure that larger PB programs depend on.

District 2 is the natural starting geography for a pilot. It has the city's highest concentration of immigrants, many of whom are eligible participants under any citizenship-neutral eligibility rule, and it includes neighborhoods where the gap between civic engagement and civic need is widest. A PB process that yields visible, funded improvements in District 2 neighborhoods within a year of launch would do more for community trust than most communications strategies.

The connection to the No Kings work is worth naming. The participatory budgeting research is producing evidence that face-to-face deliberation with real stakes increases durable civic engagement. That is the same insight driving the assembly model referenced in the PAH organizing note and the Springs-to-River coalition work. PB is the institutional form of that insight; it gives people something concrete to do with their civic energy and something they can point to as having changed because they showed up.

The budget work session schedule for FY2027 is the right moment to introduce PB formally. Even a resolution directing staff to design a pilot would put the idea on record and begin building internal administrative capacity. The Cambridge model is small enough that Frederick could adapt it without outside consultants.
