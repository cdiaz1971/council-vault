# Housing Brief - 051926

## This Week

The central story in municipal housing this week is supply-side reform, and the cities doing it seriously are generating measurable results. Dallas is the leading case study. The city already permitted more homes than most US metros, yet 72 percent of renters with mid-range incomes remained cost-burdened as recently as 2022, with a rental deficit of nearly 40,000 units affordable to households at 50 percent AMI. The city's response was to look directly at the codes creating that paradox: parking requirements and building standards that effectively blocked smaller, more affordable projects. When a senior housing project was allowed to expand without adding 200 required parking spaces, it preserved two trees residents cared about. That detail matters because it shows the human texture behind what reads as dry regulatory reform.

The National League of Cities panel from Salt Lake City captured what Dallas illustrates nationally: housing affordability is no longer a coastal problem. It is a workforce housing challenge in smaller regions that previously assumed the crisis didn't apply to them. The American Planning Association's Jason Jordan said there is no region immune from this discussion now. That framing matters for Frederick, which sometimes gets treated as an outlier in the regional housing conversation.

On the legal and regulatory architecture side, the Urban Institute analysis of the two major federal housing bills moving through Congress is worth attention. Both the ROAD Act (Senate) and H21 (House) include mechanisms to reward or penalize local jurisdictions based on housing supply growth, using CDBG funds as the lever. The Urban Institute calculates that the median community receiving a ROAD bonus would get roughly $55,000 a year, which is not transformative. But the signal is: the federal government is actively exploring using transportation funding as the larger lever. That is a significant shift in the relationship between federal infrastructure money and local zoning authority.

The DC Policy Center published an important critique of inclusionary zoning as a production tool this week. In 17 years of operation, DC's IZ program has produced fewer than 300 affordable units per year. The analysis concludes that IZ increases market rents, narrows developer feasibility, and produces units only when market-rate development is already strong. The DC policy recommendation is to suspend IZ automatically when permits fall below defined thresholds and to allow flexibility on AMI targeting rather than holding to rigid set-aside rules. This does not mean abandoning IZ, but it is a serious evidence-based case that IZ alone cannot carry the affordability load, and that mandating it too aggressively during weak market periods chokes the supply it was meant to subsidize.

The NAHRO analysis of zoning's racialized history grounds this week's technical discussion in political reality. Restrictive zoning has consistently been deployed to maintain racial segregation and wealth concentration in high-opportunity areas. In major cities, 70 percent of residential land bans or restricts apartments. That legacy is not incidental to the current crisis. Houston's experience as a largely unzoned city, building apartments at 14 times the rate of comparable metros, represents a meaningful data point, though its governance context differs significantly from cities with strong neighborhood organization and historic preservation commitments like Frederick.

The Mercatus Center policy brief for 2026 state legislative sessions identifies several reforms cities should be watching for from Annapolis: HOA statute reform to enable small-scale multi-unit HOAs (following Tennessee's model), regulatory certainty protection so that zoning cannot shift after a developer has secured permits and financing, and blanket grandfathering of existing noncompliant structures on blocks where more than a quarter of buildings are already out of code. That last proposal is directly applicable to Frederick's older residential neighborhoods and would significantly ease infill development.

## Frederick Implications

The LMC reform process is where this week's research lands most directly. Frederick is actively working through zoning amendments, and the national conversation supports being ambitious. The evidence from Dallas, Houston, and the APA is that parking minimums and building code complexity are among the highest-leverage targets for increasing housing production without requiring new public subsidy. The LMC work session materials and Libby Taylor's modernization proposal align with exactly this national consensus.

The IZ critique from the DC Policy Center is directly relevant to Frederick's MPDU program. MPDU reform is on the legislative agenda, and the evidence suggests that flexibility on AMI tiers and automatic suspension during slow permit periods would produce more affordable units over time than rigid set-aside percentages. This is worth raising explicitly in the housing committee discussion.

The federal legislation trajectory matters for Frederick's CDBG allocation. If Congress moves toward conditioning transportation funding on housing production, that creates both a threat and an opportunity: a threat if Frederick does not hit production targets, and an opportunity to argue for expanded regional housing authority or state-level production mandate coverage as protection against federal pressure landing unevenly on smaller cities.

The production target resolution that is active in the council should incorporate the language from these national models. Dallas's housing action plan explicitly quantified the deficit and set time-bound targets. Frederick having a clear number to work from strengthens the political case for every subsequent amendment the council makes to the LMC.
