# Data Center Moratorium Policy Brief -- 2026-05-21

## Executive Summary

The City of Frederick is not looking at a hypothetical future problem. The council has an active LMC text amendment before it that would make data centers a permitted by-right use in M1 and M2 zones. If that amendment passes without a full regulatory framework in place, Frederick loses its leverage permanently. The city's Planning Department has already taken the administrative position that data centers are not currently permitted because the LMC contains no definition for the use. A moratorium formalizes and legally hardens that position, halts the text amendment clock, and creates the space to build regulations that actually protect residents.

The legal authority to do this is solid. Maryland courts upheld temporary zoning moratoria in 1985 and 1988. The U.S. Supreme Court blessed them in 2002. A Maryland county attorney opinion from 2020 analyzed nearly identical facts and concluded a temporary use-specific moratorium is well within local zoning authority. The substantive basis is documented and growing: power transmission constraints that could take a decade to resolve, Potomac River water consumption projections that put Frederick in a regional stress picture, noise and air quality impacts documented in peer communities, and a Maryland state impact study due September 1, 2026 that the General Assembly itself passed on bipartisan grounds.

The moratorium is not a ban. It is a pause to do the work. The choice is not between allowing data centers and blocking them. The choice is between a structured regulatory development process and a text amendment that locks in inadequate rules under development pressure.

---

## I. Frederick's Actual Baseline: The Text Amendment and the LMC Gap

The starting point for any moratorium analysis is what is actually happening in Frederick, not the abstract national picture.

### The Current Permitted Use Posture

As of October 2025, the city's Planning Department confirmed that new data centers are not currently permitted in Frederick because the LMC contains no definition for a data center or similar use. Without a defined use category, the Planning Department has taken the position that proposals cannot be processed. This is an administrative interpretation, not enacted law. It is defensible but fragile: it can be reversed by a change in Planning Department leadership, a developer's legal challenge, or a council vote.

### The Text Amendment Is the Live Threat

The council has before it a proposed LMC text amendment that would create a data center use definition and make data centers a permitted by-right use in M1 (Light Industrial) and M2 (Heavy Industrial) zones. Frederick County amended its zoning code in 2022 to allow data centers in limited and general industrial zones. The city is now facing the same pressure. Mobilize Frederick's Government Operations Committee submitted a detailed technical research memo to the full City Council on April 3, 2026, explicitly framing it as input to the text amendment deliberation.

The December 19, 2025 council discussion on data center legislation produced a direct warning that belongs at the center of the moratorium case: reactive legislation written in response to a specific project is weaker, more politically difficult, and more legally vulnerable than proactive legislation enacted before any application is pending. The window to act proactively is now. If the text amendment passes first, the regulatory leverage shifts to applicants.

### The Sequencing Logic

The correct sequence from the December 2025 council discussion is: establish authority before applications arrive, wait for the state impact study, then enact the full regulatory framework. The moratorium is the mechanism that holds this sequence together. Without it, the text amendment can advance and collapse the sequence.

## II. Legal Authority

### Maryland Statutory Basis

Maryland municipalities exercise zoning authority under Maryland Code, Local Government Article §5-213 and Maryland Code, Land Use Article §4-101 et seq. The Land Use Article authorizes the legislative body to "regulate the construction, alteration, repair, or use of buildings, structures, or land" under §4-201(b), and permits a local jurisdiction to "impose any additional conditions or limitations that the legislative body considers appropriate to improve or protect the general character and design of the land and improvements being zoned or rezoned, or the surrounding or adjacent land and improvements" under §4-103(a).

The General Assembly has explicitly recognized that local zoning authority "will displace or limit economic competition by owners and users of property through the planning and zoning controls set forth in this division." The state anticipated that local zoning would restrict property use and accepted that consequence.

### Maryland Precedent on Temporary Moratoria

Two Maryland appellate decisions directly support a temporary zoning moratorium:

**Friel v. Triangle Oil Co., 76 Md. App. 96 (1988):** A temporary zoning moratorium of less than nine months was upheld as a legitimate exercise of local police power.

**Ungar v. State, 63 Md. App 472 (1985):** A temporary sewer moratorium was upheld on similar grounds.

No Maryland appellate court has invalidated a narrowly tailored, time-limited moratorium since these decisions. The 2020 St. Mary's County Attorney analysis of a temporary moratorium on utility-scale solar projects -- an exact structural parallel -- concluded that such a moratorium is within the authority granted under Maryland Code Land Use §4-201(b) and §4-103(a), and recommended a six-month duration with proper procedural form.

The City of Laurel enacted a moratorium in 2024 on zoning requests and text amendments while updating its Comprehensive Plan, grounding the action in Maryland Land Use Code Ann. §3-301's mandate for periodic master plan revision. This demonstrates that Maryland municipalities use moratoria routinely and that courts have not moved to restrict that authority.

### Federal Constitutional Framework

The controlling U.S. Supreme Court case is **Tahoe-Sierra Preservation Council, Inc. v. Tahoe Regional Planning Agency, 535 U.S. 302 (2002)**. The Court upheld a 32-month development ban composed of two consecutive moratoria while a comprehensive plan was being developed. It rejected a facial takings challenge and held that the appropriate analysis is the Penn Central factors: the economic impact of the government action, the extent to which it interferes with distinct investment-backed expectations, and the character of the governmental action.

The Court's language is directly useful for Frederick's council record: moratoria "are used widely among land-use planners to preserve the status quo while formulating a more permanent development strategy. In fact, the consensus in the planning community appears to be that moratoria, or 'interim development controls' as they are often called, are an essential tool of successful development." The Court further observed that a per se rule requiring compensation during all moratoria "may force officials to rush through the planning process or to abandon the practice altogether."

Post-Tahoe-Sierra challenges to moratoria have generally been upheld in other jurisdictions. Courts have invalidated moratoria only where the original brief pause was extended for multiple years without genuine regulatory progress, constituting an unconstitutional "rolling moratorium."

### The Takings Defense

Property owners and developers will argue the moratorium constitutes a taking. The response is direct. A moratorium on one category of use does not deprive property owners of all economically viable use of their land. Property in M1 or M2 zoning retains all other permitted uses under the LMC while the moratorium is in effect. That fact is the core of the Penn Central analysis and defeats a facial takings challenge.

The "vested rights" argument applies only to applicants who have already submitted a completed application before the moratorium is enacted. The city has no such applications pending.

### The One Critical Procedural Risk

The St. Mary's County Attorney analysis flagged one case where a moratorium was invalidated not on constitutional grounds but on procedural ones: it was enacted as an emergency amendment to the zoning ordinance through a process not permitted under the county's procedural rules. Frederick's moratorium must follow the correct procedural path for LMC amendments, including required public hearings. City Attorney Rachel Gray's review is the appropriate next step to confirm the correct vehicle and process.

## III. Frederick-Specific Substantive Justification

The national research on data center harms -- energy grid strain, water consumption, noise, air quality -- is real and documented in vault notes. What the first draft of this brief missed entirely is that Frederick has its own specific, locally documented grounds that are stronger than any generic national argument.

### A. The Power Transmission Problem: A Decade Away

The most important Frederick-specific fact in the vault: power reaching the city depends on reduction through the Doubs Substation. Additional transmission lines to support significant new data center load could take up to ten years to bring online, given that Northern Virginia grid priority takes precedence in regional allocation. Maryland already imports approximately 40% of its power; the hyperscale data center complex already operating at Adamstown is compounding this dependency.

This creates a specific local harm that national research does not capture. Approving a data center use in M1 and M2 zones without resolving power supply does not create a data center industry. It creates an expectation problem: developers and property owners act on the approval, pursue sites, incur costs, and then discover the power is not available for a decade. The moratorium protects the city from building a regulatory approval that cannot be operationalized on any foreseeable timeline. Only facilities with existing site plan approval have any assurance of power availability. The Federal Energy Regulatory Commission has taken the position that data centers should provide their own generation -- for a city without the generating resources that attract hyperscale operators, this compounds the problem further.

The December 19, 2025 council discussion identified this as the central constraint: the ten-year transmission timeline is the single most important fact to communicate to any developer or advocate who raises data center interest in Frederick.

### B. Potomac River Water Consumption: Frederick Is Already Named

The vault's Potomac River Basin water analysis provides specific, citable, local figures. The Interstate Commission on the Potomac River Basin (ICPRB) published an analysis in March 2026 that explicitly names Frederick County as a site of rapid data center expansion. The Potomac River supplies 75% of regional drinking water for approximately 5 million people and is the sole drinking water source for Washington D.C. and Arlington County.

Data centers in the Washington Metropolitan Area currently consume approximately 4 million gallons per day on average, surging to 15 million gallons at summer peak -- nearly three times the annual average, at precisely the point when river flows are lowest. Despite representing only 1% of total withdrawals in the basin, data centers account for 9% of annual consumptive use and up to 12% in summer months. With over 100 million square feet of additional development planned regionally, ICPRB projects WMA data center peak demand could exceed 80 million gallons per day by 2050.

The regulatory gap is specific and actionable: data centers in the basin are served by public water utilities and currently hold no direct withdrawal permits, placing them outside existing consumptive use regulations. Frederick has the local permitting authority to fill that gap. A moratorium preserves the ability to impose those conditions through the full ordinance. A text amendment without those conditions forecloses them.

The Mobilize Frederick research memo adds a dimension the ICPRB study notes but does not foreground: the indirect water footprint of grid electricity is additional to the on-site consumption figures. A data center's draw on thermoelectric generation creates an upstream water impact that efficiency measures at the facility level do not eliminate.

### C. Cooling Technology: The "It's Not Available" Objection Is False

Developers commonly represent that water-efficient cooling is expensive or unavailable. The vault's cooling technology note, drawn from the same Mobilize Frederick research memo, documents that this is not accurate.

Immersion cooling, direct-to-chip liquid cooling, and air-side economization are commercially deployed at scale by major operators. A 2025 Nature life cycle assessment found immersion cooling reduces freshwater consumption by 31-52% compared to traditional systems, reduces energy demand by 15-20%, and pays back capital costs within two to five years -- faster for high-density AI workloads. Microsoft's next-generation data center design achieves zero water consumption for cooling using direct-to-chip systems and is in active deployment. Google operates facilities with water use efficiency values as low as 0.1 liters per kilowatt-hour at some locations, compared to the industry average of 1.8 liters per kilowatt-hour for evaporative systems.

Approximately 40% of Potomac Basin data centers already rely exclusively on air cooling. Requiring disclosure of cooling technology at permit application and mandating closed-loop or zero-water systems for new builds is technically defensible and economically grounded. The moratorium is the period during which Frederick develops these specific requirements.

### D. Noise and Air Quality: The Aero Derivative Problem

The March 2026 community correspondence forwarded by Betty Law and Jim Ballard, on file in the vault, provides technical vocabulary that belongs in the council record. The noise impact at nearby data centers -- documented in Vineland, NJ and Sterling, VA -- frequently originates from aero-derivative turbogenerators: commercial jet engines adapted for power generation. These are portable, fast to deploy, and routinely used during data center construction and early operation to get power quickly before grid connection is established. They are loud, inefficient, and more polluting than utility-grade combustion turbines. The difference is significant: aero-derivatives combust inside the turbine (gas turbines), while utility-grade combustion turbines combust outside. The former are substantially worse on noise, efficiency, and emissions.

The correspondence specifically flagged the facility referenced as "Atmosphere" as one to track for generator configuration. Any data center permitted in Frederick without generator technology requirements creates the same conditions that have produced residential complaints and documented health impacts in peer communities. The Ohio moratorium experience confirms that enforceable decibel limits and power draw caps are the most durable technical standards to put in ordinance language -- measurable, enforceable, and harder to litigate around than outright bans.

### E. The Maryland State Impact Study Is Not Complete

Maryland HB 270/SB 116, passed on a bipartisan veto override on December 16, 2025, requires the Maryland Department of the Environment, the Maryland Energy Administration, and the University of Maryland School of Business to produce a comprehensive environmental, energy, and economic impact analysis of data center development in the state. The report is due to the Governor and General Assembly by September 1, 2026.

The study will assess air and water quality impacts, bay restoration implications, energy demand and supply effects, grid infrastructure needs, the state's ability to meet greenhouse gas commitments, and economic and fiscal impacts including effects on state and local revenues and job creation. State lawmakers representing Frederick County urged the county to delay major data center decisions until this study is complete.

A moratorium running through or shortly after the September 2026 study date is directly aligned with the General Assembly's own reasoning for commissioning the study. Proceeding with a text amendment before that data exists is precisely what the legislature determined was premature.

As of May 12, 2026, Governor Moore signed the Utility RELIEF Act, which creates a state-level data center tariff to recover grid infrastructure costs from operators who impose those costs on ratepayers. Maryland has now established the legislative principle that data centers must bear infrastructure costs. This strengthens the case for Frederick's own cost-recovery requirements and the community benefit agreement framework to be developed during the moratorium period.

## IV. Equity Grounding

The original brief gestured at equity through utility cost arguments. The vault supports a stronger, more specific claim.

Frederick's city ALICE rate is 43%, meaning more than four in ten city households cannot afford basic necessities at the local cost of living threshold. Utility cost increases driven by data center energy demand fall disproportionately on lower-income renters -- the households least able to absorb bill increases and least able to leave for lower-cost housing elsewhere.

The data center and affordable housing connection runs deeper than utility bills. The synthesis note in the vault on data centers versus affordable housing documents that data center tax incentives divert local revenue that would otherwise be available for housing programs, that data center infrastructure investment competes for capital budget capacity that could support housing-related infrastructure, and that data center water and power demand creates operating cost pressures that squeeze affordable housing operators already facing structural stress. A data center ordinance that includes no community benefit requirements is also housing policy -- it forecloses contribution requirements that could capitalize the city's housing programs.

This is the policy context in which a text amendment without a full regulatory framework lands. Frederick does not face a tradeoff between data centers and nothing. It faces a tradeoff between regulated data center development that extracts community benefit and unregulated development that extracts resources while leaving costs with residents.

## V. Peer City Precedents

### What Works

**City of Madison, Wisconsin:** A 12-month moratorium enacted while staff developed zoning code definitions and land use standards for data centers. The moratorium included a concrete staff work plan with milestones and terminated automatically upon adoption of permanent regulations. This is the model for Frederick: time-limited, tied to a specific regulatory deliverable, with a defined end date.

**Larimer County, Colorado:** A temporary moratorium adopted in January 2026 and extended through August 2026 to allow staff adequate time to develop land use regulations addressing data centers' specific infrastructure impacts.

**Ohio (multiple jurisdictions):** Approximately fifteen to eighteen Ohio municipalities have enacted or are considering moratoriums. The most instructive lesson from Ohio is operational: Vienna Township used its moratorium period to develop enforceable decibel limits and megawatt caps -- specific technical standards that are measurable, defensible, and durable. Lordstown enacted a blanket ban, got sued, and had to retreat to a temporary moratorium. The lesson is that performance standards survive litigation better than categorical bans.

**Maine (Westbrook and others):** In the absence of a statewide moratorium (Governor Mills vetoed it), a patchwork of local moratoriums is forming. Westbrook's draft ordinance grounds the moratorium in inadequacy of current land use rules to prevent serious public harm from data center development.

### What Fails

**Charlotte, North Carolina:** The council attempted a data center moratorium. The mayor used tie-breaking executive authority to kill it. This is the most important cautionary precedent for Frederick.

Mayor O'Connor is described in the council's own political intelligence as likely oppositional on housing and, by extension, on policies that restrict developer latitude. If the moratorium requires mayoral signature and cannot pass on a council override, the Charlotte outcome is the Frederick risk. Before moving forward, the vote structure question must be answered: does a moratorium ordinance require the mayor's signature? What is the veto override threshold? City Attorney Gray's procedural review should address this directly. If a veto is a real risk, the strategic posture shifts -- the more durable alternative path may be a council resolution establishing the intent to regulate, combined with Planning Department guidance on permit processing, rather than an ordinance requiring executive sign-off.

**New Jersey (anonymous township):** A chaotic public meeting produced a total data center ban. The ban forecloses any community benefit agreement, any tax revenue capture, and any conditional approval framework. This is the outcome that results when the council has no proactive framework and a hot public meeting forces a binary vote. The moratorium prevents this by creating the structured process that keeps the council in control of the outcome.

## VI. The Negotiating Leverage Argument

The vault note from Good Jobs First frames the data center situation in terms that belong in the council record. The five largest tech companies announced over $710 billion in AI capital expenditure for 2026. Companies in that kind of buildout are not making location decisions based on whether a county waives a tax abatement. They need specific infrastructure conditions that are increasingly scarce near major markets: land, power, fiber, and local approvals. That dependency gives cities far more bargaining power than site-selection consultants want elected officials to believe.

The standard developer playbook -- LLC concealment, non-disclosure agreements, consultant-manufactured urgency, threats to take the project elsewhere -- is designed to exhaust leverage before residents know it exists. By the time a project becomes public, the site is selected, utilities are lined up, and zoning is nearly settled. The public's leverage is spent before the public knew it could have any.

The Virginia pattern, documented in the vault's race-to-the-bottom note, shows where this leads: localities approve rezoning under pressure, often at the edges of agricultural or historically significant lands, under NDAs that block public input, with no enforceable community benefit requirements, and with sales and use tax exemptions that amounted to $928 million in avoided taxes in Virginia alone in 2023. Frederick does not have to follow that pattern. The moratorium is the instrument that keeps the leverage intact while the regulatory framework is built.

## VII. Drafting Requirements for Legal Defensibility

Any moratorium ordinance must include the following elements to survive challenge.

**Specific factual findings.** Each substantive ground must appear as a finding in the WHEREAS recitals. Do not rely on general references. Name the ICPRB March 2026 water analysis. Cite the ten-year transmission constraint. Reference Maryland HB 270/SB 116 by name and the September 2026 study date. Cite the Planning Department's existing position that data centers are not a permitted use. Findings must be specific because the Cellular Telephone Co. v. Village of Tarrytown line of cases shows courts will invalidate moratoria based on unproven or speculative fears.

**A defined use definition.** The moratorium must define what it covers. The absence of a definition in the LMC is what created the current administrative vulnerability. A moratorium definition should distinguish large-scale data center facilities from small server rooms and colocation spaces, likely using electrical load thresholds or square footage minimums. This limits Penn Central exposure and prevents the moratorium from being applied to uses it is not designed to address.

**A time limit with an automatic termination clause.** Target no more than twelve months, with automatic termination upon adoption of permanent LMC regulations. Six months with a defined renewal mechanism requiring a new public hearing and council vote is the most legally conservative posture. Do not allow open-ended extension. Courts look unfavorably on rolling moratoria that extend without genuine progress.

**A concrete staff work plan.** The Madison model -- a moratorium tied to a defined regulatory deliverable with milestones -- is what distinguishes a defensible moratorium from a delay tactic. The work plan should reference the September 2026 state impact study as a deliverable that will inform the permanent regulations.

**Exemptions for vested rights.** Explicitly exempt any applications already in the pipeline with a completed submission before the moratorium's effective date. Structure the moratorium to suspend processing rather than deny, which preserves the city's ability to resume review without creating liability.

**Procedurally correct enactment.** The moratorium must follow the LMC amendment procedure for the City of Frederick. City Attorney Rachel Gray's review is essential before introduction.

## VIII. Political Landscape and Strategic Considerations

**The mayor.** The Charlotte precedent is the controlling risk. If Mayor O'Connor opposes the moratorium and has veto authority, the council needs either a supermajority override path or an alternative instrument that does not require his signature. This question must be resolved before the moratorium is introduced.

**Council composition.** A 7-0 vote on the City Values Act demonstrates the council can achieve unanimity on politically contested legislation when the case is well-made and the framing is right. The data center moratorium is a different calculation -- there is economic development pressure and property rights framing that will be used against it. The vote count among council members should be assessed before introduction to avoid a public defeat.

**The text amendment timing.** The moratorium's purpose is to halt the text amendment. The two measures are in direct competition. If the text amendment advances to a vote before the moratorium is introduced, the moratorium's value collapses. Controlling the agenda sequence is the immediate tactical priority.

**Mobilize Frederick as a resource.** Kathy Kinsey and the Mobilize Frederick Government Operations Committee have already done substantial research and presented it to the council. They are an organized, credible community voice with technical depth. Coordinating with them on the moratorium framing, the public hearing record, and the broader community benefit framework is a low-cost, high-return engagement.

**The state study as political cover.** The bipartisan veto override of HB 270/SB 116 is the strongest political anchor available. The General Assembly determined that communities across Maryland are "currently rushing to approve data center projects through a poorly planned, chaotic process that lacks a foolproof and regulatory framework to protect our communities from harm." That is the legislature speaking about exactly what Frederick is being asked to do. Using that language -- and the September 2026 study date -- as the explicit justification for the moratorium's duration is both legally sound and politically insulated.

---

## IX. Recommended Next Steps

**Immediate.** Ask City Attorney Gray two questions: (1) does a moratorium ordinance require mayoral signature, and what is the veto override threshold; (2) is the correct vehicle an ordinance amending the LMC or a standalone interim ordinance, and what procedural steps apply. Do not introduce the moratorium until these answers are in hand.

**Short-term.** Control the agenda sequence. The text amendment and the moratorium are incompatible on a concurrent timeline. If the text amendment has a scheduled hearing date, that date is the deadline.

**Before introduction.** Assess the council vote count privately. Brief Mobilize Frederick and other community stakeholders. Draft the WHEREAS findings using the specific figures from the ICPRB water analysis, the December 2025 council discussion on power constraints, and the HB 270/SB 116 veto override.

**During the moratorium period.** Use the time to develop: a data center use definition for the LMC; specific performance standards for noise (decibels), power draw (megawatts), and water use (gallons per day with cooling technology disclosure requirements); a community benefit agreement framework with housing fund contribution requirements; and a coordination protocol with Frederick County on zoning transparency, given the split annexation/zoning authority structure identified in the December 2025 council discussion.
