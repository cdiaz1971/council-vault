# Housing Policy Brief -- City of Frederick
**Prepared for:** Council President César Díaz
**Date:** April 4, 2026
**Sources:** 22 vault notes across `10 - Council/` root, `100 - Housing/`, `100 - Housing/LMC/`, `195 - Political People/`, plus daily notes March 16--31, 2026
**Scope:** Frederick's housing affordability landscape, with emphasis on AMI-based need, the current regulatory baseline, active legislative levers, and the political dynamics shaping what moves and what stalls.

---

## Executive Summary

Frederick has a documented shortage of 8,133 housing units serving cost-burdened households below $75,000 annually, within a total projected need of 13,774 additional units by 2035. The city's MPDU framework, established in 2023, reaches 60% AMI for one-third of rental affordable units -- but only in projects of 25 or more units, and only when developers don't pay the $2.00/sq ft payment-in-lieu fee instead of building. The Housing Production Target Resolution, which would establish a formal 1,400-unit annual goal with a 15% affordability floor and a 33% sub-target at 60% AMI, has been deferred twice and faces continued pressure to delay via the housing study timeline. The April 9 H&HS budget session is the next concrete lever: $12.6M in spending, a $250k affordable housing RFP, and an unconfirmed City Housing Fund balance that Council has not yet demanded to see. Simultaneously, three state bills -- one of which has passed the House 123-8 -- will reshape Frederick's local land use authority by October 1, 2026 if enacted. The gap between what Frederick's code currently requires and what the documented need demands is wide, and the time to act inside the FY2027 budget window is closing.

---

## I. The Affordability Need -- What the Numbers Actually Say

The starting point for any AMI-based conversation in Frederick is the Housing Needs Assessment, which projects demand for approximately 13,774 additional housing units by 2035 -- roughly 1,377 per year -- and identifies approximately 8,133 cost-burdened households earning below $75,000 annually. The $75,000 figure is roughly the 80% AMI threshold for the Frederick metro area, which means the 8,133 figure represents the households that are the intended beneficiaries of workforce and affordable housing programs: not deep poverty, but the range spanning 50% to 80% AMI that includes teachers, nurses, public employees, and service workers.

The [[Housing Production Target Memo]] structures the production targets around this documented need and maps the AMI tiers to established housing finance structures. The 15% affordability baseline in the resolution corresponds to the regional norm established by Montgomery County (12.5--15%) and Fairfax County (12.5%). Within that 15%, the resolution requires one-third of affordable units -- effectively 5% of total units -- to be accessible at or below 60% AMI. The 60% figure is not arbitrary: it is the ceiling for LIHTC-financed housing, meaning the sub-target is explicitly aligned with the primary federal subsidy mechanism for deep affordable production.

The MACo housing preview in [[Housing Digest 02-24-26]] provides the state-level context: average Maryland home prices rose 39% from 2019 to 2025 ($320,600 to $446,400), and fewer than half of Marylanders can afford the average-priced home. Single-family build costs rose 80% from 2017 to 2024. Those numbers explain why the 8,133 figure is not a backlog that will self-correct -- it is a structural gap that market production at current prices and densities will not close.

The vault does not contain Frederick's specific DHCD-published per-jurisdiction production target from the December 2025 release. That figure is a confirmed gap. The DHCD targets are the accountability framework that legislators and state agencies will use when evaluating local compliance with preemption bills. Council should confirm Frederick's specific target and calibrate the 1,400-unit resolution figure against it.

## II. What the Current Code Actually Does (and Doesn't Do)

[[Frederick Housing Policy Baseline]] is the authoritative reference for what Frederick's LMC and City Code Chapter 19 currently require. The synthesis below draws from it directly.

**MPDU threshold and affordability depths:** Chapter 19, re-enacted November 2023, applies to all residential projects of 25 or more units. The baseline requirement is 12.5% MPDUs -- below the 15% target in the Housing Production Target Resolution. The affordability depth structure for rental units already reaches 60% FCAMI for one-third of rental MPDUs, which is consistent with the resolution's sub-target. For-sale units are tiered at 70%, 80%, and 90% FCAMI. The 40-year control period is solid but falls short of the permanent affordability that a community land trust model would provide.

**The payment-in-lieu escape valve:** Developers may substitute a payment of $2.00 per square foot of each dwelling unit instead of building MPDUs. This fee is structurally low and not calibrated to actual affordability gaps. Mayor O'Connor acknowledged in 2025 that most developers choose to pay the fee rather than build, and the fee pool has reportedly grown to approximately $6M with limited units to show for it. The fee is eligible for recalculation after three years of data -- a trigger that lands in late 2026. This is the clearest near-term legislative lever in the housing ordinance sequence: recalibrating the payment-in-lieu fee to reflect actual affordability gaps would either force more MPDU production or generate substantially more City Housing Fund revenue for other uses.

**Missing middle by-right:** R4 (8,000 sq ft minimum) and R6 (7,000 sq ft minimum) zones permit only single-family detached dwellings. No duplexes, townhouses, or triplexes are permitted by right in these zones. Triplexes, fourplexes, and sixplexes are not defined as permissible dwelling types anywhere in the LMC's dimensional tables. The LMC is more permissive than a pure exclusionary code -- duplexes and townhouses are permitted in R8 through R20 and downtown zones -- but the R4 and R6 gap is the most significant zoning constraint on missing middle production in Frederick's residential fabric.

**Parking minimums:** Per-unit parking minimums remain in all residential districts outside the downtown overlay. The multi-family minimum is 1.5 spaces per unit (maximum 2.5). The [[Parking Minimums as a Housing Cost Driver]] note documents the cost arithmetic: a single structured garage space runs $40,000 or more, passed through to tenants and buyers. Washington State's 2025 reform model -- exempting specific use categories (affordable housing, ADUs, small multifamily, childcare) rather than tying reform to transit proximity -- is the actionable template for Frederick's LMC review.

**ADUs:** Permitted by right in most residential zones including R4, per the 2021 revision. The owner-occupancy requirement and the 10% cap in planned developments are the identified reform targets. No ordinance is pending to address either.

**City Housing Fund:** Exists in law (§19-18) with broad authorized uses. Capitalized only through payment-in-lieu fees and MPDU foreclosure proceeds -- no general fund appropriation, transfer fee, or bond component. Actual current balance is unconfirmed in the vault and should be extracted at the April 9 H&HS session.

**Confirmed gaps in current code:** No local rental registry. No just cause eviction ordinance. No relocation assistance requirement. No rent stabilization. No CLT partnership or public land disposition policy. No funded IZ mechanism comparable to Baltimore's 2024 property tax credit model.

## III. AMI Tiers in Practice -- What the Targets Mean for Real Households

The resolution's AMI structure maps to the following household income ranges for the Frederick metropolitan area (using approximate 2025 HUD figures):

At 60% AMI, a family of four in the Frederick area earns approximately $75,000--$80,000. This is the household a LIHTC project targets: working families, not the extremely poor. A two-bedroom unit affordable at 60% AMI rents for approximately $1,500--$1,600 per month at the 30% of income standard. These units are the core of the subsidy pipeline because LIHTC financing is the primary federal tool available, and LIHTC is structured around the 60% ceiling.

At 80% AMI -- the resolution's outer boundary -- a family of four earns approximately $100,000--$105,000. These are teachers, firefighters, mid-level city employees. Units affordable at 80% AMI rent for approximately $2,000--$2,100 per month. The MPDU program reaches this tier; market-rate inclusionary requirements are calibrated here.

The households most acutely cost-burdened in Frederick -- the 8,133 figure -- are concentrated between 50% and 80% AMI. The households below 30% AMI (extremely low income) are not meaningfully served by MPDU production or market-rate inclusionary requirements; they require deep subsidy through project-based Section 8, LIHTC with rental assistance, or supportive housing. The vault has a note on HUD's defunding of the Family Self-Sufficiency Program ([[Family Self-Sufficiency Program -- HUD Defunding Context]]) that documents the broader federal retreat from this space, which will increase pressure on local tools.

The Himes Ave Conifer LIHTC development ([[Himes Ave -- Conifer LIHTC Development Meeting Notes (2026-03-16)]]) is Frederick's most concrete live project in the 60% AMI range: 145 apartments, LIHTC-financed, 2028 timeline contingent on LIHTC award. This project is the clearest illustration of what production at the relevant AMI tier looks like in practice -- and it highlights that 145 units over a two-to-three-year timeline, with a parking variance request and community engagement obligations, is a significant local effort for modest absolute numbers against an 8,133-unit gap.

The [[Social Housing Requires Zoning Reform to Scale]] note makes the structural argument that connects AMI targets to zoning: public financing alone cannot close the gap if the regulatory environment blocks most new construction. The New York City case study -- where the Creedmoor development was shrunk by 800 units due to land use vetoes even with state financing -- is the cautionary benchmark. The LMC review is Frederick's equivalent of New York's zoning reform debate, and the stakes are the same: a housing production target resolution that is not backed by a regulatory environment capable of producing housing is a resolution, not a policy.

## IV. State Legislative Environment -- What Changes by October 1, 2026

Three bills shape Frederick's operating environment. [[Housing Policy Trends Digest -- 2026-03-22]] is the primary synthesis document.

**Housing Certainty Act (HB 548/SB 325) -- most likely to pass:** HB 548 cleared the House 123-8 on March 20 and is in Senate Economic Matters. If enacted, it vests project approvals under laws in effect at time of application for a minimum of five years. Frederick's practice of renegotiating development conditions post-approval would be eliminated. Development agreements need to be reviewed before October 1 -- a task for Legal and Public Works. MML has sought amendments to protect impact fee timing; the bill also streamlines fee collection.

**Starter and Silver Homes Act (HB 239/SB 36) -- uncertain:** Neither bill has had a committee vote. If enacted in current form, Frederick's R4 (8,000 sq ft) and R6 (7,000 sq ft) single-family minimums would be preempted. A proactive LMC amendment extending townhouse permissions by right in R4 and R6 -- ahead of state action -- would allow Frederick to control the implementation framing. Without proactive action, the change happens to Frederick rather than by Frederick.

**Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act (HB 894/SB 389) -- uncertain:** HB 894 on special order as of March 19; SB 389 in committee. The bill eliminates parking minimums for qualifying TOD projects near rail stations meeting specific frequency thresholds. The critical unresolved question for Frederick: does MARC Brunswick Line service to Frederick meet the qualifying hourly frequency standard? Legal has not confirmed this. If it does, Frederick's parking minimums near MARC stations would be preempted. If it does not, the bill has no direct application to Frederick -- but parking reform is the right move regardless.

**Frederick-county tension:** [[Frederick City vs Frederick County on State Housing Preemption|Frederick City vs. Frederick County on State Housing Preemption]] identifies the jurisdictional dynamic. Frederick City and Frederick County may have opposing interests on preemption bills. Frederick County's land use interests -- managing exurban development, protecting infrastructure capacity -- are structurally different from Frederick City's interests. MACo's BAMBY Act is organized partly around county resistance to state preemption. Watch for Frederick County's formal positions on these bills, and do not assume county government shares the city's housing production goals.

## V. Budget -- The April 9 Session as Leverage Point

[[FY2027 Budget -- Housing and Human Services April 9 Session]] identifies the specific leverage mechanics. The session covers Housing and Human Services at $12.6M -- the primary opportunity to press on what that budget produces against the 8,133-unit shortage. Key questions that should not leave the session unanswered:

What does the City Housing Fund actually contain? The fund exists in law and is capitalized by payment-in-lieu fees, but the current balance is unconfirmed in any vault note or public document. This is a line-item question: how much is in the fund, how is it categorized, and what has been allocated from it in the past three budget cycles?

What does Nash's $250k affordable housing RFP line actually fund, and is it oriented toward production, preservation, or program design? The relationship between the RFP and the production target resolution is unresolved. If the RFP is being used to commission a housing study that defers the resolution, that is a direct conflict of interest between the executive and legislative housing agendas.

Is the $250k legal analysis consultant line available for preemption analysis on tenant protection work? The session is the moment to press this.

[[Housing Preservation -- Durability as Affordability Policy]] adds the preservation frame: Frederick's housing challenge is not only production. Older housing stock along the Golden Mile and adjacent corridors is the existing affordable inventory -- and it deteriorates faster than subsidized units can be built. If the H&HS budget has no preservation component, that is a gap to name explicitly in session.

May 14 is the hard deadline for Council leverage on the FY2027 budget. The April 9 session is the last major opportunity before that window closes.

## VI. Political Context -- Who Controls the Housing Agenda

The housing production target resolution has been deferred twice as of March 20, 2026. The mechanism of deferral -- using the housing study timeline as a prerequisite for the resolution -- is a function of executive-legislative friction, not a policy requirement.

From the [[Michael O'Connor]] profile: the mayor acknowledged publicly that most developers choose the payment-in-lieu option rather than building MPDUs, and that the fee pool has grown to approximately $6M with limited production. His 2025 primary opponent called the MPDU system "working backwards against the people." These are statements that can be used in budget session advocacy. O'Connor has also vetoed council-passed budgets to protect mayoral housing spending priorities (specifically the $2.5M community center and $2M housing subsidies in the FY26 veto). This pattern -- executive assertion over housing budget line items -- is likely to continue.

The mayor-council relationship is described in the March 20 daily note as something that "could be better," confirmed through a conversation with Gayon Sampson. That is a fragile working relationship going into a budget session where Council needs to press on executive housing decisions.

From the [[Katie Nash]] profile: Nash has an ally on the Housing Task Force (confirmed in the March 20 daily note). That intelligence is operationally useful -- it means Council has an internal voice on the task force that can surface information before the housing study is released. Nash's institutional pattern of asserting legislative independence from the executive -- documented in the hotel funding dispute -- aligns with what the housing production resolution needs: a council that is not waiting for the mayor's housing study to define the legislative agenda.

[[Derek Shackelford]] has been a reliable vote on housing and equity measures across three terms. His stated position on housing affordability is that it is "complex" -- a framing that does not signal strong alignment with the production target resolution's specific AMI commitments. He ran unopposed in 2025, which means there is no disclosed donor network or political obligation to development interests on record, but the [[Gayon Sampson]] profile confirms Shackelford's working relationship with the O'Connor administration. He is not an adversary on housing, but he is not a known champion of the resolution's specific numerical commitments either.

## VII. Information Gaps and Research Priorities

### Critical Gaps

**Frederick's DHCD-published production target (December 2025):** DHCD published per-jurisdiction targets on December 31, 2025. Frederick City's specific number is not in the vault. This is the external accountability benchmark that the 1,400-unit resolution figure should be calibrated against. A mismatch in either direction changes the political framing. Confirm via DHCD's 2030 Housing Production Targets report directly or through the city's housing office.

**City Housing Fund current balance:** The fund exists in §19-18 but no vault note documents the current balance, recent allocations, or how the fund is structured within the FY2027 budget. The April 9 session is the moment to extract this.

**Payment-in-lieu recalculation timeline:** The $2.00/sq ft rate is eligible for recalculation after three years of square footage and affordability gap data. The three-year trigger falls in late 2026 under the November 2023 re-enactment. Has the administration initiated that analysis? What process governs the recalculation?

**MARC frequency threshold:** Does MARC Brunswick Line service to Frederick meet the Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act's qualifying frequency standard (hourly service Monday through Friday, 8 AM to 6 PM)? This determines whether Frederick's parking minimums near MARC stations are subject to preemption. Legal needs to answer this before October 1.

### Significant Gaps

**Council vote alignment on the Housing Production Target Resolution:** Which council members are holdouts, and why? The resolution has been deferred twice, which implies it does not currently have the votes. Understanding the specific objections -- whether they are to the unit target, the AMI percentages, or the timing relative to the housing study -- is essential before the next deferral vote.

**Nash's $250k RFP specifics:** What program design does the $250k affordable housing RFP fund? Is it oriented toward production only, or does it have a preservation component? Who controls the RFP process, and what is the expected output?

**Designated housing agencies under Chapter 19:** Section 19-11 allows up to 20% of MPDUs in any development to be purchased or leased by designated nonprofit or government housing agencies. Which organizations, if any, have been designated? This determines whether there is an existing pipeline for affordable unit acquisition beyond the MPDU resale program.

**Himes Ave LIHTC application status:** The Conifer project is contingent on a LIHTC award. What round is the application in, and what is the competitive position? A failed LIHTC application sets the project back by at least one cycle (12--18 months).

### Contextual Gaps

**College Park CLT replication assessment:** The College Park Community Preservation Trust used $14.9M in combined funding (city, state, federal, COG) to acquire and sell nine affordable homes. What is the equivalent minimum capitalization for a Frederick CLT or CLT partnership, and what state and federal funding streams are currently available? The DHCD Reinvest Baltimore initiative funded the South Baltimore CLT -- is there an equivalent non-Baltimore program?

**Baltimore funded IZ tax credit mechanism in detail:** Baltimore's property tax credit for inclusionary housing (Ordinance 24-308) eliminated the financial feasibility barrier by reimbursing forgone rent annually. The preconditions for this model -- state enabling authority, city council ordinance, DHCD approval -- need to be mapped for Maryland municipalities outside Baltimore City.

## VIII. Recommended Next Steps

**Immediate (before April 9):**

- Confirm Frederick's specific DHCD-published production target from the December 2025 report; if the 1,400-unit resolution figure is below the state target, that is a powerful argument for acceleration, not deferral.
- Prepare the City Housing Fund balance question as a specific line-item demand for the April 9 H&HS session; frame it as a basic accountability question rather than a housing policy debate.
- Confirm with Nash what intelligence her Housing Task Force ally can provide about the housing study timeline and scope before April 9.

**Short-term (before May 14 budget deadline):**

- Press at the April 9 session on the payment-in-lieu recalculation trigger and whether the administration has initiated the analysis. If not, request a formal timeline.
- Direct Legal to assess: (1) development agreement compatibility with the Housing Certainty Act before October 1; (2) MARC frequency threshold for Transit and Housing Opportunity Act coverage; (3) preemption risk for any tenant protection ordinance relative to the BAMBY Act's statewide responsible owner registry.
- Identify whether the $250k legal analysis consultant line in the FY2027 budget can be directed toward preemption analysis for tenant protection work, rather than being used as an undifferentiated executive discretionary fund.

**Policy horizon (LMC review and ordinance sequence):**

- Initiate a proactive LMC amendment extending townhouse permissions by right in R4 and R6 before state preemption forces the change on the state's terms. Frame it as council-initiated housing policy, not compliance.
- Begin drafting a Chapter 19 amendment raising the MPDU baseline from 12.5% to 15%, aligned with the Housing Production Target Resolution.
- Commission a scoping analysis for a Frederick CLT or CLT partnership, using the College Park Community Preservation Trust as the funding model reference.
- Evaluate parking reform legislation using the Washington State use-case exemption model (affordable housing, ADUs, small multifamily, childcare) rather than transit-proximity framing, which is politically complicated in Frederick's context.
