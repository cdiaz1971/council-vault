## Housing Affordability

The most substantive housing signal this week comes from Princeton's Eviction Lab, whose 2025 data shows national eviction filings dropping slightly to 1.23 million, roughly 3% below the post-pandemic average. The headline finding is about what drives filings more than rent levels: renter protections. New York City, with strong tenant protections, maintained one of the lowest filing rates in the country despite high rents and a tight vacancy market. Cities where filings spiked — the greater Austin area saw a 30% jump — share a profile of weaker tenant protection frameworks. This is direct ammunition for the housing production target work: production matters, but without protection policies running alongside, you're building into a market that still punishes renters. (Next City, https://nextcity.org/urbanist-news/the-weekly-wrap-a-new-national-strike-fund-plans-to-get-money-into-workers)

Also worth flagging: EQR settled the RealPage lawsuit for $56 million. The algorithmic rent-setting litigation is moving, and peer cities are watching to see what enforcement posture local governments can take alongside federal action.

## Immigrant Support

This is the most actionable item of the week. Route Fifty reports that states are building public data reporting tools that allow community members to document potentially unlawful federal immigration enforcement actions. The theory is both legal and political: documented community reports can inform litigation, build the evidentiary record for legal challenges, and create public accountability for what federal agents are doing at the local level. (Route Fifty, https://www.route-fifty.com/management/2026/04/states-leverage-data-push-back-federal-immigration-enforcement/413116/)

For Frederick, this is worth examining directly. District 2 has the city's highest immigrant population and is already the political center of immigration advocacy in Frederick. A municipal or community-level reporting tool, even a lightweight one, could serve two purposes: giving residents a structured way to report enforcement incidents, and building a locally-held record independent of state systems. This connects cleanly to the City Values Act framework and to RISE Coalition's existing documentation and advocacy infrastructure. It's worth a conversation with RISE about whether they're already doing informal documentation and whether a more formal tool would serve them.

## Citizen Participation

Nothing direct on civic engagement this week, but two adjacent signals from the Next City weekly wrap are worth noting. The National League of Cities published a piece on what it would look like for cities to onboard residents the way employers onboard new employees. The framing is worth reading for anyone working on participation equity: most cities assume residents know how to engage, and most don't. The piece reportedly applies employer onboarding principles (orientation, explicit pathways, skill-building, community connection) to civic participation. That framing resonates with where Frederick needs to go on public comment reform and community engagement more broadly.

The Knight Foundation's $5 million Knight Cities Challenge is also accepting applications through April 30 across its 26 communities. Frederick is not a Knight community, but local nonprofits working on civic participation and community development may have connections to Knight cities worth exploring.

## Broader Municipal Watch

Data center backlash is now visibly shifting local elections, per NPR. In Edgecombe County, North Carolina, an incumbent board of commissioners member lost a primary to a challenger running against a data center proposal. In a town near St. Louis, four council members lost seats after supporting a $6 billion data center. In Independence, Missouri, two council members were voted out for backing data center tax breaks. This is nationally significant for Frederick, where data center pressure in the county is already a political flashpoint. The electoral consequences are now documented and recent. This is worth tracking as a political context item for any council or county-level engagement on data center policy. (Next City, same URL above)

The Montana mobile home park story (AP) is also worth a bookmark. Mobile home park residents organizing lease negotiations is an emerging tenant protection front nationally, and while Frederick's mobile home park footprint is small, the legal and organizing models being developed there could matter.
