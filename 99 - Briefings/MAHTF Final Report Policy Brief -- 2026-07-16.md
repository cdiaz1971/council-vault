# MAHTF Final Report Policy Brief

## Summary

Detailed analysis of the Mayor's Affordable Housing Task Force Final Report against César's active housing legislative threads, identifying alignment, new openings, risks, and political dynamics. The report is primarily a mayoral instrument, but several of its highest-priority recommendations are things the council is already driving, and the report creates political cover to move faster on LMC amendments and MPDU reform than a typical mayoral implementation timeline allows.

## Bottom Line

This is a useful document, but it is primarily a mayoral instrument, not a council instrument. It names the council as an actor needed to adopt code changes, fee revisions, and ordinance approvals while routing decision-making authority and narrative framing toward the Mayor's Office. Several of its highest-priority recommendations are things you are already driving. Several others create openings you should move on before the Mayor does. Three require careful reading: the MPDU section endorses changes that go in both your direction and directions that could weaken affordability depth if handled badly. The staffing and commission recommendations have real governance implications for council oversight authority.

## What the Report Is and Is Not

The task force convened March 19 to May 15, 2026 under mayoral appointment. The report was produced for Mayor O'Connor. It is grounded in the countywide Housing Needs Assessment finding of 13,774 units needed by 2035. Its four workgroups covered Land, Policy and Process, Fees and Funding, and Communications. Its output is a set of strategies with urgency ratings and implementation timelines, not legislation.

The political significance is that this now exists as a shared reference document. Every future housing debate in Frederick will be tested against it. You need to have a clear public position on it before the Mayor controls that frame.

## Where the Report Validates Your Existing Work

**MPDU Reform.** The report calls for increasing in-lieu fees to incentivize MPDU production, offering deeper bonuses for units at or below 60% AMI and 30% AMI, and reducing the development threshold from 25 units to 10 units to capture more projects. The current 12.5% baseline is named as insufficient. All of this is consistent with your Housing Production Target Resolution, which sets a 15% affordability target and a 33% sub-target at 60% AMI. The report explicitly frames the $2/sq ft payment-in-lieu rate as structurally inadequate and calls for revising it to incentivize actual unit construction rather than cash-out.

**City Housing Fund Capitalization.** The report projects that MPDU in-lieu revenue will reach only $4.58 million by 2035, with $1.83 million by 2030, and explicitly states this is insufficient. It recommends general fund allocations, rental licensing fees as special funds, and federal earmarks to build real capitalization. The current City Housing Fund balance is named at $4.3 million, estimated to produce about 450 units. The call for a dedicated navigator position and incentive-package tool is worth tracking as a potential budget ask, though it also carries governance implications.

**LMC Modernization.** The report endorses missing middle housing expansion, parking minimum reduction for affordable housing, ADU expansion and streamlining, and by-right approvals in targeted zones. All of these track directly to the targeted LMC amendment priorities: missing middle in R4/R6, parking reform, and ADU reform. The report gives strong political cover to accelerate those tracks rather than wait for Taylor's comprehensive LMC review.

**APFO Reform.** Among the report's highest-urgency recommendations. The current APFO structure allows development at 100% school capacity or at up to 120% with PAYGO fees. Three schools in the city are above 120% and six are above 100%. The report recommends a tiered weight system where units at or below 40% AMI don't count toward APFO tests at all, units at 40-60% AMI count as 0.5 units, and units at 60-80% AMI count as 0.75 units, limited to developments where 100% of units are affordable at or below 80% AMI. Council would need to adopt this as an ordinance amendment.

**Permitting Streamlining.** The report calls for concurrent review across departments, targeting an 8-10 month review timeline down from 18-24 months, and expedited fast-track designation for affordable housing projects. These are process changes implemented by the administration, but the council can create accountability mechanisms, request reporting, and use a Housing Trust Fund framework to condition funding on process compliance.

**Faith-Based Land.** The report calls for by-right zoning approval for housing on faith-based land, with a dedicated city contact and process map for congregations. Virginia's YIGBY law is the relevant peer model. This is a LMC text amendment and can be introduced as a targeted amendment rather than waiting for comprehensive LMC review.

**Single-Room Occupancy and Co-Living.** The report explicitly names SRO and co-living as tools for expanding affordable options. The report's explicit endorsement from a mayoral task force gives this a credible political opening that didn't exist before.

## Where the Report Creates New Openings

**Affordable Housing Commission.** The report recommends creating a new Affordable Housing Commission with authority to review housing funding priorities annually, draft reports to the Mayor, and make staffing and funding recommendations. This is a governance question: who creates this commission, who appoints members, and what is its relationship to the council. If the Mayor constitutes this by executive action, it is a mayoral advisory body. If it is created by ordinance, the council has a role in structure and appointments. A commission with real authority over City Housing Fund spending priorities needs council oversight, not just mayoral oversight.

**Affordable Housing Navigator.** The report recommends a city staff position dedicated to identifying developers, packaging incentive deals, and helping stalled market-rate projects convert to affordable. This is an HHS staffing ask that will show up in a future budget. The council should condition it on reporting to both branches.

**TIF and Economic Development Finance.** The report recommends Tax Increment Financing, CDFI partnerships, bond financing, and braiding of economic development funds for affordable housing. The council has more direct authority here than on executive-branch process changes. A resolution directing the CFO to evaluate TIF feasibility for specific housing corridors would be concrete and relatively uncontested.

**Recordation Fee Reduction.** The report recommends exploring with Frederick County how to reduce or refund recordation taxes for affordable housing projects and redirect that revenue to housing. This is a county-city negotiation question worth flagging for the intergovernmental relationship.

**Public Education Campaign.** The report calls for an ongoing public education effort framed around workforce stability, economic sustainability, and generational opportunity. This language framework is one you should adopt and model publicly before the Mayor does.

## Where the Report Requires Careful Reading

**Displacement and Tenant Protection Language.** Strategy 3.1 mentions rent stabilization and anti-displacement tools in one sentence but does not develop them. The report is supply-side in its dominant framing. It does not name the Real Rent Ordinance or tenant protections as parallel priorities. You need to be on record that production and tenant protection are not substitutes and that the council is pursuing both tracks simultaneously.

**MPDU Threshold Reduction.** The report recommends reducing the MPDU trigger threshold from 25 units to 10 units. Sound policy, but the report does not propose increasing the MPDU percentage requirement alongside the lower threshold. If the threshold drops to 10 without accompanying increases in affordability depth or in-lieu fee rates, you could end up with more projects subject to MPDU requirements but producing weaker affordability outcomes per project. Move threshold reduction and fee increase together.

**"Modernize the MPDU Ordinance" Framing.** The language about creating an "incentive-based MPDU framework" and developing "a realistic target of MPDU units as a percentage of development" could be a pathway to weakening the 12.5% baseline. Specify in any council discussion that the 12.5% floor is a floor, not a negotiable starting point, and that the 15% target in the Housing Production Target Resolution is the political baseline.

**4,000-Unit Estimate.** The report estimates 4,000 additional units over ten years if all recommendations are implemented. The Housing Needs Assessment identifies 13,774 units needed by 2035. The gap between 4,000 units under favorable conditions and 13,774 units of documented need is not addressed in the report. The council should name this gap publicly and make clear that the task force recommendations, while necessary, are not sufficient.

## Connection to Active Council Threads

**Real Rent Ordinance.** The report does not address rental junk fees or all-in pricing disclosure. Your Real Rent Ordinance addresses something the task force did not.

**LMC Modernization and Targeted Amendments.** The report's endorsement of missing middle expansion, ADU reform, parking reduction, and zoning flexibility is the strongest political argument yet for moving the targeted LMC amendments without waiting for the comprehensive review. The task force has rated these high urgency and near-term.

**Housing Production Target Resolution.** The report's numbers are broadly consistent with the resolution's 1,400-unit annual target, though the task force's 4,000-unit ten-year projection implies a slower pace. The resolution's framing is more ambitious. The two documents reinforce each other; say so publicly while noting that the resolution's targets are the council's commitment and the task force's recommendations are the pathway to get there.

**Housing Trust Fund.** The report's funding section is essentially making the case for a Housing Trust Fund without naming it. The combination of dedicated general fund appropriations, recordation fee redirection, rental licensing revenue, and in-lieu fee reform it recommends is exactly the capitalization structure for a Housing Trust Fund. Propose that the council build the fund on the task force's own recommendation.

**Data Center ZTA.** The task force's documentation of how thin the housing funding base is strengthens the argument against the data center ZTA. The City Housing Fund cannot absorb the fiscal uncertainty that comes from displacing reliable tax base with a business personal property tax-exempt use.

## Political Dynamics

The report was written for the Mayor. O'Connor's letter of transmittal explicitly frames this as his administration's work and positions him as the person who will "work with the City Council" to implement recommendations. The council is named as a required actor for policy adoption, fee changes, and ordinance approvals, not as an equal author of the agenda.

The strategic risk is that the Mayor uses this report to take ownership of the housing issue while the council is positioned as a supporting player. The countermove is to respond publicly with a specific council housing agenda that adopts the task force's strongest recommendations, names you as Council President as the legislative driver, and makes clear that the council will act on a faster timeline than mayoral implementation tends to produce.

Nash, Hempel Irani, Shackelford, and Taylor are all likely favorable to the core recommendations. Adkins and Brehm are the question marks. Brehm's rental licensing fee reduction proposal cuts directly against the task force's finding that the City Housing Fund is undercapitalized and needs the rental licensing fee stream.

Adkins' planning background could be constructive on APFO reform and LMC amendments if engaged early. Getting him on record supporting specific task force recommendations before the Mayor claims them would shift the political alignment in a useful direction.

## Work Plan Outline

The eleven areas for planning and council discussion:

1. **MPDU Reform** -- The task force validates raising the in-lieu fee, deepening affordability to 60% and 30% AMI, and dropping the trigger threshold from 25 to 10 units; move these together, not separately.
2. **City Housing Fund Capitalization** -- The task force's own numbers show the fund is structurally undercapitalized; use this to build the case for a Housing Trust Fund with dedicated revenue streams.
3. **LMC Targeted Amendments** -- The task force rates missing middle, ADU reform, parking minimum reduction, and faith-based by-right zoning as high urgency and near-term; use this as political cover to move without waiting for the comprehensive LMC review.
4. **APFO Reform** -- The tiered AMI weight system is the highest-urgency, council-actionable item in the report; it can be introduced as an ordinance amendment with Adkins likely reachable given his planning background.
5. **Affordable Housing Commission** -- If this body gets created by executive action rather than ordinance, the council loses appointment authority and oversight; push for ordinance constitution before the Mayor moves first.
6. **Permitting Streamlining** -- The 8-10 month target and concurrent review process are administrative, but the council can create reporting obligations and tie funding conditions to compliance.
7. **TIF and Alternative Housing Finance** -- The council has direct authority here; a resolution directing the CFO to evaluate TIF feasibility for housing corridors would be concrete and relatively uncontested.
8. **Tenant Protection Gap** -- The report is supply-side and does not address rental junk fees, disclosure requirements, or displacement protection; make clear publicly that the council is running both tracks.
9. **The 4,000-Unit Shortfall** -- The task force projects 4,000 units under favorable conditions; the documented need is 13,774 by 2035; name this gap publicly so the task force report doesn't get treated as sufficient.
10. **Public Messaging Frame** -- The task force recommends a workforce-stability and generational-opportunity narrative; adopt this language publicly and do it before the Mayor controls the frame.
11. **Data Center Fiscal Connection** -- The task force documents how thin the housing funding base is, which directly strengthens the argument that the data center ZTA puts housing finance at risk.

## Recommended Next Steps

**Immediate.** Issue a public statement as Council President welcoming the report, naming the specific recommendations the council will take up, and stating that the council is committed to moving faster on LMC amendments and MPDU reform than a typical mayoral implementation timeline allows.

**Short-term.** Introduce the APFO tiered-weight amendment as a council-sponsored ordinance. The task force rates it high urgency and near-term. It is the kind of technical-but-impactful change that demonstrates council capacity on housing without requiring a culture fight.

**Short-term.** Use the task force's in-lieu fee analysis to justify the MPDU ordinance amendments in development. The task force's own planning department data undercuts the argument that the $2/sq ft rate is adequate.

**Ongoing.** Propose that any Affordable Housing Commission created in response to this report be constituted by ordinance rather than executive action, with council-appointment authority over some seats and council oversight of the commission's annual reporting.
