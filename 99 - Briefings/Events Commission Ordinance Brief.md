# Events Commission Ordinance Brief - 2026-07-23

## Summary

The legislative manager produced a draft Special Events Commission ordinance on July 17, 2026, modeled on frameworks from other jurisdictions. The draft establishes a commission with permitting authority over special events, creates a special event management plan requirement, and adds noise regulations under Chapter 34. The immediate legislative context is the pending Gateway Property approval and the anticipated need for a regulatory body capable of managing large recurring events like cricket matches at that site. Fourteen items require discussion with the legislative manager before the ordinance is ready to advance.

## Background and Context

César requested this ordinance to fill a regulatory gap the Gateway Property approval will create. The goal is a body with strong authority to ensure that large events are held in a manner that protects residents and city resources, with traffic plans, noise controls, and community impact mitigation required before any event proceeds. The draft was built on general-purpose special events permitting models from other jurisdictions and reflects that origin -- it functions better as an administrative coordination mechanism than as a strong regulatory gate.

The ordinance makes four substantive code changes: it amends Sec. 6-19 on alcoholic beverages to transfer approval authority from the Mayor and Board of Aldermen to the new commission, repeals and replaces Chapter 18 Article I, adds Articles I and II to Chapter 18 establishing the Special Events Code and the commission, and adds Chapter 34 Article III on special event noise management.

## Items for Discussion with Legislative Manager

**1. Summary language understates commission authority.** The summary describes the commission's role as administering "City policies and procedures related to the permitting and operation of events." That framing positions the body as an administrative function rather than a regulatory one. It does not capture the power to set conditions, deny permits, or hold sponsors accountable. The summary should be rewritten to reflect regulatory authority.

**2. FOR clause needs regulatory reframe.** The FOR clause describes the purpose as "streamlining, clarifying, and centralizing" permitting and "improving coordination." The word streamlining in particular cuts against the idea of a body with strong gatekeeping power. Recommended replacement: "FOR the purpose of establishing a regulatory body with authority to review, condition, approve, and deny permits for special events; protecting residents and city resources from adverse impacts of large events; ensuring that traffic, noise, public safety, and community impact plans are in place before any special event proceeds; and improving coordination among City departments and other governmental entities involved in providing services for special events."

**3. Sec. 6-19(d)(3) -- alcohol authority transfer needs confirmation.** The draft transfers authority from the Mayor to the commission for alcohol consumption approvals at events taking place entirely within a structure. That would include an enclosed venue at Gateway. This was a mayoral function. Confirm this transfer is intentional and that the commission has the process to handle it.

**4. Sec. 18-3(e) -- 2,000-attendee threshold may not capture Gateway events.** The large private event definition triggers at 2,000 outdoor attendees. A cricket match drawing fewer than 2,000 people at a dedicated facility escapes the framework entirely. The threshold needs to be examined in the context of the specific Gateway use case. Additionally, the Great Frederick Fair is exempted by name in the definitions section rather than in the exemptions section, which is unusual drafting. If the Fair changes hands or character, that carve-out remains in the code regardless.

**5. Sec. 18-9 -- membership structure conflicts with stated design intent.** The commission as drafted is composed entirely of ex officio government staff and institutional designees. The COO chairs the body, serves at the mayor's pleasure, and controls the meeting calendar under Sec. 18-10(b). The Chamber of Commerce holds a permanent voting seat. The council holds one seat out of nine, designated by the council president. There is no mayoral nomination or council confirmation process anywhere in the membership structure. This puts the body under executive control and is inconsistent with César's stated design intent of mayoral nomination and council confirmation. The entire membership section needs to be rewritten.

**6. Sec. 18-11(a) -- "minimum information necessary" language is too restrictive.** The application form requirement states forms shall require "only the minimum information necessary." That phrase is borrowed from privacy-protective regulatory contexts and works against a strong gatekeeping commission. A sponsor could use it to resist providing comprehensive traffic, noise, or security plans. The phrase should be removed or replaced with language requiring all information necessary for informed review.

**7. Sec. 18-12(b) -- 92-day clock has no completion trigger.** The commission must act within 92 days of receiving a "complete application" or the application is deemed denied. Nothing in the ordinance defines what makes an application complete, and nothing requires the special event management plan to be submitted and approved before the clock starts. A sponsor could file a thin application, start the 92-day timer, and force a decision before the commission has what it needs. The definition of "complete application" needs to be spelled out, including a requirement that a satisfactory management plan be part of the complete package.

**8. Sec. 18-12(d) -- criteria are considerations, not mandatory findings.** The eleven review criteria are framed as things the commission "shall review applications in accordance with." There is no requirement that the commission make affirmative findings on each criterion before issuing a permit. That is a weaker standard than the ordinance needs. Recommended replacement for the opening of Sec. 18-12(d): "The Commission shall make affirmative written findings on each of the following criteria before issuing a special event permit. The Commission may not issue a permit unless it finds that each criterion is satisfied." This flips the burden and requires documented findings that can support a denial if challenged.

**9. Sec. 18-12(d)(10) -- financial responsibility requires a binding commitment, not acknowledgment.** The tenth criterion requires the applicant to "understand and accept responsibility" for special expenses including police, public works, and parking costs. Understanding and accepting is not the same as a legally binding commitment to pay. This should require a signed financial commitment or bond, not acknowledgment language.

**10. Sec. 18-14 -- after-action reports are disconnected from future permit decisions.** The ordinance requires an after-action report at the conclusion of each permitted event, covering enforcement actions, challenges, and event impacts. Nothing in the ordinance connects a negative after-action report to future permit decisions. A sponsor with a history of violations or compliance failures faces no additional scrutiny on renewal or future applications. The section needs a provision explicitly making prior compliance history a factor in future permit review.

**11. Sec. 18-17 -- $5,000 maximum fine has no escalation for repeat violations.** The penalty for any violation is a municipal infraction not to exceed $5,000 per violated provision. A commercial event operator running a season of events at a dedicated facility faces the same maximum fine on the tenth violation as on the first. There is no repeat violation multiplier and no provision allowing the commission to deny future applications based on a pattern of violations. Fine escalation and a compliance history provision need to be added.

**12. Sec. 18-18 -- judicial review provision contains a drafting error.** The section routes appeals from decisions made by "the Mayor." The Mayor has no decision-making role anywhere in this ordinance. The reference should be to the Commission.

**13. Sec. 34-18 -- noise thresholds are fixed in the ordinance text.** The section sets maximum sound levels at 100 dBA and 110 dBC at the mixing desk, and 70 dBA at the closest residential property. Because these numbers appear in the ordinance itself, adjusting them requires a full code amendment rather than an administrative regulation. If community experience or acoustic research suggests different thresholds are appropriate for specific venue types, the commission has no flexibility. Consider whether these numbers should live in commission administrative regulations rather than the ordinance.

**14. Enforcement framework is inconsistent between Chapter 18 and Chapter 34.** Sec. 34-20(c) includes a forward-looking remedy not present in Chapter 18: the city may deny future permit requests, including previously issued permits for future events, for a period of six months following a violation. Chapter 18 has no equivalent provision. The two enforcement frameworks operate in the same regulatory space and need to be aligned. The stronger forward-looking denial authority in Chapter 34 should either be extended to Chapter 18 or the discrepancy should be a deliberate and explained choice.

## Political Context

The Gateway Property ZMA and annexation vote was anticipated for July 16. The Events Commission ordinance is positioned as a companion regulatory mechanism to accompany that approval. The timing creates both an opportunity and a risk: moving quickly signals responsiveness to community concerns about Gateway impacts, but a structurally weak ordinance that does not deliver real regulatory authority would undercut the stated goal. The fourteen items above represent the gap between what was drafted and what César asked for.

The membership structure is the most politically consequential issue. An ex officio commission chaired by the COO gives the Mayor effective control over event permitting decisions. That is inconsistent with council oversight and with César's goal of an independent regulatory body. Resolving the membership question before introduction is more important than resolving any of the technical drafting items.

## Recommended Next Steps

Immediate: Schedule a working session with the legislative manager to walk through the fourteen items. The membership structure and the FOR clause reframe should be the opening agenda items since they define what the ordinance is trying to do before getting into section-by-section fixes.

Short-term: Once membership is resolved, return to the criteria findings requirement in Sec. 18-12(d) and the 92-day clock definition in Sec. 18-12(b). Those two provisions together determine whether the commission has real gatekeeping power or is just processing paperwork.

Ongoing: Monitor the Gateway Property approval timeline and calibrate introduction of this ordinance accordingly. Introducing before Gateway is final may be premature. Introducing after without a regulatory framework in place creates a gap.
