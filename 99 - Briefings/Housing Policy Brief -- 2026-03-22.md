# Housing Policy Brief -- City of Frederick
**Prepared for:** Council President César Díaz
**Date:** March 22, 2026
**Sources:** 26 vault notes across `10 - Council/`, plus daily notes March 16-20
**Scope:** Local legislation, state legislative environment, active development pipeline, regulatory reform

---

## Executive Summary

Frederick is in the most consequential housing policy moment of the current council term. Three tracks are running simultaneously: a locally-authored production target resolution that has been deferred twice and remains unfinished, a state legislative session that will impose new constraints and preemptions on local land use authority regardless of what Frederick does, and an active LIHTC development pipeline with one concrete project (Himes Ave) already in negotiation. The LMC review commission is the long-game vehicle for regulatory reform, but it operates on a 2-year horizon while state preemption may arrive this session.

The central tension in all three tracks is the same: Frederick needs to produce more housing faster, at deeper affordability levels, without triggering displacement -- and the regulatory, financial, and political tools to do all three simultaneously are in conflict with each other.

---

## I. The Local Production Mandate

### What Exists

The [[Housing Production Target Resolution]] establishes 1,400 units per year through 2035, with a 15% affordability floor at 80% AMI and a deep affordability tier requiring at least 33% of those affordable units at 60% AMI -- effectively 5% of total production. The companion [[Housing Production Target Memo]] grounds these numbers in Frederick's own Housing Needs Assessment: 13,774 units needed by 2035, approximately 1,377 annually, against a backdrop of 8,133 cost-burdened households earning below $75,000 per year.

The resolution is structurally sound. The affordability tiers align with LIHTC financing structures, match regional inclusionary norms (Montgomery County at 12.5-15%, Fairfax at 12.5%), and the tiered model balances production volume against depth of subsidy. The annual reporting requirement in Section 5 creates a public accountability structure that future councils cannot easily ignore.

### What's Unresolved

As of March 20, the resolution had been deferred twice and was still unfinished. The vault has the draft but not the current negotiating status or specific sticking points.

There is also no note capturing Frederick's current annual housing production baseline. The resolution sets a target of 1,400 units per year, but without a baseline permitting number there is no way to assess whether 1,400 is a 10% stretch or a 100% increase over current output. That baseline is the single most important number missing from the vault.

## II. The State Legislative Environment

### Governor Moore's 2026 Package

Three bills are moving and all three affect Frederick directly.

**[[Maryland Starter and Silver Homes Act (2026)]]** allows smaller single-family homes on lots as small as 5,000 sq ft statewide, projecting homes up to 30% cheaper than current construction costs. If Frederick's current minimum lot standards exceed 5,000 sq ft in any residential zone, this bill overrides them. The vault notes the risk but no note documents Frederick's actual minimums or which zones would be affected.

**[[Maryland Housing Certainty Act (2026)]]** locks in zoning and development rules at time of project approval, eliminating post-approval negotiation. This is a direct hit on how Frederick structures development agreements. The Renn Quarter phasing work session is a preview of exactly this dynamic: post-approval flexibility over commercial delivery obligations would be unavailable in future projects once this bill passes.

**Maryland Transit and Housing Opportunity Act** eliminates minimum parking requirements near transit and gives the state authority over land adjacent to transit hubs, unlocking an estimated 7,000+ units on 300+ acres of state land. Frederick has MARC connections, making this indirectly relevant.

### Production Targets

DHCD published per-jurisdiction housing production targets on December 31, 2025. [[Maryland Housing Production Targets 2030]] flags confirming Frederick's specific number as an open action item -- it has not been confirmed in any vault note. This is a significant gap. The state target is the number Frederick will be held publicly accountable to, and it establishes the political and reputational stakes for everything else in this brief. Jurisdictions meeting targets are eligible for the Maryland Housing Leadership Award; those falling short face scrutiny and stronger preemption arguments from state legislators.

### City-County Tension

[[Frederick City vs Frederick County on State Housing Preemption]] documents a structural risk: MACo and Frederick County may oppose or seek to weaken the same state bills that Frederick City wants to engage strategically. The city and county have historically had jurisdictional friction on development boundaries. Frederick City should not assume Frederick County is an ally and should track county government testimony independently.

## III. The Active Development Pipeline

### Himes Ave LIHTC Project

The most concrete housing project currently in negotiation is the Conifer LIHTC development on Himes Ave: 145 units, LIHTC financing, 2028 timeline contingent on LIHTC award, with community and commercial space. Key watch items from [[Himes Ave -- Conifer LIHTC Development Meeting Notes (2026-03-16)]]:

The parking variance request -- reducing from 1.5 to 1.25 spaces per unit -- will come before Council or Planning and is likely to generate public comment. Traffic on Himes Ave is a legitimate safety concern that Conifer committed to study but has not yet delivered. Community engagement with the NAC and Golden Mile Alliance was committed to but has not yet occurred. The conflict of interest issue -- the project is directly behind César's home -- requires a formal disclosure determination before any vote posture is established.

The LIHTC timeline deserves scrutiny. LIHTC awards are competitive and state-administered; a 2028 delivery date assumes a successful application in an upcoming funding cycle. No vault note tracks where the application stands or when the relevant cycle closes.

### Sage Way Farm Annexation

[[Sage Way Farm annexation petition]] documents a 25-acre agricultural parcel at 5509 Old National Pike petitioned for annexation for high-density residential at Frederick's western border. The note is from April 2025 with no follow-up tracking what happened to the petition.

### Single Room Occupancy

[[SRO thread]] documents a Bluesky thread framing SROs as a low-cost housing option, identifying unresolved policy questions: affordability requirements, property management standards, shared facility ratios, tenant protections, conversion rules, and design standards. There is no ordinance draft, no staff memo, and no timeline note in the vault.

## IV. Regulatory Reform: LMC and Zoning

### LMC Review Commission

The [[LMC Review]] establishes the commission structure: advisory with formal recommendation authority, 2-year timeline, full code scope, with housing supply, affordability, equity, and walkability as explicit evaluation lenses. The [[Land Management Code Review Commission Council Decision Points & Open Question]] note documents unresolved questions on scope, membership, conflict of interest standards, and the development moratorium question.

The commission is the right vehicle for the following reforms the vault has well-documented evidence for:

**Parking minimums.** [[Parking Minimums as a Housing Cost Driver]] presents the Washington State use-case reform model as the politically viable path for Frederick -- exempting specific sympathetic uses (affordable housing, childcare, small commercial, ADUs) rather than proximity-to-transit arguments that do not work in a city without robust transit. A surface parking space costs $10,000 to build; structured parking runs $40,000+.

**Building codes for small multifamily.** [[Applying Residential Building Codes to Small Multifamily (Dallas Model)]] identifies a probable code mismatch: 2-8 unit buildings in Frederick may currently be subject to commercial code standards rather than residential standards. Dallas fixed this for buildings up to 8 units and 3 stories, significantly reducing missing middle construction costs. No vault note confirms whether this mismatch exists in Frederick's current code.

**Form-based approach.** [[Form Based Code]] provides the policy framework. Most applicable to downtown, key corridors, and activity centers. The LMC review is the right moment to evaluate which corridors would benefit.

**Mobile Dwelling Units.** [[Mobile Dwelling Units (MDUs) as a Low-Cost Housing Tool]] identifies MDUs as a potential low-cost production tool that may not have a defined place in Frederick's current LMC. The state preemption question on MDU siting is unresolved.

### Mixed-Use Phasing (Renn Quarter)

The [[City Council Work Session - 022526]] on MU-1/MU-2 phasing is directly relevant even though framed as a commercial issue. The Planning Commission voted 4-0 to deny the developer's request to replace uniform phasing requirements with project-specific flexibility. The core risk -- residential build-out without delivery of promised commercial -- is the exact anti-displacement question that [[Two Rules for Neighborhood Change That Serves Everyone]] identifies as the key test for any development decision.

## V. The Equity and Anti-Displacement Framework

Multiple notes converge on a tension the production target resolution acknowledges but does not fully resolve: increasing housing supply and deepening affordability are both necessary but can work against each other in the short term if upzoning drives localized rent increases before new supply comes online.

[[Two Rules for Neighborhood Change That Serves Everyone]] provides the analytical frame: every housing proposal should be evaluated for whether it serves the people already in the neighborhood or replaces them. The production target resolution addresses this in Section 3 (Preservation and Anti-Displacement) with aspirational commitments -- NOAH preservation, acquisition partnerships, rehab support -- but without funding mechanisms, specific programs, or timelines.

The Golden Mile is the most concrete geographic context for this tension. The Himes Ave LIHTC project serves the Golden Mile corridor directly. [[Legislative Priorities]] includes rental assistance and eviction prevention as active priorities, but there is no dedicated vault note synthesizing Frederick's current tenant protection framework or what gaps exist.
