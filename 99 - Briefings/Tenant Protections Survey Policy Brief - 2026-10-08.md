# Tenant Protections Survey Policy Brief - 2026-10-08

## Executive Summary

Clara's survey is a strong long list. It names roughly 45 tenant-protection tools and collects notes from about 35 jurisdictions and guides, but it only works through Maryland law in detail for security deposits, and several of its Maryland statements come from secondary websites that are out of date. Sorted against what the vault has already verified, the list breaks into four groups. About a third is already Maryland law, where Frederick's job is education and referral rather than legislation. Several items are already in your pipeline through the Real Rent revisions and In-Progress Plans. A meaningful middle group, mostly disclosure, screening, notice, language access, and enforcement design, looks like real room for a Frederick ordinance. A small set, including rent stabilization and good cause, runs into vote math or state authority this term.

**Bottom line.** The most useful thing to come out of this survey is a second tenant ordinance that sits alongside Real Rent: a "Renter Right to Know and Fair Screening" package built mostly on disclosure and process rules, which are cheap to administer, hard to attack as rent control, and land hardest in favor of immigrant and lower-income renters. Clara's own closing note (landing page, law, handbook) is also the right three-part shape for the whole effort. Rockville's adopted Chapter 18 (Section 9) supplies a tested Maryland municipal template for much of it, including a full fee schedule, landlord-paid relocation, and unit-level rent and eviction reporting. The open legal questions on the Group C tools are listed in Section 6 as risk notes for your call.

## 1. What the survey is and is not

- **Author and date.** Clara Jackson, council legislative manager. File dated 2026-09-30, marked "DRAFT IN PROGRESS." The "Compiled ___" line and the per-tool template (What is it / Maryland? / City?) are mostly unfilled.
- **Depth.** Only the five security-deposit tools have all three template fields filled in. The other ~40 are headings, some with a sub-bullet.
- **Notes section.** Excerpts and Clara's reactions from state AG guides, city tenant portals, statutes, two national advocacy documents (NHLP), landlord-side material (AAOA), and several commercial explainer sites. Her bolded notes mark what she found interesting.
- **Her closing structure.** "1) Rental Landing Page, 2) Law, 3) Handbook/other materials," then a list of model jurisdictions. This is a good product plan and this brief adopts it.
- **What it does not contain.** Any Frederick baseline (City Code Ch. 12.5, property maintenance code, the City's tenant-rights web page), any Maryland county or municipal model other than Rockville (no Montgomery County, Takoma Park, Prince George's, or Baltimore City detail even though they are named), any local data, and any authority or preemption analysis.

Each survey tool is placed in one group. "Vehicle" is the most likely route. Preemption calls in Group C are working judgments on legal risk for you to weigh, not legal conclusions.

### Group C. Plausible new Frederick ordinance tools (authority questions noted)

These are the tools that make the survey worth more than what you already have. Grouped into three possible packages.

**C1. Renter Right to Know (disclosure at application, signing, and renewal).** Likely vehicle: Ch. 12.5 licensing conditions. Low administrative cost.
- Code citations on the unit or common areas in the prior 12 months, pending code or court actions, utility shutoff notices (Chicago 5-12-100)
- Foreclosure filing within 7 days and before signing (Chicago 5-12-095)
- Ownership change notice with new owner and agent contact (Rockville, New York)
- Local 24-hour contact posted in buildings under 100 units without an on-site manager (Rockville)
- 12 months of utility cost history for tenant-paid utilities (Maine, New York)
- Pest treatment and bedbug history, mold, pending demolition permits (California)
- Lease translation when negotiated in another language, delivered before signing (California Civil Code model). Directly serves the households the [[City Values Act]] protects.
- Plain-language lease standards and a City-published model lease (New York readability rule, Ontario standard lease)

**C2. Fair Screening and Stable Tenancy.** Likely vehicle: new article or Ch. 12.5.
- Applicant gets a copy of any screening report on request regardless of fee amount (Clara's bolded note, building on § 8-213's $25 line)
- No eviction-history question on the application (Rockville)
- Written screening criteria disclosed up front, first qualified applicant gets the unit (Seattle First-in-Time). Maryland already requires landlords with more than five units who screen criminal history to make a conditional offer first and limits pre-offer questions, so a local fair chance rule would extend that to smaller landlords or go further, as Montgomery County's Housing Justice Act does.
- One rent increase per 12 months for a continuing tenant (Rockville, California allows two)
- Tenure-tiered notice for increases and nonrenewal: 30 / 60 / 120 days by length of tenancy (Chicago Fair Notice), building on § 8-209's local-protection savings clause
- Offer of a two-year lease unless a listed exception applies (Rockville)
- Move-out walk-through right before deposit deductions (California § 1950.5)
- Rent receipts on request and for cash payments (New York RPL § 235-e)
- Right to organize: access to common areas and distribution of materials (NYC, Rockville). Goes beyond § 8-208.1's retaliation shield.
- Early lease release for seniors and people with disabilities moving to care or subsidized housing (New York RPL § 227-a), and Clara's original idea of early release when a tenant is offered an affordable unit. That last one connects to the allocation-principle item in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]].

**C3. Enforcement architecture.** The survey's most important structural question, and the one Clara flagged herself ("Summoning power, can we even do that?").
- **City-authorized repair and deduct through licensing.** Rockville lets the City Manager, after a failed inspection deadline, authorize a tenant to have a licensed contractor fix the violation and deduct up to two months' rent. Because the City authorizes the remedy rather than creating a private lawsuit, this may sidestep the *Aleti* problem that already shapes Real Rent. Worth a close read of the *Aleti* opinion against Rockville's adopted text.
- **Landlord-Tenant Commission.** Rockville's long-standing Commission on Landlord-Tenant Affairs (expanded in Chapter 18, see Section 9), Connecticut's local Fair Rent Commissions (complaint-driven review of a specific increase, not a cap), and Montgomery County's commission. Rockville's version shows a Maryland municipality running summons power, administrative money awards up to $5,000 per unit, and lease termination orders, with awards enforceable in court by the winning party. Open questions for you: whether Frederick's Charter supports the same structure, and how much of Rockville's authority comes from its own charter.
- **Office of the Tenant Advocate** or a staff complaint line that resolves cases informally before escalation (Kansas City, Rockville City Manager step).
- **Habitability standards with response clocks**: heat and cooling (New York heat season, Chicago cooling ordinance, Ontario right to install a window AC), radon testing, security devices and common-area lighting (Texas, New York). Maryland already requires AC (80°F, June 1 to September 30) in buildings of 10 or more units under certain triggers, private well testing every three years, mold assessment within 15 days, and 24 hours' written entry notice limited to Monday to Saturday, 7 a.m. to 7 p.m. A local rule adds value only for smaller buildings or beyond those triggers. Check what Frederick's property maintenance code already requires.
- **Tenant buyout ("cash for keys") disclosure** filed with the City (Los Angeles LAMC 151.31)
- **Condominium conversion notice and purchase right** (Connecticut, Seattle)

## 4. Equity lens

- **Who benefits most from Group C.** Renters with the least information and bargaining power: limited-English households, immigrant families, people with eviction or criminal records, seniors on fixed incomes, and tenants of small unlicensed-type units. Disclosure and screening rules cost them nothing to use.
- **Where options could cut against those renters.**
  - Licensing-based enforcement can displace tenants if a license is revoked. Every C3 tool needs the post-revocation stewardship piece and relocation funding.
  - Disclosure rules tied to Ch. 12.5 only reach licensed units. Code § 12.5-76(a)(5) exempts buildings with certificates of occupancy eight years old or newer, the same coverage gap the Real Rent revisions are fixing. Fix coverage once, for both ordinances.
  - Fee or compliance costs on small landlords can pass through to rent. Keep new requirements to disclosure and process, and avoid per-unit fees in this package.
  - A screening-report or translation rule is only as good as its enforcement. Complaint-only enforcement favors tenants who already know their rights.
- **Implementation risk.** HHS spending and staffing were already flagged as stretched. Each package needs a named responsible Director and a reporting date (Agenda practice).

## 6. Research the survey does not cover yet

In rough priority order.

1. **Preemption and authority analysis** for each Group C tool, as risk notes for your decision. Core questions: which Title 8 sections occupy the field and which preserve local protections (§ 8-209 does, per the vault), whether City-authorized repair and deduct avoids *Aleti*, whether a Landlord-Tenant Commission can hold hearings, compel documents, and order awards without State enabling law or a Charter change, and whether disclosure rules can reach units exempt from licensing. Sources to work from: Title 8 statute text, the *Aleti* opinion, the Charter, and how Rockville, Takoma Park, and Montgomery County structured the same powers.
2. **Frederick's own baseline.** City Code Ch. 12.5 (licensing, including Article V and the new Article VI Rodent Control), the property maintenance code (heat, cooling, locks, lighting, pests), any existing landlord-tenant provisions, and the City tenant-rights page. ### Provisions the survey did not capture

These are the pieces most relevant to Real Rent and the second ordinance.

**Fee regulation (§ 18-163). This is a full junk-fee schedule, directly comparable to Real Rent.**
- Banned outright: fees for services previously included in rent, fees for repairs the landlord already owes, any charge not billed within 120 days, and utility administrative fees (including trash) not expressly allowed by Maryland law.
- Capped: lost key at cost plus $25, lockout at $25 or actual third-party cost with proof, bicycle parking at one-sixth or one-tenth of the car parking fee.
- Opt-in only: parking, internet, cable. Internet and cable billed at the provider's cost split evenly among opting units, with documentation.
- No fee for private storage tied to the unit.
- Anything else only with written, informed opt-in after a disclosure of what it is, what it costs, and how to cancel.
- Regulated and opt-in fees can rise only once per 12 months with 90 days' notice.
- Landlord must refund anything collected above actual rent and required fees.

**All-in price advertising and disclosure (§ 18-180, § 18-147(g)).**
- Every advertised price must disclose all mandatory fees and whether utilities are included. Does not apply to sites the landlord does not control.
- Before signing and at renewal: every fee, whether optional, its basis, amount, and frequency, plus estimated utility costs.
- **Three years of rent history** before signing: increases and lease terms for that unit, and average year-over-year increases across the whole building. Neither Clara's survey nor the vault has this. It gives tenants real information to negotiate with and costs the City nothing to administer.
- Renewal offer rent must match the renewed lease.
- Lease must state the total monthly charge including mandatory fees and which utilities the tenant pays.

**Relocation assistance (§ 18-178). Answers the post-revocation question in [[Housing -- In-Progress Plans]] item 2.**
- *Temporary* (unit unfit, under six months): landlord provides a hotel, per diem, or comparable housing, plus moving and storage. Triggered before the landlord takes possession for code work or within 24 hours of a City unfit notice. Applies even if the tenant is behind on rent or facing eviction.
- *Permanent*: triggered by license suspension or revocation when the landlord stops renting, a displacement of six months or more, the City finding the unit unfit for six months or more, or redevelopment, demolition, or substantial renovation that forces out a household under 50% AMI before lease end.
- Permanent payment: full deposit with interest, prorated rent, **three months of HUD small area fair market rent**, actual moving and storage costs, and an extra amount set by the City Manager for households with someone over 62, a person with disabilities, or a minor child. Paid regardless of arrears.
- Exceptions: natural disasters and incidents outside the landlord's control, tenant-caused conditions, legal eviction, or a transfer to a comparable unit.
- If the City pays to relocate tenants, the landlord is billed, and unpaid bills become a **lien collected like property taxes**.
- Displaced tenants get a right of first refusal to return on substantially similar lease terms.

**Expanded early lease termination (§ 18-146(18)).** 30 days' notice, termination charge capped at one month's rent, for: involuntary job relocation, unit unfit for 60 days, license suspended or revoked within 60 days, unrepaired health and safety violations, domestic violence against the tenant or a dependent, death of a primary wage earner, change in disability status, entry into inpatient substance use or mental health treatment, or other cause beyond the tenant's control. This covers most of Clara's "seniors and people with disabilities" and DV items in one clause.

**Lease terms (§ 18-146).** No jury-trial waiver, no court costs above actual costs, no attorney fees unless court-awarded and found reasonable. If the lease makes the tenant pay landlord attorney fees, those fees are not rent, need not be paid to redeem in a nonpayment case, and the clause must be reciprocal. Executed lease to the tenant within three days (was ten). Proposed lease to the applicant at least two days before signing. Landlord must explain in writing why a guarantor is required.

**Tenant rights and organizing (§ 18-161, § 18-181).** Self-organization, concerted activity, and distributing information are listed rights. Free use of meeting rooms for tenant organization meetings (the prior fee language was struck). Tenant organizations have standing to file complaints on behalf of tenants who authorize them. Local anti-retaliation section covering harassment, privacy violations, service cuts, and unreasonable increases.

**Other.** Full rent payment history within 14 days of request (§ 18-149(a)). Entry on 24 hours' written notice (existing). Late fees capped at 5% after a 10-day grace period (existing lease term). Commission may award up to three times any deposit collected above one month's rent plus attorney fees, unless the State exception applies (§ 18-80(b)(1)a). Household definition expanded to allow up to five unrelated people, with one household per unit (§ 18-1, § 18-96).

**Licensing tightened.** Multi-unit and hotel licenses renew annually, single-unit and ADU every two years. Mandatory denial for three violations in two years not cured within 30 days, or one serious health and safety citation. An enhanced inspection program adds at least 25% more units for buildings with prior failures. Out-of-area owners need an agent in Maryland, Virginia, or D.C.

**Data collection (Article V, § 18-195).** At every license issuance or renewal, landlords report a standard lease, owner contact, building age and size, amenities, included utilities, a fee schedule, and unit-level rent, rent increase offers, rent increases, **evictions**, and rent restrictions. Landlords must keep utility costs and operating expenses by category for City review on request. The housing director reports annually, with some data published online. Rent increase notices must include the City's voluntary guidelines and the landlord-tenant program contact.

### What Rockville did not do

No rent cap, no good cause eviction, no lease translation requirement, no screening-report right, no fair chance or first-in-time rule beyond the eviction-history question, no climate control standard, and no private right of action. Enforcement runs through municipal infractions, the City Manager, and the Commission. Section 18-4(c) says State law prevails where the two conflict.

### What this means for Frederick

- **Real Rent revisions.** § 18-163 and § 18-180 are a tested, adopted Maryland municipal template for the fee and disclosure pieces. The 120-day stale-charge rule, the opt-in disclosure standard, the once-a-year fee increase limit, and the three-year rent history disclosure are worth lifting directly.
- **The coverage gap.** Rockville covers every rental facility through licensing, including single units and ADUs. Frederick's Ch. 12.5 exempts buildings with certificates of occupancy eight years old or newer. Rockville is a precedent for closing that gap.
- **Enforcement without a private lawsuit.** Rockville combines City-authorized repair and deduct, Commission damage awards, municipal infractions, license denial, and relocation costs recovered through a property lien. That stack is your strongest answer to the *Aleti* problem. How much of it Frederick's Charter supports is your call to weigh.
- **Data.** Article V's unit-level reporting at licensing, including evictions, would feed the annual housing production and affordability report in the Affordability Agenda directly.
- **Gaps Frederick could lead on.** Lease translation and screening fairness, the two survey items Rockville skipped, carry the most weight for immigrant renters.
