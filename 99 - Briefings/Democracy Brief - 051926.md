# Democracy Brief - 051926

## This Week

The Open Government Partnership Local Roundup for April 2026 captures a moment when open government practices are spreading beyond the formal OGP membership network into municipalities across Argentina, Cote d'Ivoire, Ecuador, and beyond. The unifying thread is not technology but consent and participation: governments that are co-creating policies with residents rather than announcing them, using public data to make commitments trackable, and building civic infrastructure that outlasts individual administrations. Navarra, Spain ran a mobile citizen service office that reached 4,500 people in its first year by bringing public services directly to rural communities rather than requiring residents to travel to administrative centers. Makueni, Kenya built a procurement data portal as part of an open contracting commitment. Mendoza, Argentina tied open government practices to a new Green Fund for climate action. The diversity of contexts makes the core principle clearer: open government is a method, not a set of particular tools.

In the United States, the most concrete and urgent open government story this week is coming from New York, where Reinvent Albany is pushing hard for the Legislature to make COVID-era hybrid meeting provisions permanent before they sunset on July 1, 2026. The argument is not about convenience. It is about who can participate in democracy. In-person-only government meetings systematically exclude people who cannot take time off work, have caregiving responsibilities, lack transportation, or have disabilities that make travel difficult. The organizations pushing for hybrid meeting legislation include 38 groups that have co-signed the bill requiring local governments to maintain remote participation options. If Albany fails to act before July 1, New York municipalities will lose the ability to continue hybrid meetings and will be required to revert to in-person-only formats.

The transparency technology conversation is increasingly focused on making data meaningful rather than just accessible. The distinction matters. Government portals full of downloadable PDFs technically provide transparency but practically deny it to residents who cannot parse budget line items or permit databases. ClearPoint Strategy's analysis points to a Deloitte study finding that digital capability is the primary driver of citizen trust in local government: residents who are satisfied with a government's digital services rate that government highly on overall trust metrics. The inverse is also true. Governments that bury information in unusable formats are seen as less credible and less competent regardless of their actual performance.

The e-voting and e-democracy conversation is moving forward in cities exploring digital civic infrastructure. The argument that electronic voting increases turnout by removing friction is well-supported, and several cities are using platforms like Consul (originally built for Madrid's participatory budgeting) to manage the full lifecycle of civic input from idea submission to feasibility review to ballot. The Cambridge participatory budgeting program covered in the Civic Brief this week offered in-person, online, and phone voting in 10 languages, which is what accessible democratic participation looks like in practice.

The OGP Local Circle on Digital Governance and AI is meeting this month on May 19 to explore how local governments are applying open government principles to artificial intelligence and emerging technologies. This is the leading edge: the question is not just whether government is transparent about what it buys and spends, but whether it is transparent about what automated systems are being used to make decisions that affect residents, and whether residents have any meaningful recourse when those systems produce harmful outcomes.

## Frederick Implications

Frederick's council operates under Maryland's Open Meetings Act, and the question of hybrid access to council meetings and hearings is live. If Maryland follows New York's trajectory and fails to make hybrid meeting provisions permanent, Frederick would lose the ability to continue the hybrid format that has expanded public participation in council proceedings. This is worth flagging to the full council before July as a policy position worth communicating to the state delegation.

The digital transparency argument connects directly to the dashboard work and the municipal technology strategy in the vault. The finding that digital capability drives resident trust is an argument for investing in the public-facing side of Frederick's technology infrastructure, not just the back-office efficiency side. A resident-facing budget dashboard, a housing production tracker, or a permit status portal would do more for civic trust than equivalent investments in internal systems that residents never see.

The AI governance and open government conversation intersects with the Flock ALPR work and the broader surveillance technology governance brief. If Frederick is going to use automated systems that affect residents, whether license plate readers, housing permit AI, or public safety tools, the open government standard is: document the system, publish the use policy, and create a process for residents to challenge decisions made by or with those systems. The Municipal Tech Consent Architecture note in the vault is the right starting framework.

The democracy under authoritarianism thread across the vault is relevant here. Open government is not just a best practice in normal times. It is a structural protection against the concentration of power. The more transparent Frederick's operations, the harder it is for any single actor (including the mayor) to make consequential decisions without public knowledge and council oversight. The transparency infrastructure is also democratic resilience infrastructure.
