# ADU Talking Points — Draft
**For Item A discussion, August 13 Work Session**

---

**Opening position**

I want to be direct about where I stand. I want more ADUs built in this city. Four years since the last amendment, zero detached ADUs permitted. That's not a success we're protecting, that's a failure we're trying to fix. The changes in front of us tonight, ending owner-occupancy, easing parking, raising the size ceiling, are the changes most likely to actually move that number.

**On the Planning Commission's recommendation**

I take the Commission's vote seriously, and I respect why they landed where they did. But I want to walk through why I'm not persuaded that keeping owner-occupancy gets us what they're hoping for.

There's actually a real-world test of exactly this. A Portland neighborhood required owner-occupancy on ADUs specifically to keep speculative investment out. What happened was the opposite of what they intended: that neighborhood ended up with fewer owners actually living there, 35 percent, than the rest of the city without the requirement, 90 percent. The rule was hard to enforce and it mostly discouraged the honest, first-time homeowner who wanted to build a small unit for a parent or a tenant. It didn't stop anyone determined to get around it.

And on the investor question specifically, California's own data shows the share of ADUs built on corporately owned property tracks almost exactly the share of corporately owned single-family homes generally. No flood of outside capital. The fear is understandable. The evidence doesn't back it up.

**On Mr. Sellner's proposal**

I want to take this seriously because it's dressed in language I care about, ALICE households, AMI thresholds. But look at what it actually asks for.

Fifteen years, fixed rent, one inspection every single year for the full term. Our own rental licensing ordinance requires 15 percent of units inspected annually. This is roughly six or seven times that standard, applied to one housing type alone.

And there's no offset. Every affordable housing tool that has actually worked, LIHTC, MPDU bonus density, tax abatement, pairs a restriction like this with something that makes it worth doing. This proposal offers nothing beyond what the ordinance already gives away for free. Run the numbers yourself: fifteen years of that rent comes to roughly $190,000 gross, before taxes, insurance, maintenance, or financing. Construction alone runs $100,000 to $300,000. Nobody signs up for that voluntarily, and nowhere in the country has a mandate shaped like this produced more units. San Francisco's own inclusionary program, which offers no offsetting bonus, saw production fall 31 percent the year after it was adopted. That's the pattern this proposal fits.

**On the Citizens for Responsible Growth letter**

I'd note there's a tension in the letter itself. It argues these changes won't meaningfully increase ADU production, and in the same letter argues we need to worry about investor activity and parking strain from all the production it's about to cause. Those can't both be true.

I'd also point out CRG's own cost figures, $100,000 to $300,000 for new construction, are the clearest argument against Mr. Sellner's proposal. Read on its own terms, their letter shows exactly why an uncompensated mandate can't work on the units it's attached to.

On parking specifically, I think this one's a fair question and I'll answer it straight: the transit exemption removes a requirement, it doesn't stop anyone from building a space if they want one. And 1,320 feet isn't a new number invented for ADUs, it's the same distance we already use for parking reductions downtown.

**Where I'd go from here**

I'm not closed to an affordability mechanism for this program. But it has to look like something that's actually worked somewhere: an income restriction paired with a real offset, a fee waiver, City Housing Fund dollars, additional size or height beyond what's already free in this draft. That's a trade. What's been proposed tonight isn't a trade, it's a mandate with nothing on the other side of it, and the record on that design is consistent everywhere it's been tried: fewer units, not more.

I want lower rents and better-inspected housing as much as anyone in this room. I don't think this is the road that gets us there.
