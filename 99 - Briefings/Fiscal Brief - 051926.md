# Fiscal Brief - 051926

## This Week

The National League of Cities annual survey of municipal finance officers, published through Governing in late 2025, reads as a coherent warning for 2026 budget seasons. Fewer than half of finance officers believe they will be able to meet their cities' budget needs in fiscal year 2026. That is the lowest confidence reading in recent NLC survey history. The structural causes are converging: ARPA money expires at the end of December 2026, income tax revenue is projected to decline nearly 2 percent in FY2025 after a 9 percent growth year in FY2024, and sales tax revenue has flattened after years of post-pandemic growth. Cities are entering a fiscal plateau after a period of artificially elevated resources.

The tariff variable is new and underappreciated in municipal finance planning. Over 40 percent of surveyed finance officers said tariffs affect their ability to procure some items. Among those affected, over a third said they would simply spend more, while others were canceling or delaying capital projects or seeking alternative domestic suppliers. That procurement pressure is landing on top of existing inflation in employee wages and benefits, which cities have been absorbing for two budget cycles already. The combination is a cost squeeze with no obvious pressure release.

The two stabilizing factors are meaningful but bounded. Property tax collections are still growing, estimated at 3.4 percent this cycle, because assessment values reflect a housing market that remained strong through 2024. The lag in property assessments means cities are still receiving the benefit of 2023-2024 appreciation, but that lag also means a downturn in home values will hit property tax revenues two to three years after the market turns. The other stabilizer is the preservation of the federal income tax exemption for municipal bonds. House Republicans proposed eliminating this exemption during 2025 budget negotiations, and its survival was a significant relief for cities that depend on tax-exempt borrowing to finance infrastructure. The NLC survey showed that over 40 percent of cities would have delayed or reduced capital projects if the exemption had been eliminated.

Kansas City's FY2026-27 budget, adopted in March 2026, illustrates how a mid-sized city is navigating this moment. A $2.5 billion spending plan cuts the previously projected general fund deficit from $100 million to $55 million while adding 200 public safety positions. The mechanism is discipline on the general fund without cuts to capital or service commitments, paired with explicit public budget hearings where residents could submit comments electronically as well as in person. That combination of fiscal restraint and public transparency is the political model that makes tough budget years governable.

New York City's $119.7 billion FY2026 budget is technically balanced but carries what the State Comptroller describes as significant concerns about federal funding uncertainty and slowing economic growth. NYC benefited from reduced asylum seeker costs and strong business tax collections, but the Comptroller's analysis is explicitly cautionary: the City must make balanced and prudent fiscal choices because external risks from federal budget cuts remain substantial. What happens at the federal level with Medicaid work requirements, SNAP changes, and housing voucher funding will land on city balance sheets in ways that are not yet fully modeled.

## Frederick Implications

Frederick's FY2027 budget process has been running through April hearings and work sessions. The NLC picture validates several things the Finance department has likely already flagged: ARPA obligations need to be tracked carefully through December 2026, income tax projections should be conservative given the national trend, and procurement cost increases need to be built into capital project estimates rather than held at last year's numbers.

The densification as fiscal strategy note in the vault is worth revisiting in this context. The Governing coverage of cities cutting deficits through revenue growth rather than service cuts points to a medium-term fiscal argument for housing production that is separate from the equity and affordability arguments. More units mean more property tax base, more utility connection fees, and more permit revenue, without proportional growth in service costs if density is handled well. That argument deserves a line in the production target resolution.

The GASB accounting standards note flagged earlier this year is also live. If GASB changes how municipalities must account for certain liabilities, that creates compliance costs and potentially affects Frederick's credit rating dynamics. The public comment opportunity on those standards is worth tracking.

The preservation of the municipal bond tax exemption matters for Frederick's capital projects. The city has active bond programs and CIP commitments. If that exemption had been eliminated, the cost of borrowing for projects like the Community Bridge restoration would have increased materially. The political risk is not fully resolved; it will likely return in future budget negotiations and warrants ongoing monitoring.
