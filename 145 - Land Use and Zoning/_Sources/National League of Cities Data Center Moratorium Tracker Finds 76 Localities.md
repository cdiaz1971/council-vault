# National League of Cities Data Center Moratorium Tracker Finds 76 Localities

## Summary

A new National League of Cities tracker found at least 76 local governments have attempted data center moratoriums or bans, with most succeeding and three enacting permanent bans, while a separate Georgia Tech tracker counts nearly 40 moratoriums in Georgia alone and Illinois and North Carolina also show strong local activity, including Charlotte's 150-day moratorium.

## Key Points

The National League of Cities (NLC) released a Data Center Local Action Tracker finding that at least 76 cities, counties, or towns have attempted a moratorium or permanent ban on data center construction, and that the majority of those attempts succeeded. Three localities have gone further and enacted permanent bans: Marshall County, Indiana; Monterey Park, California; and St. Charles, Missouri.

State-level activity is a separate track from this local count: several states have moved on stringent rules, audits, removed tax breaks, or outright moratoriums (Pennsylvania, Texas, New York), but NLC's tracker specifically documents local government action, which is where most of the volume actually sits.

Georgia is the single most active state for local moratoriums, with a Georgia Tech Energy Policy and Innovation Center tracker counting almost 40 local governments there with some form of moratorium in place, some running through 2027. Illinois and North Carolina both show at least 10 jurisdictions each that have proposed a moratorium, including Charlotte's unanimous June 2026 vote for a 150-day moratorium on new data centers over rising electricity and water price concerns (existing projects were unaffected). Proposed moratoriums have failed in only a small number of places tracked so far: Cheyenne WY, Gardner KS, and Pulaski County (the source article lists this as Alaska, though Pulaski County is in Arkansas, worth independently verifying if cited).

Beyond moratoriums, NLC found at least 28 city and county governments have passed ordinances specifically tweaking zoning and development rules for data centers, and some jurisdictions have faced resident-led petitions for a public vote or recall elections targeting officials who supported data center projects. Federal-level pressure runs the other direction: at a recent White House event, President Trump urged local officials to accept data center development, and a Cato Institute fellow argued the tradeoffs should be decided locally rather than through a blanket anti-data-center stance.
