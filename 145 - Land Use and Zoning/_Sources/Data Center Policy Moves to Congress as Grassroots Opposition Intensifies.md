# Data Center Policy Moves to Congress as Grassroots Opposition Intensifies

## Summary

The data center regulatory fight has moved from city councils and statehouses to Congress, with multiple competing bills introduced in 2026 addressing electricity rate protection, environmental review, and developer cost-shifting. Industry has poured more than $226 million into federal lobbying. Grassroots opposition continues intensifying -- communities are booing county commissioners at public meetings. No legislative consensus has emerged, and the debate is entangled with AI competitiveness politics and energy pricing.

## Key Points

Several competing legislative approaches are now before Congress. The GRID Act (Hawley-Blumenthal) would guarantee no increase in consumer utility rates due to data centers and require data centers to source energy separately from the grid. The Energy Bills Relief Act (148 Democratic co-sponsors) would ensure data centers pay their own costs rather than passing them to households. The Power for the People Act would direct FERC to require data centers to pay for local transmission upgrades they need. A Sanders-Ocasio-Cortez moratorium bill would bar new AI data centers until national safeguards on safety, worker benefit, and environmental impact are in place -- it has gained little traction.

The EPA proposed on May 11 to make it easier for data centers to begin construction before receiving federal clean air permits, framing it as supporting AI infrastructure. The White House issued a Ratepayer Protection Pledge in March, which major tech companies signed, committing to build or buy new generation capacity rather than drawing from the existing grid -- but this is a voluntary pledge, not enforceable law.

Industry has deployed enormous lobbying resources: the electric manufacturing and equipment sector, including Microsoft and Oracle, spent more than $226 million on lobbying in 2025 alone. The Data Center Coalition frames itself as committed to being a "good neighbor," but the political dynamics show industry primarily working to prevent mandatory rate protection or environmental standards with teeth.

In communities, the politics are raw. County commissioners in Box Elder County, Utah, were booed when they unanimously approved resolutions supporting a 40,000-acre data center campus. This kind of direct community opposition is the same pattern documented in Frederick's own data center research.

## Relevance

This is directly relevant to the Data Center Text Amendment thread. The federal debate clarifies what leverage local governments do and do not have. Congress may pre-empt some local land use decisions or create new standards that override local zoning -- but as of now, local permitting authority is where the fight is actually being decided. The lobbying figures ($226M+) confirm the pattern documented in [[Surveillance Technology Governance -- Frederick Policy Brief]]: industry targets the layer of government with actual authority and floods it with resources.

The Hawley-Blumenthal GRID Act's requirement that data centers source energy separate from the grid is particularly relevant. If that becomes law, it changes the economics of siting decisions and the leverage communities have in community benefit agreement negotiations. Frederick should be watching this bill.

The moratorium debate frames the political poles clearly: full stop vs. full speed. Neither is likely. The realistic landing zone is mandatory cost-shifting and some environmental review requirements -- which still leaves local land use decisions as the primary site of control. That is where Frederick's leverage lives.
