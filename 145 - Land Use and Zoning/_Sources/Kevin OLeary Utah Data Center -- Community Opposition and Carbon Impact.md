# Kevin OLeary Utah Data Center -- Community Opposition and Carbon Impact

## Summary

Kevin O'Leary received approval from Box Elder County commissioners to build a 40,000-acre data center campus in Utah running entirely off-grid via a private natural gas pipeline. The project would generate 9 GW of power -- more than double Utah's current statewide electricity use -- and is projected to increase the state's total carbon emissions by roughly 50%. Approximately 1,100 locals attended the county fairgrounds meeting to oppose it. The commission voted yes anyway.

## Key Points

**Scale:** 2.5x the size of Manhattan. 9 GW off-grid via private natural gas pipeline. Fully outside public utility oversight by design.

**Community opposition:** 1,800 written objections were filed on the water-rights change alone. Concerns centered on water scarcity, Great Salt Lake damage, grid strain, and doubled greenhouse emissions. When the commissioner dismissed written objections, the audience responded with "cowards" and "people over profit." The commissioners walked out partway through the meeting and projected the remainder from a separate room.

**O'Leary's response:** He claimed most protesters were "paid activists bused in from out of state" and that online opposition was being amplified "by AI" -- a notable rhetorical move that attempts to delegitimize constituent voice by attributing it to external manipulation rather than genuine local concern.

**The carbon math:** A single private data center campus adding 50% to a state's total carbon output, funded by off-grid natural gas, represents exactly the kind of infrastructure that community benefit agreement frameworks and environmental review processes exist to evaluate. In this case those processes were either absent or captured.

**The broader pattern:** Microsoft is simultaneously walking back its 2030 100%-renewable pledge, citing AI data center power demand outrunning renewable supply -- the first major hyperscaler to publicly retreat from a climate commitment because of compute scaling.

## Relevance

This is the clearest recent example of what happens when data center permitting is treated as a pure economic development question rather than a land use, environmental, and community sovereignty question. The Box Elder County process failed on every dimension: inadequate public notice, hostile treatment of written objections, dismissal of community opposition as illegitimate, and approval of a project with massive off-grid carbon infrastructure.

For Frederick's data center legislative work, this case argues for mandatory environmental impact review, water use disclosure requirements, and community benefit agreement frameworks with real teeth -- not voluntary.
