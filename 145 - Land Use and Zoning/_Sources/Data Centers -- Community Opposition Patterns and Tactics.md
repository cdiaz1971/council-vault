# Data Center Community Opposition -- Patterns and Tactics

## Summary

Community opposition to data centers in Maryland is organizing rapidly -- a Landover Mall petition gathered 20,000 signatures in two weeks -- driven by concerns about energy grid strain, electricity affordability, and the pace of approval processes.

## Key Points

Taylor Frazier McCollum, a first-time activist, launched a petition against a proposed data center at the former Landover Mall site and collected over 20,000 signatures within two weeks of posting it on Instagram. This illustrates how quickly organized opposition can scale even without established organizing infrastructure.

Critics of data center development -- including environmental and climate groups -- argue that Maryland's current approval process is "poorly planned" and "chaotic," lacking a regulatory framework to protect communities from harm. Sen. Charles Sydnor (D-Baltimore County) has been vocal on this point, describing constituents responding with "deep concern, fear and even outrage" to a proposal in Woodlawn, his district.

A core concern driving opposition is electricity affordability: data centers impose significant load on the grid, and without sufficient tariffs or requirements to bring their own clean energy, those costs can shift to residential ratepayers. Agricultural landowners near proposed sites have also voiced opposition, with one Frederick County farmer describing the situation as an attack on farming communities.

The state-commissioned environmental and economic impact study -- due September 2026 -- is expected to be a key reference point for future regulatory action and will likely influence the Frederick conversation directly.

## Relevance

Understanding how community opposition organizes -- and what arguments resonate -- matters for Frederick's zoning text amendment process and any community benefit agreement framework. The rapid petition growth at Landover suggests that if opposition emerges locally, it can escalate quickly. The energy affordability argument is particularly salient given Frederick's working-class constituents on the Golden Mile. The September 2026 state study may create an opening to revisit or strengthen local requirements in light of new findings.
