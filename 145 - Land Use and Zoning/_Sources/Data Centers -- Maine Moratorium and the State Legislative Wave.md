# Data Centers -- Maine Moratorium and the State Legislative Wave

## Summary

Maine became the first state in the nation to pass a temporary ban on large data centers, with both chambers approving LD 307 on April 14, 2026. The bill imposes a moratorium on data centers requiring 20 megawatts or more of power until November 2027, and establishes a council to study grid impacts and recommend future guardrails. Governor Janet Mills has not yet signed; she previously sought an exemption for a $550 million project at a former paper mill in Jay that the legislature rejected.

## Key Points

The moratorium applies to data centers with power needs of 20 MW or more -- a threshold that covers the large AI-oriented facilities driving national community opposition. Smaller operations below that threshold are unaffected.

The council created by the bill would analyze electric load projections on New England's grid, identify strategies to protect ratepayers from higher electricity costs, and produce policy recommendations for future data center development. This is the study-before-permitting model that Frederick's own data center legislative work has been exploring.

Similar bills are moving in more than a dozen other states including New York, South Carolina, Oklahoma, and Vermont -- across partisan lines. Dozens of local bans at the county and municipal level have also emerged in response to specific projects. The national pattern is communities demanding regulatory frameworks before facilities arrive, not after.

Industry opposition frames the moratorium as putting Maine behind in a competitive race. The Maine State Chamber of Commerce sent a letter to Mills asking for a veto. Critics note that Maine has not been a significant data center market, making the symbolic and precedent-setting value of the bill greater than its direct economic impact.

The political driver in Maine was intense local backlash to specific proposals: a project at the Bates Mill complex in Lewiston and one at the former Loring Air Force Base in Limestone near the Canadian border.

## Why This Matters for César

Maine's moratorium is directly relevant to Frederick's active data center legislative track. The 20 MW threshold is a concrete model for what a power-based permitting trigger could look like. The council-and-study mechanism is the same basic structure being discussed for Frederick -- pause, study, then set standards. The fact that this approach passed a Democratic state legislature and is now before a Democratic governor lends it political credibility as a template. Frederick is ahead of most municipalities in already having community benefit agreement language under development; the Maine model reinforces that a temporary pause paired with a permanent framework is defensible.
