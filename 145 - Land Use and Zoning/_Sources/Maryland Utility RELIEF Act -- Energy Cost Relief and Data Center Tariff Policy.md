# Maryland Utility RELIEF Act -- Energy Cost Relief and Data Center Tariff Policy

## Summary

Gov. Moore signed the Utility RELIEF Act into law on May 12, 2026 -- bipartisan emergency legislation that curbs utility rate practices, cuts the EmPOWER efficiency program temporarily to reduce bills, and creates new tariff requirements for data centers that impose grid costs on Maryland ratepayers.

## Key Points

**Core provisions:**

- **Forecasted spending moratorium:** One-year ban on utilities using projected future spending (rather than actual spending) to justify rate increases. Already produced results: Pepco reduced a pending rate increase by $8.6 million in anticipation of the law.
- **EmPOWER Maryland cuts (2027-2029):** The program offering rebates for home insulation and HVAC upgrades is significantly scaled back for three years, then gradually restored. This cuts the $15-20/month surcharge most households pay but draws criticism from environmental groups who argue it will cost more in infrastructure long-term -- an estimated $1.2B in added infrastructure costs over the next decade versus $640M in near-term savings.
- **Low-income bill cap:** $37M allocated to limit electric bills to 6% of income for low-income ratepayers, funded without shifting costs to other ratepayers initially.
- **Solar auction:** New state-level auction process to fund solar energy projects, aimed at increasing supply as AI data center demand strains the grid.
- **Data center tariff:** A broader group of data centers must now pay a special tariff to account for infrastructure costs they impose on the grid. A tiered system rewards data centers that compensate for their power demands (e.g., by sponsoring power facilities).

**Political context:** The law passed with bipartisan support but drew Republican criticism that Democrats were "admitting Republicans were right" about the consequences of prior energy mandates. Senate Minority Whip Justin Ready (R-Carroll and Frederick) was among the critics.

## Why This Matters

The data center tariff provision is directly relevant to Frederick's pending data center zoning text amendment work. Maryland has now established a state-level precedent that data centers should bear infrastructure costs -- this strengthens the case for Frederick's community benefit agreement approach at the local level. The EmPOWER trade-off (short-term bill relief vs. long-term efficiency investment) is also a useful case study in the politics of energy policy: what looks like relief today may cost more later, a pattern worth naming in any local energy or infrastructure conversation.
