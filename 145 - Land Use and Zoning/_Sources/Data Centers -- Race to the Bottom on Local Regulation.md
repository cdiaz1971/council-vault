# Data Centers -- Race to the Bottom on Local Regulation

## Summary

Local governments competing to attract data centers are surrendering regulatory leverage on noise, water, land use, and tax policy in exchange for tax revenue. UVA researchers call this a "race to the bottom" that ultimately harms communities while benefiting data center developers who locate in whichever jurisdiction is most financially convenient.

## Key Points

The core tension is that data centers generate significant local tax revenue, which creates pressure on municipalities to weaken the very regulations that could make data centers genuinely beneficial. Economist João Ferreira at UVA's Weldon Cooper Center notes that research shows local governments give up regulations specifically to attract the tax revenue boost. The result is that communities absorb the harms -- noise, air pollution, power grid strain, water consumption, and incompatible land use -- without adequate mitigation.

Virginia's data center sales and use tax exemption delivered $928 million in savings to the industry in 2023 alone. State budget negotiations are currently stalled over whether to end that exemption. The tension between revenue dependence and community protection is playing out at both the state and local level simultaneously.

Non-disclosure agreements between localities and developers are a documented problem. University of Mary Washington research found NDAs routinely signed before public participation, allowing projects to advance with no community input. FOIA requests in Culpeper uncovered private coordination between town planners and developers outside normal public processes.

The county-level pattern: localities approve rezoning or zoning exceptions under pressure, often at the edges of historic, agricultural, or protected lands. When communities push back, the battlegrounds are courtrooms and rezoning hearings, not proactive policy.

## Relevance

This is the cautionary tale directly relevant to Frederick's data center ordinance work. The Virginia pattern -- tax incentive dependence, NDA signing, siting conflicts, grid and water strain -- is exactly what Frederick's ordinance is designed to avoid. The article strengthens the case for:

- Mandatory community benefit agreements before any approval
- Cooling and water use disclosure requirements
- No NDAs or private coordination outside public process
- Zoning standards that require siting away from sensitive land uses
