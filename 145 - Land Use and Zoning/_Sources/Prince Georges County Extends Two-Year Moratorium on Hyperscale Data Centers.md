# Prince Georges County Extends Two-Year Moratorium on Hyperscale Data Centers

## Summary

The Prince George's County Council voted on July 8 to extend its moratorium on hyperscale data center development for two years, following nearly an hour of public testimony and debate among council members. Council Chair Krystal Oriadha, who opposed data centers, framed the moratorium as creating space for comprehensive legislation that would lift it once adopted.

## Key Points

Councilmember Wala Blegay, who served on a prior data center task force, summarized the driving concern: "There are too many unanswered questions. How does it impact our water? We don't know. We don't have all of the answers."

Councilmember Sydney Harrison, who is generally favorable toward data centers for their tax revenue, acknowledged the "health, environmental and utility strain perspectives" as legitimate concerns and called for a community benefit task force. He specifically argued that zoning changes allowing data centers to generate their own energy need further discussion. "I believe this is part of the negotiation with the data center companies, and I believe the county sets the rules, not the data center companies."

Three council members abstained rather than vote no, largely over the two-year length. One member, Tim Adams, voted against on the grounds that two years was too long. The moratorium passed.

Council Chair Oriadha was explicit that the moratorium is not a permanent rejection: "If we pass comprehensive legislation, it lifts the moratorium." Councilmember Shayla Adams-Stafford is developing a comprehensive bill.

Pro-data center attendees wearing neon green shirts testified and then left en masse before the vote.

## Relevance

This is the most directly relevant Maryland peer-jurisdiction action to date for the Frederick ZTA opposition. Prince George's County has more institutional scale and data center industry pressure than Frederick, and they are still choosing to pause and require answers before proceeding. The framing from Harrison is the most useful for Frederick: the county sets the rules, not the companies. That is exactly the posture César has taken on PC25-626ZTA.

The community benefit task force model is also worth tracking. If comprehensive data center legislation eventually lifts the PG moratorium, the standards it establishes will be a useful benchmark for what Frederick can demand of any future applicant.

The neon shirt contingent leaving en masse is a behavioral tell. When industry realizes it cannot win the public argument, it performs for the record and exits. Frederick should expect the same if the opposition record remains solid.
