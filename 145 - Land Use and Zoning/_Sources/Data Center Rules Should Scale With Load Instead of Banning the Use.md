# Data Center Rules Should Scale With Load Instead of Banning the Use

## Summary
Ryan Puzycki argues that categorical data center bans are good politics and bad policy: they push hyperscale projects into unincorporated land with less oversight and sweep in small facilities. He proposes treating data centers like any other large load, with zoning tied to megawatt and water thresholds, performance standards, no special tax breaks, and costs paid by the user.

## Key Points
**The backlash.** About 575 restrictions across 46 states, from pauses to outright bans. It is bipartisan: Hochul imposed a moratorium on large data centers in New York, and Abbott paused grid approvals for new ones in Texas. NBC found 69 percent oppose a nearby AI data center; Gallup found more people would rather live near a nuclear plant. Quinnipiac respondents cited electricity costs (72 percent), water (64 percent), and noise (41 percent).

**Scale is the real variable, and "data center" has no fixed legal meaning.** Austin Energy treats loads up to about 30 MW as traditional; Texas defines large load at 75 MW; hyperscale runs 100 to 1,000 MW. One MW serves roughly 250 homes. ERCOT routinely sees 300 MW requests and some for 5 to 11 GW, against Austin Energy's 3 GW peak. Austin's ten existing data centers are 1 to 20 MW.

**Grid timing is the core infrastructure problem.** ERCOT has inquiries for over 730 projects totaling 474 GW against a 91 GW all-time peak, much of it speculative or duplicate. A data center can be built in 18 months; transmission takes 5 to 8 years and generation 3 to 5.

**Water depends on cooling design, not the use itself.** Austin's ten data centers used 131 million gallons in 2024, 0.28 percent of Austin Water's total, compared with 9.3 billion gallons lost to leaking pipes and 2.36 billion used by Samsung's fab. Abilene's closed-loop campus needed about 1 million gallons per building to fill and roughly 50,000 gallons per building per year after.

**Two failure modes.** Abilene courted a 1.2 GW, 875-acre campus with an 85 percent property tax abatement. Water fears didn't materialize, but 5,000 to 9,000 construction workers strained housing (residents say rents doubled) and roads, and an on-site gas plant raised air quality concerns. San Marcos rejected a 200-acre project, then banned data centers in every zoning district with no size threshold. The ban may violate state law, and six projects are proposed or under construction just outside city limits in Hays County, which has no zoning authority.

**Displacement.** Municipal bans don't stop grid hookups, aquifer draws, or construction in unincorporated areas. In Texas 94 percent of land is unzoned (36 percent in New York, 2 percent in California).

**Policy recommendations.**
- Equal treatment: repeal Texas's 2013 data center sales tax exemption (projected $3.2 billion over two years) and skip local abatements. Neither favor nor punish the use.
- Make large loads pay: Texas SB 6 (2025) requires 75 MW+ customers to fund interconnection studies, show they can cover upgrades, accept emergency curtailment, and disclose duplicate requests, and it applies to any large load. Add minimum payments on subscribed capacity and prioritize loads that self-supply or accept curtailment first.
- Water budgets for large users where supply is strained, with closed-loop cooling as a compliance path.
- Map thresholds to zoning: for example, projects above 75 MW or 85 million gallons a year only in heavy industrial zones, smaller traditional data centers allowed in lower-intensity categories, and performance standards for noise, air, heat, and water. Austin Energy already requires 20 MW+ customers to curtail at least half their load in grid emergencies and to pay line extension costs upfront.
- Allow on-site construction worker housing to avoid Abilene's rent spike.

**Benefits he cites.** Loudoun County data centers occupy under 3 percent of land and generate over $1 billion a year in property tax, about a third of the budget. Few permanent jobs, but low service demand. The Rainey Center found support rises when voters learn about specific safeguards.

## Relevance
This is the strongest version of the argument César will hear against the City moratorium and against blocking PC25-626ZTA, so it is worth having in the file. Several pieces support the existing position rather than cutting against it: the size-blind definition problem argues for writing any Frederick restriction around load and water thresholds rather than the word "data center," and the call to end abatements and make large loads pay matches the ratepayer and tax break concerns already in the file. The displacement argument is weaker in Maryland than in Texas because Frederick County zones its land and the Quantum and Adamstown fights are happening under county authority, but it still matters if a City ban simply shifts projects to the county side of the line. The Abilene construction-worker housing surge is a concrete housing impact worth citing alongside the Wiscasset housing-funds case.
