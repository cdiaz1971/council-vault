## Summary

Pennsylvania's House of Representatives passed legislation in March 2026 that increases power costs for data centers that enable AI, shifting more of the infrastructure burden onto data center operators. Colorado continues to grapple with similar questions about who pays for grid upgrades needed to serve large data center loads. Both are relevant to Frederick's data center permitting posture.

## Key Points

Pennsylvania's legislation makes data centers absorb more of the cost of the electrical infrastructure required to serve their operations, rather than spreading those costs across all ratepayers. The political logic: AI-enabling data centers are private commercial ventures whose power demands are extraordinary; it is not appropriate to socialize that cost.

Colorado's situation is less resolved -- the state is still debating the mechanism for cost allocation. The core question is whether grid upgrade costs triggered by large data center loads should be paid by the data center, the utility, the ratepayer broadly, or some combination.

The Kansas state government separately approved transportation tech grants including data center-adjacent projects (drones, AI, smart signals, digital twins), showing that data center and AI infrastructure investment policy is moving in multiple directions simultaneously at the state level.

The federal Sanders-AOC proposal to ban new data center construction until AI regulation passes is the outer bound of this policy trajectory.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick's data center community benefit agreement negotiations should account for the power infrastructure question explicitly. If Pennsylvania's model spreads -- and the political logic is compelling -- then data center operators will increasingly expect to negotiate power infrastructure commitments at the local level as well as state and utility levels. Frederick should understand what grid upgrade obligations, if any, accompany any data center proposal, and whether the city or county has the authority to condition permits on power infrastructure contributions. This is a gap in the current data center policy framework worth flagging.
