# Data Center Host Communities Should Plan for Full Lifecycle Before First Shovel

## Summary

Former nuclear host communities offer a cautionary template for cities and counties considering data center development. Both asset types are large, capital-intensive, place-based, and tax-generating -- and both eventually close. When they do, the fiscal and employment shock can be severe. The lesson is not to avoid development, but to negotiate the full lifecycle into agreements before the first shovel hits the ground: what happens when the facility closes, who pays for decommissioning, and how does the community avoid fiscal dependency on a single employer.

## Key Points

Nuclear host communities provide concrete evidence of what happens when a large industrial facility closes without lifecycle planning in place. The economic disruption is significant, and practically no meaningful redevelopment has occurred at former nuclear sites. Data centers share the structural characteristics that made nuclear plants a fiscal risk: they are large, capital-intensive, generate significant tax revenue, and are essentially immovable once sited.

The key differences cut both ways. Data centers are less workforce-intensive than nuclear plants -- fewer local jobs, which limits economic multiplier effects but also reduces the human displacement when closure comes. On the other hand, data centers can depreciate or become obsolete faster than nuclear facilities, and closure decisions can be made quickly based on business conditions rather than safety requirements that mandate extended decommissioning processes.

The recommended framework has three elements. First, understand the potential speed of closure decisions -- unlike nuclear decommissioning, which is heavily regulated and slow, a data center can be decommissioned in a fraction of the time. Tax revenue can disappear far faster than communities anticipate. Second, plan for eliminated tax payments explicitly in fiscal agreements, not as a contingency but as a planned scenario with written terms. Third, define decommissioning obligations -- site restoration, equipment removal, building repurposing or demolition -- in the regulatory agreement, not after the fact.

The broader recommendation: communities should continue diversifying their local economy during the operational phase so that no single facility defines the community's fiscal future, regardless of how attractive that facility looks at the time of siting.

## Relevance

This directly strengthens the data center community benefit agreement framework for Frederick. The lifecycle planning argument is the strongest counter to the "jobs and tax revenue" framing that data center developers typically lead with. When a developer says the facility will generate X million in tax revenue over 20 years, the answer is: what are the terms when that facility closes in year 12? What is the decommissioning obligation? What happens to the tax base?

The nuclear analogy is politically useful because it is not ideological -- it is a fiscal planning argument that any council member can engage with regardless of their position on data centers generally. It reframes the question from "do we want a data center" to "what does responsible stewardship of this decision look like." That framing can build coalition across the council on the community benefit agreement requirements even among members who are favorable to data center development.
