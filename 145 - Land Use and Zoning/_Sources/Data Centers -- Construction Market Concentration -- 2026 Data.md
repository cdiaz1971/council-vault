# Data Centers -- Construction Market Concentration -- 2026 Data

## Summary

Dodge Construction Network data from March 2026 shows that data center projects were the sole driver of nonresidential construction planning growth. Strip out data centers and commercial construction planning would have fallen 12.7% year-over-year. This concentration dynamic has significant implications for how policymakers read construction "boom" headlines and for understanding the actual leverage data center developers hold in local land use negotiations.

## Key Points

The Dodge Momentum Index -- a measure of nonresidential building projects entering the planning stage, which leads actual construction spending by 12 to 18 months -- rose 1.8% in March to 250.5. Commercial planning grew 7% on the surface. But that headline number is entirely attributable to data centers. Every other commercial sector eased back. Institutional planning fell 8.8%.

The largest individual projects entering planning in the period include an Amazon Data Center Campus in Hamlet, North Carolina (17 buildings at $500M each) and a Microsoft campus in Dallas, Iowa (10 buildings at $250M each). These are not marginal projects -- they are megaprojects distorting aggregate sector statistics.

A separate ConstructConnect analysis puts the broader picture in sharper relief: nonresidential building starts rose 22.8% in 2025, but excluding data centers the growth rate drops to 11.5%. Nearly 90% of spending in the "Offices" construction category in 2025 was data centers -- a category classification that further obscures how dependent the market has become on a single sector.

Annualized data center construction spending reached approximately $47 billion by January 2026. Construction costs per square foot have risen from roughly $183 in 2020 to $415 in 2025, a compounded annual growth rate of nearly 18%.

## Why This Matters for César

This data reframes the leverage question in Frederick's data center zoning discussions. Developers will present data center investment as essential economic growth. The macro data suggests the opposite: data center developers are operating in a market where their projects are the only game in town, which means municipalities that hold firm on community benefit requirements, cooling technology standards, and water use limits are not uniquely hostile to investment -- they are simply negotiating from a position they may not realize they have.

The concentration dynamic also means that "nonresidential construction is up" headlines are not evidence of broad economic health. They are evidence of data center demand specifically, which is driven by AI infrastructure investment rather than local economic conditions.
