# Quantum Maryland Sues Frederick County Over Data Center Moratorium

## Summary
Quantum Maryland LLC, the Catellus-managed developer of the Frederick Digital Campus near Adamstown, sued Frederick County, County Executive Jessica Fitzwater, and the Planning Commission, arguing the executive-order moratorium on data center applications oversteps the County Council's zoning authority and was politically motivated.

## Key Points
The complaint was filed in Frederick County Circuit Court on Thursday, October 8, 2026. Plaintiff is Quantum Maryland LLC, managed by Catellus Maryland, master developer of the Frederick Digital Campus. Defendants are Frederick County, Fitzwater (the complaint seeks damages against her personally, according to Fitzwater), and the Frederick County Planning Commission.

The legal theory is separation of powers. The County Charter gives zoning authority to the County Council, which adopted the Critical Digital Infrastructure Overlay (about 2,600 acres) in December 2025 and January 2026. Quantum argues the County Executive cannot suspend legislation the Council already approved by executive order.

The political theory runs alongside it. A sworn affidavit from Catellus executive Michael Kuykendall says Fitzwater's chief of staff, Patrick Murray, told him in June that Fitzwater needed to "get past the election." The complaint also says Fitzwater told a campaign donor in September that rejecting the developer's agreement was necessary to win the general election. Its framing: "When an agreement with Quantum served her politically, she negotiated it and celebrated it. When the political winds shifted, she did too."

Moratorium timeline. July 1, 2026, the day after the Maryland Supreme Court held the overlay zoning was not subject to referendum, Fitzwater ordered a pause on new data center and substation applications through December 31, 2026, pending the state Data Center Impact Analysis. On September 1 the developer offered a roughly $110 million community benefits agreement ($30M for Carroll Manor Elementary renovations, $30M for an Adamstown park, $14.5M workforce development, $10M youth recreation, $1M fire engine, plus agricultural preservation) in exchange for protection from future land-use changes. On September 13 Fitzwater rejected it ("An industry changing this fast should not be shielded from future local laws") and extended the moratorium to July 1, 2027.

Claimed harm is a land transaction worth more than $500 million that was expected to close in July. Relief sought: invalidate the moratorium, restore access to the development approval process, and award damages. Fitzwater called the suit "rife with factual errors and outright lies" and said she will fight it. The county declined comment on pending litigation. County Council members had not responded at press time.

## Relevance
The suit tests the vehicle, not the policy. Its core claim is that an executive cannot freeze applications under a zoning scheme the legislature adopted. The City's proposal is structurally different: Shackelford's two-year moratorium would be adopted by the City Council itself, and the City's LMC currently has no data center use at all, so there is no adopted zoning for a moratorium to suspend. That makes the City's exposure lower than the County's, but the Trammell Crow text amendment is the pending application a City moratorium would freeze, and Trammell Crow has already argued on the record that a moratorium is the wrong tool.

Worth watching for how a court treats the "applications already submitted" question, since the City draft explicitly covers pending applications. The rejected benefits agreement is also a live data point for what a hyperscale developer will put on the table, and what it wants in return (vesting against future law), which connects to the Housing Certainty Act vesting concern on the LMC side.
