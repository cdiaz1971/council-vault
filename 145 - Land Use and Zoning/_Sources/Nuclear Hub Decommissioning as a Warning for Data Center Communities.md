# Nuclear Hub Decommissioning as a Warning for Data Center Communities

## Summary

Communities that hosted nuclear power plants offer a cautionary model for communities considering data centers. Both are large, capital-intensive, tax-generating, place-based assets -- and both eventually close. Nuclear decommissioning history shows that closures devastate local economies, take fiscal contributions with them, and produce almost no significant redevelopment. The lesson for data center host communities is lifecycle thinking embedded in agreements before construction begins, not after closure is announced.

## Key Points

Jim Hamilton of the Nuclear Decommissioning Collaborative identifies the structural parallel: nuclear plants and data centers both generate significant tax revenue and employment during their operational phase, creating fiscal dependence in host communities. When either type of facility closes, it takes those contributions with it. Communities that grew dependent on the single revenue source face budget crises.

The nuclear record is instructive: practically no significant redevelopment has occurred at former nuclear sites. The decommissioning process is complex, expensive, and long -- the facility sits on the landscape for years or decades before any reuse is possible. Data center decommissioning is a different process but carries analogous uncertainties about what happens to the site, the equipment, and the workers.

Hamilton's recommendations for data center host communities: understand the potential speed of closure decisions (which in data centers can be faster than nuclear plants given shorter technology cycles), plan for eliminated tax payments, and define facility decommissioning responsibilities in regulatory agreements. "Lifecycle thinking should be embedded into every data center agreement, investment, and plan."

The broader economic resilience argument: communities should continue diversifying their local economy during the operational phase so that no single facility defines the community's fiscal future, regardless of how attractive the initial deal appears.

## Relevance

This is the long-term fiscal risk argument for Frederick's data center CBA framework, which focuses primarily on the operational phase (energy costs, noise, water use, community benefits). The decommissioning question is almost entirely absent from local data center policy conversations nationally. Adding lifecycle provisions to Frederick's text amendment or CBA requirement -- including decommissioning bonding or a community benefit obligation tied to closure -- would put Frederick ahead of most jurisdictions and address a real risk that nuclear history makes visible.

The parallel is also useful for constituent communications: "what happens when it closes?" is a fair question that deserves a policy answer, not a dismissal.
