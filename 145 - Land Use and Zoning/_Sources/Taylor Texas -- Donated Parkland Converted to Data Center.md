## Summary

In 1999, a farming family (the Blands) donated 87.97 acres in Taylor, Texas to a public trust for $10, with the deed explicitly restricting use to parkland for the community. Over the following 26 years, the land passed through four entities before the City of Taylor sold it to Blueprint, a data center developer, for $10 million in 2025. A 135,000 square foot data center is now planned for the site, 500 feet from the home of Pamela Griffin, a Black woman whose family has lived adjacent to the land for generations and who played baseball on it as a child. Griffin's family filed a lawsuit; it was dismissed at the trial court level and an injunction was denied. An appeal is pending.

## Key Points

**The Original Deed**
On July 7, 1999, Bland's descendants granted 87.97 acres to the "Texas Parks and Recreation Foundation, a Texas non-profit corporation, to be held in trust for future use as parkland by Williamson County, Texas." The deed condition was explicit. The land transferred for $10.

**The Chain of Transfers**
- 1999: Bland family to Texas Parks and Recreation Foundation (public trust, parkland condition)
- 2003: Texas Parks and Recreation Foundation to Williamson County Park Foundation (non-profit)
- 2003: Williamson County Park Foundation to City of Taylor (one month later)
- 2008: City of Taylor to Taylor Economic Development Corporation (TEDC) for $15,000
- 2025: TEDC to Blueprint (data center developer) for $10 million

**The Community**
Griffin's grandmother bought land near the site at a time when Black and brown people were excluded from buying within Taylor city limits. The family has owned adjacent homes for generations. Griffin's father used to interact with farmer Bland, who promised to give the land for parkland because the neighborhood kids had nowhere to play. Griffin learned about data centers only when local organizers knocked on her door.

**The City's Position**
Taylor's executive director of community services stated that Blueprint did not require city approval to build a data center because the property's existing Employment Center zoning already allowed it. The city's Land Development Code regulates form, not function. The city website's FAQ states: "Can the City just say no to data centers? In short, no." The city projects $30 million in tax revenue from the data center over the next decade, $20 million going to the school district.

**The Legal Fight**
Griffin's lawyer found the 1999 deed and pursued a lawsuit on the grounds that the deed restriction should prevent the data center. Blueprint filed a motion to dismiss; the judge granted it. The request for an injunction was denied. An appeal is pending at the Third Court of Appeals in Austin.

**Property Values**
Taylor's FAQ compares the data center's neighborhood to Round Rock, a suburb of Austin with a population over 100,000. Taylor's population is 16,267. Median home values near the proposed data center are approximately $90,000. Griffin and her family cannot afford to move, and even if they could, selling a home next to a data center is extremely difficult.

**The Structural Problem**
Griffin's lawyer told her: "If you'd been fighting an apartment complex or anything else, you would have won that case." Data centers have acquired a special legal and political status that makes community opposition extremely difficult to sustain even when there are legitimate deed-based grounds for a challenge.

## Relevance to Frederick

This case is a direct case study for Frederick's data center zoning text amendment work and for the community benefit agreement framework. Three specific lessons:

First, deed conditions and community benefit commitments are only as durable as the governance structures that enforce them. The Taylor case shows a 26-year chain of transfers that progressively stripped the original parkland restriction without any community notice or recourse. Frederick needs zoning language and CBA structures that cannot be quietly reassigned through entity transfers.

Second, the city's argument that it "can't say no" to data centers because the zoning already allows them is precisely the gap the Frederick text amendment is designed to close. Employment Center zoning in Taylor functions like permissive commercial zoning in Frederick -- once a use is allowed by right, community control disappears. The text amendment creates the leverage point before construction begins.

Third, the racial equity dimension is central, not incidental. The Griffin family's presence on the land traces directly to exclusionary housing policy that pushed Black families to the city's outskirts. The data center's siting replicates and deepens that pattern -- using community-adjacent land for industrial infrastructure that extracts value from the area while degrading quality of life for residents who cannot leave. This framing should inform how Frederick discusses the equity dimensions of data center siting.
