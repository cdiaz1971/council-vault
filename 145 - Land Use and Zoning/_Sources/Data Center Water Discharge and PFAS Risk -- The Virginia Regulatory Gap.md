# Data Center Water Discharge and PFAS Risk -- The Virginia Regulatory Gap

## Summary

Virginia Mercury investigation reveals that data centers discharging into creeks and municipal wastewater systems face no requirement to test for PFAS (per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances), despite known presence of PFAS in liquid cooling equipment. At least one Amazon facility is permitted to discharge up to 460,000 gallons per day into a creek feeding Lake Anna. Virginia's General Assembly is advancing SB 138, but it explicitly excludes data centers from self-reporting requirements.

## Key Points

**What's being discharged:**
Data centers use two primary cooling approaches: evaporative (non-contact water) and closed-loop liquid cooling (water touches equipment directly). Chemicals used in treatment include sodium hydroxide, sulfuric acid, sodium bisulfite, polyaluminum chloride, and calcium chloride. These are removed during treatment, but PFAS presence in the equipment itself -- particularly in liquid cooling systems -- is the unresolved concern.

**What's monitored:**
Amazon's Northeast Creek permit (up to 460,000 gallons/day) requires monthly or daily testing for residual chlorine, aluminum, cadmium, copper, zinc, hardness, and pH. Temperature must stay below 90°F. No PFAS testing required.

**The PFAS gap:**
Neither Virginia's DEQ nor the federal EPA requires PFAS testing for data center discharge water. Community advocates and EarthJustice describe this as "a dangerous lack of information." The draft permit for the Sedges Creek location includes a clause allowing PFAS testing to be added in the future -- but does not require it.

**State legislative response:**
Virginia SB 138 requires public wastewater treatment facilities, industrial companies using PFAS, airports, and firefighting facilities to test discharge. Data centers are excluded. Bills also require PFAS testing of biosolids. This is a partial response to a known gap.

**PFAS health stakes:**
PFAS exposure -- even at small amounts -- is linked to decreased fertility, elevated cancer risk, and immune system weakening. EPA has documented these risks extensively.

## Relevance

This is directly additive to the existing data center water notes in the vault ([[Data Centers -- Water Use and Environmental Risks]], [[Data Centers -- Potomac River Basin Water Impact Projections]]). Those notes document the water volume problem; this note documents the chemical contamination dimension that the volume framing alone does not capture.

For Frederick's text amendment work: discharge permit conditions and PFAS testing requirements are exactly the kind of specifics that belong in either the text amendment or any community benefit agreement negotiated with a data center operator. Virginia's DEQ permit language -- allowing PFAS testing to be added later -- is a floor, not a ceiling.

The Trump administration's fast-tracking of new chemicals for data center use (EPA announcement cited in the article) moves in the opposite direction from caution, making local and state-level conditions even more important.
