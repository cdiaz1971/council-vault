# Utah Stratos Data Center -- Community Resistance and Environmental Concerns

## Summary

The proposed Stratos data center in Box Elder County, Utah -- backed by Kevin O'Leary of Shark Tank and slated to be one of the world's largest if fully built -- was approved by the state's Military Installation Development Authority (MIDA) on April 24, 2026 and by county commissioners on May 4, without public comment, triggering a protest at the Utah Capitol on May 15 by hundreds of residents demanding independent environmental review and a genuine public comment period.

## Key Points

The approval process moved in two stages with no public comment: a seven-member appointed MIDA board approved on April 24; county commissioners voted on May 4 at a "tense meeting where protesters shouted in opposition" but the commission did not take public comment before voting. No environmental impact study has been conducted at any point in the process.

The project is designed to produce 9 gigawatts of power from its own natural gas plant -- double Utah's current state consumption. Backers claim a closed-loop cooling system that recirculates water, but opponents dispute the impact on the already-dwindling Great Salt Lake Basin. Conservationists point to Locomotive Springs, historically a major flow source adjacent to the project site, which is now producing a fraction of historical flows due to drought and agricultural groundwater pumping.

Gov. Spencer Cox initially said slowing the process was "the dumbest thing ever," then walked back those comments after public outcry and outlined a phased completion plan with a commitment to environmental review of cooling technology. An opposition group, the Box Elder Accountability Referendum (BEAR), is pursuing a referendum to put the project on the county ballot.

The broader context: communities from Virginia to Montana are organizing against data center approvals. Washington and Minnesota have already moved to limit tax breaks. Several other states are considering similar measures.

## Relevance

This case directly parallels the dynamics Frederick is navigating with the data center ordinance. The core failure in Utah was process: a fast-moving approval through an appointed body with no public comment and no environmental review. Frederick's ordinance is the pre-emptive version of what Utah communities are demanding retroactively. The case strengthens the argument for mandatory community benefit agreements, environmental review requirements, and public comment processes before any data center approval in Frederick -- not because a specific project is currently proposed, but because the approval architecture matters before the project arrives.

The BEAR referendum effort is also worth watching as a model for community accountability mechanisms.
