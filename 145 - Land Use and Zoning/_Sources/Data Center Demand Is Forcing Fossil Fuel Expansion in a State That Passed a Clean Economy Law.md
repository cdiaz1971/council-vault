# Data Center Demand Is Forcing Fossil Fuel Expansion in a State That Passed a Clean Economy Law

## Summary

Virginia passed a Clean Economy Act in 2020 to decarbonize its grid by mid-century; data center demand is now forcing the state's largest utility to plan eight gigawatts of new natural gas capacity, threatening the law's core goals and serving as a near-future scenario for any jurisdiction approving large-scale data center growth.

## Key Points

Virginia's experience is the most complete case study available for what happens when a state or locality commits to clean energy and then permits unconstrained data center growth.

The Virginia Clean Economy Act (VCEA) set a pathway to decarbonize Dominion Energy's grid by 2045. It works through incentives for renewable deployment and retirement of fossil fuel sources -- not a hard carbon budget. The flexibility that made the law politically viable is also its vulnerability: utilities can seek regulatory approval to build or retain fossil plants if demand threatens supply.

Data center growth triggered exactly that mechanism. In 2023, no new natural gas plants were planned. Then data center demand surged, and Dominion called for six -- now eight -- gigawatts of new natural gas capacity. A separate research study found that data center demand could contribute to a 28% increase in power sector carbon emissions compared to a no-data-center baseline, partly through "coal-fired generation rebounds to meet demand in Northern Virginia."

Virginia's Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission found in 2024 that meeting projected demand would be "very difficult, with or without meeting VCEA requirements." Even Democrats acknowledge the tension: Virginia's Lt. Governor Ghazala Hashmi said the "single greatest threat to our clean energy goals" is "the sheer speed and size of what is coming at us" from data center demand.

Key figures: Virginia had 81 million metric tons of carbon emissions in 2024, down from 85 million in 2020. That progress is now at risk. By 2030, the state is projected to rank 30th nationally in power-related emissions -- a projection made before the full scale of the current data center build-out was accounted for.

The data center capital of the world (Loudoun County and Northern Virginia) is now the clearest example of a local land use decision -- permitting data centers -- having statewide and regional climate consequences that local decision-makers neither anticipated nor controlled.

## Relevance

This is the sharpest available evidence for the Planning Commission record on the Frederick ZTA. The Virginia case demonstrates:

1. A state with a statutory clean energy commitment could not prevent fossil fuel expansion when data center demand outpaced the grid.
2. The utility, not the data center operator, absorbs the regulatory process -- meaning operators face no direct accountability for the generation mix their demand forces.
3. The timeline from "no new gas plants planned" to "eight gigawatts of new gas capacity" was approximately two to three years.

For Frederick, the argument is that local zoning decisions are the last point of control before this dynamic becomes locked in. Once data centers are permitted and operating, the grid demand is real, the utility responds, and the climate commitments become aspirational.

Cross-reference with Maryland SB 116 (state study due September 1, 2026) -- the Virginia case should be in that study's baseline. If it is not, that is a gap worth naming in the Planning Commission record.
