# Maryland Weakens Commercial Building Energy Code, Deletes Solar Requirement

## Summary

Maryland's Department of Labor proposed building code changes for large commercial buildings (including data centers, warehouses, and multifamily apartment buildings) that would weaken the 2024 International Energy Conservation Code -- deleting a modest rooftop solar requirement and allowing double-counting of efficiency credits. Environmental groups and Del. Lorig Charkoudian argue the changes may violate state law, which permits Maryland to adopt a more stringent code than the IECC but not a less stringent one. Public comment closes July 27.

## Key Points

**The deleted solar requirement.** The 2024 IECC requires newly constructed large buildings to generate 0.75 watts of renewable energy per square foot for the largest three floors on-site. Maryland's proposed code deletes this requirement entirely. Experts at the July public meeting characterized it as modest and cost-effective -- the solar array would pay for itself through reduced electricity bills.

**The double-counting problem.** Maryland's proposal would allow builders to count certain energy efficiency measures toward multiple code compliance thresholds simultaneously. The effect is to reduce the total energy efficiency work required relative to the national code, without the work actually being done. Opponents called it "a lower baseline standard" with "a bunch more things flexible."

**The legal question.** State law allows Maryland to adopt a building code more stringent than the IECC -- but not less stringent. Del. Charkoudian (D-Montgomery), vice chair of the Economic Matters Committee and a member of the AELR joint committee that reviews regulations, has asked AELR to hold the regulations for legislative discussion. Committee Chair Del. Rosenberg said the committee gives "significant weight" to the opinions of subject-matter leaders.

**Who benefits and who doesn't.** The Maryland Building Industry Association (home builders) supports the flexibility; their argument is that the 2021 code adoption was difficult and costly, and more flexibility reduces builder costs and stimulates housing construction. Environmental groups counter that lower efficiency requirements mean higher long-term energy bills for occupants, greater strain on the grid, more infrastructure investment costs passed to all ratepayers, and more climate pollution.

**The timing problem.** This is happening simultaneously with cuts to the EmPOWER Maryland energy efficiency program (reduced by approximately a third after 2026 legislative session), which provides free and reduced-price upgrades for home and building owners. Less new-building efficiency plus less retrofitting equals cumulative higher energy demand.

## Relevance

This connects to the data center ZTA opposition record through the ratepayer harm vector. The FERC complaint and BGE rate case already document that data center electricity demand is a driver of grid strain and rate increases. A state building code that reduces energy efficiency standards for the commercial buildings that data centers occupy -- and that deletes the modest solar generation requirement that would at least partially offset their load -- compounds that harm.

If Maryland weakens the energy code for large commercial buildings at the same moment it is considering whether to authorize more data centers in industrial zones, the two policy trajectories are working against each other: more electricity demand, less on-site generation, more grid pressure, higher ratepayer costs.

This note belongs in the consolidated opposition brief as evidence of the broader regulatory environment in which the ZTA is being considered.

**Action:** Public comment on the proposed code closes July 27. Worth checking whether Frederick has standing to comment, or whether a council letter is appropriate given the ratepayer implications.

---
**Update 2026-08-06:** The AELR joint committee granted Del. Charkoudian's request to hold the regulation, sending a letter to Governor Moore signaling intent to conduct a more detailed study before allowing final adoption. Committee Chair Del. Rosenberg said the committee gives significant weight to her judgment as a subject-matter leader on energy issues. Public comment period closed July 27 with no resolution reached. Procedurally, if AELR and the Department of Labor cannot reach agreement, the agency can still give notice that it intends to move forward, at which point the hold ends 30 days after that notice or 105 days after the regulation's original Register publication (June 26), whichever is later. If AELR votes to formally oppose the regulation, it needs the governor's approval before it can take effect. The hold is a real institutional check but not a guaranteed stop.
