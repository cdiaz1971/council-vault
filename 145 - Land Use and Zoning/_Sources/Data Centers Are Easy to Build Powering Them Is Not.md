# Data Centers Are Easy to Build Powering Them Is Not

## The Core Infrastructure Gap

A data center can be built in under a year. The energy infrastructure to power it can take a decade. This structural mismatch is the defining constraint of the current AI buildout and the core reason communities are paying the price.

Key timelines from energy consultant Suzanne Glatz (co-author of a Johns Hopkins report on data centers and the power grid):

- **Transmission lines:** 7-8 years from approval under ideal conditions, often 10+ years
- **Large power transformers:** 1-2 years from order, with increasing delays and tariff pressure
- **Gas turbines:** Lead times cited at up to 5 years in PJM-area discussions
- **Nuclear (small modular reactors):** Promises of being "5 years away" have continued for over a decade

## The July 2026 Grid Incident

On a Wednesday in late July, power flickered from Washington DC to Chicago. The cause: a data center disconnecting from PJM, the grid serving 13 states and 67 million customers. This is a preview of structural risk as data center load grows without commensurate new generation.

Glatz: PJM is already projecting a reserve shortfall in coming years, with outage risk highest on peak demand days -- which are becoming more frequent and more extreme.

## The Workaround Economy

Unable to solve the infrastructure gap, companies are improvising:

- **On-site gas turbines:** xAI's Colossus 2 in Tennessee runs on 59 natural gas turbines across state lines in Mississippi, operating without public permits and destroying air quality near residential areas. A Southern Environmental Law Center attorney stated this violates the Clean Air Act. On July 27, the Trump EPA announced power plants serving exclusively data centers are exempt from Clean Air Act requirements.
- **Repurposed jet engines:** Company FTAI is converting commercial jet engines into data center power sources as an "urgent" short-term solution.
- **Kevin O'Leary Utah proposal:** 9 gigawatts -- more than double Utah's current consumption -- drawing from the Ruby Pipeline, projected to raise state carbon emissions 64%. Utah's governor has said it should never run fully on natural gas.

## Ratepayer Impacts

Henrico County, VA (37 data centers) recently informed government employees and teachers that electricity bills for government and school facilities will increase 25%. They were asked to help cut energy use.

This is the concrete, local manifestation of the abstract grid burden argument -- and it's an exact analogue for the Frederick case.

## Nuclear Bet Not Paying Off

Meta, Amazon, and Google are all pursuing nuclear solutions. Microsoft's Three Mile Island restart keeps slipping. Small modular reactor breakthroughs remain perpetually five years away. The nuclear bet is real capital being committed to an uncertain timeline, with no near-term relief.

## Implications for PC25-626ZTA

This piece provides the most current national data on the energy infrastructure lag and ratepayer harm. The Henrico County case is a near-perfect peer-city example: documented, quantified cost shifting to public institutions from data center load growth.
