# Ohio Data Center Bill Is the Most Comprehensive State Policy Model to Date

## Summary

Ohio state Sen. Brian Chavez introduced legislation in June 2026 that represents the broadest state-level data center regulation attempted in the current wave -- touching tax incentives, NDAs, water use, utility billing, local government protection, and carbon backup generation. The bill reflects testimony from a Select Committee on Data Centers and is the closest existing model to what a comprehensive state data center accountability framework looks like.

## Key Points

**Tax provisions:** The existing 100% sales and use tax exemption (which reached $1.6B in one year) would be cut to 50% generally, with a 75% exemption for projects built on brownfields that bring their own power. Local property tax abatements capped at 50%. Access to Ohio's 30-year mega project job creation grant eliminated.

**NDA transparency:** NDAs cannot supersede public records law. (Criticized as insufficient -- Sen. DeMora argued it does nothing to stop NDAs, only allows public records access to exist alongside them. The Tucson/Marana NDA precedent from Arizona is the cautionary case.)

**Water:** Ohio EPA directed to develop a water quality testing plan and report anomalies. Data centers must track and report water usage to state regulators and employ water conservation best practices including closed-loop cooling systems.

**Utility rate class:** The Public Utilities Commission of Ohio would create a dedicated data center rate class, similar to an AEP Ohio tariff already approved -- designed to assign transmission and distribution costs to data centers rather than spreading them across residential ratepayers. Ohio Manufacturers Association is challenging the AEP tariff in state Supreme Court.

**Surety bond:** Data centers must post a bond equal to the average salary of all workers over a ten-year period -- described as "financial insurance to local governments and communities impacted by the development." This is the most novel provision in the bill and directly addresses the nuclear decommissioning-style abandonment risk documented in [[Nuclear Hub Decommissioning as a Warning for Data Center Communities]].

**Criticism:** The rate class structure spreads infrastructure costs over time rather than requiring full upfront payment, which critics argue could still shift costs to ratepayers long-term. One project in AES Ohio territory has $230M in required equipment that, financed over 40 years, grows to $850M -- and the tariff would recover only $300M. Environmental advocates wanted explicit directives on carbon-free energy requirements.

## Relevance

The Ohio bill is the most useful single-source legislative template currently available for the ZTA record. Several provisions are directly adaptable to Frederick's situation even without state legislation -- specifically the NDA transparency argument (relevant to any future community benefit agreement negotiation), the surety bond concept (adapts the decommissioning risk into a bond requirement), and the water use reporting mandate (directly applicable to Frederick's golf course effluent comparison work).

The rate class provision is the Ohio analog to what David Lapp is arguing at FERC for Maryland -- both are attempts to ensure data centers bear the infrastructure costs they generate rather than externalizing them to existing ratepayers. The Maryland SB 116 study due September 1 may produce similar recommendations.

The fact that Ohio is moving this legislation rapidly (introduced and potentially voted on within days) suggests the state legislative wave is accelerating. Maryland missed the 2026 session window but the September SB 116 study sets up a 2027 session opportunity.
