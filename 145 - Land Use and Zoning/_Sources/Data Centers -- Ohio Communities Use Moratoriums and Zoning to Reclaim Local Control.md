# Data Centers -- Ohio Communities Use Moratoriums and Zoning to Reclaim Local Control

## Summary

Across Ohio, communities are confronting a wave of AI data center development with a range of tools: temporary moratoriums, new zoning codes, noise and power limits, and in at least one county, a proposed constitutional amendment. The Ohio experience provides a practical toolkit -- and a set of cautionary lessons -- for any municipality trying to get ahead of data center pressure.

## Key Points

Ohio is the fifth-largest data center state in the country, with 217 facilities. State government has aggressively courted the industry with sales tax exemptions that totaled an estimated $2.5 billion in avoided taxes from 2017 through 2024. Despite that state-level posture, local resistance is widespread and growing.

**The governance gap in unzoned communities:** In many rural Ohio townships, data center developers do not need to file permits or seek local approval at all -- without zoning, the community has no formal hook. This is the structural problem: the state invited the industry in, but local governments without zoning have no mechanism to shape or condition development.

**Moratoriums as a first-response tool:** Around 15 to 18 Ohio municipalities have enacted or are considering moratoriums that pause data center construction and approvals. The moratorium buys time but creates its own risks -- Lordstown enacted a ban, got sued by a developer, and ultimately had to drop it in favor of a temporary moratorium. Jerome Township enacted a moratorium and still approved permits during the pause. Moratoriums are temporary by design.

**Zoning as the durable solution:** Vienna Township in northeast Ohio is using its moratorium period to develop lasting zoning changes, including enforceable decibel limits on noise and megawatt caps on power consumption. The township trustee's framing is instructive: "We can't just ban an industry. What we can do is regulate it." South Bloomfield went further and eliminated all industrial zoning to foreclose data center applications entirely. Cincinnati is studying the issue to update its own zoning requirements.

**Noise and power as regulatory levers:** The Vienna Township approach -- limiting noise output in decibels and power draw in megawatts -- points toward the specific technical standards that can be written into ordinances. These are measurable, enforceable, and harder for developers to litigate around than outright bans.

**Constitutional amendment proposal:** Residents in Adams County are pursuing a statewide ballot initiative that would prohibit new data centers larger than 25 megawatts. The Data Center Coalition opposes it, arguing it would harm Ohio's competitive position.

**State-local tension:** Ohio's state government actively recruited data centers with tax incentives. Local governments are now left managing the community impacts with limited tools and, in many cases, no zoning infrastructure at all.

## Relevance

This is the most detailed body of precedent available for Frederick's data center permitting work. The key lessons are: (1) moratoriums buy time but must be followed by durable zoning or disclosure requirements; (2) noise limits and power draw caps are the most enforceable technical standards to put in ordinances; (3) the Lordstown litigation risk is real -- a blanket ban invites legal challenge, while specific performance standards are harder to overturn; (4) Frederick already has zoning infrastructure, which is an advantage Ohio's rural townships lacked.
