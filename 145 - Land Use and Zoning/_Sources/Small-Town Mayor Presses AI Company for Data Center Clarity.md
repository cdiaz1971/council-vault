# Small-Town Mayor Presses AI Company for Data Center Clarity

## Summary

Dowagiac, Michigan Mayor Patrick Bakeman published an open letter demanding that Las Vegas-based Hyperscale Data, Inc. submit formal development plans within 45 days for its proposed 340-megawatt AI data center expansion. The company had announced major expansion plans solely through press releases, without filing any permits or notifying city officials, catching the city off guard and fueling resident anxiety.

## Key Points

**The situation:** Hyperscale Data acquired a 617,000-square-foot industrial building in Dowagiac in 2021, initially for cryptocurrency mining. Last year it announced plans to scale up to 340 megawatts of AI/ML data center capacity -- enough to power 200,000 to 300,000 homes -- entirely through press releases, with no formal submissions to the city.

**The mayor's response:** First-term Mayor Bakeman (a barber shop owner elected last year) published an open letter to Hyperscale Data CEO Will Horne and posted a video of himself reading it on camera while on vacation. He called the announcements "vague" and said they had "bred uncertainty and eroded our confidence that you will do the right thing in our community." He demanded the company: identify which property it is purchasing, submit development plans within 45 days, and achieve full regulatory compliance.

**What the city has already done:** Dowagiac spent months and money preparing to review Hyperscale Data's plans, worked with outside experts on data center impacts, and updated its noise ordinance with enforceable decibel limits after resident complaints.

**The utility gap:** Hyperscale Data claimed an "agreement in principle" with a local utility for power. Indiana Michigan Power, which serves the facility, publicly stated it has no agreements or commitments in place to provide that level of power.

**Scale context:** The Dowagiac project at 340 MW is smaller than the OpenAI/Oracle project in Saline Township (1,400 MW) and a proposed Google facility in Wayne County (1,000 MW) -- but still a significant infrastructure and community impact burden for a city of 5,700 residents.

**The governance gap on display:** The company communicated entirely through press releases and NDAs, leaving the city unable to answer basic resident questions about noise, water, power, traffic, and property acquisition. No permits filed. No formal plans submitted. The city had no formal hook to require disclosure.

## Relevance

This is the Frederick data center scenario made concrete. Hyperscale Data's behavior -- announcing expansion via press release, claiming utility agreements that the utility denies, acquiring property under NDA, and leaving the city to field resident questions without any submitted plans -- maps directly to the governance gap identified in Frederick's data center permitting work. Mayor Bakeman is doing ad hoc what a formal community benefit agreement requirement and disclosure ordinance would institutionalize: requiring developers to submit plans, disclose tenants and power agreements, and commit to community standards before expansion proceeds. The 45-day plan submission demand and the noise ordinance update are both replicable mechanisms.
