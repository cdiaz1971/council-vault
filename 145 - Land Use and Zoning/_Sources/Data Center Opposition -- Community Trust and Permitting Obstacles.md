# Data Center Opposition -- Community Trust and Permitting Obstacles

## Summary

Over $64 billion in US data center projects were delayed or canceled between May 2024 and March 2025 due to organized local opposition. Community pushback is not primarily about being anti-technology -- it centers on concrete, local impacts: energy costs, water use, noise, land use change, and a pattern of opaque deals that exclude residents from meaningful participation. Permitting reform at the federal level cannot solve problems that originate in local trust deficits.

## Key Points

Data center opposition has become a significant political force at the local level. In Warrenton, Virginia, residents voted out every town council member who supported an Amazon data center proposal. In Cascade Locks, Oregon, two Port Authority officials were recalled for backing a data center project. Communities are increasingly learning from each other's fights as developers branch out seeking faster approvals.

A major structural problem: most permitting decisions are made by local authorities, meaning even a supportive White House has limited control over project delays. A review of 31 Virginia municipalities with existing or proposed data centers found that 80% had non-disclosure agreements in place with developers -- limiting public access to information about project scale, energy demands, water use, and grid upgrade requirements. This opacity is a primary driver of opposition.

Real local impacts driving opposition include: massive electricity demand (some facilities operate around the clock), water consumption for cooling, new substation and transmission upgrade requirements, backup diesel generator noise and emissions, and land use changes from average site sizes that have grown to 224 acres as of 2024 -- a 144% increase since 2022.

The argument that communities need trust, not just permits, is the practical frame: enforcement of community benefit commitments has historically been weak, and residents with experience of other infrastructure projects assume loose conditions will not be honored.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick is actively working on data center policy. The community trust and permitting obstacle landscape described here is the national context for that local work. The NDA pattern in Virginia is particularly relevant -- Frederick should understand what information it can and cannot require developers to disclose, and what the precedents are for community benefit agreements that actually have teeth.
