# Evaporative Cooling vs Closed Loop -- Data Center Water Use Patterns

## Summary

Data centers use two main cooling approaches with meaningfully different water use, discharge, and environmental risk profiles. Evaporative systems use non-contact water primarily in hot months; closed-loop liquid cooling systems use water that directly contacts equipment and presents higher PFAS risk but is more energy-efficient in some configurations. Understanding the difference is essential for evaluating permit conditions.

## Key Points

**Evaporative cooling (non-contact):**
- Water does not touch IT equipment
- Used primarily during hot months -- Amazon's Louisa County facilities use air cooling 96% of the year, evaporative cooling 4%
- Water is cycled through a membrane, fans blow cooled air onto data halls
- After several cycles, water is discharged -- treated to dechlorinate and balance pH before release
- Lower PFAS risk from this system because PFAS is introduced through direct equipment contact, not evaporation

**Closed-loop liquid cooling:**
- Water or fluid touches chips/processors directly to absorb heat
- Water is recycled through the system, cooled, and returned
- Requires hundreds of gallons to charge the system initially; topped off as needed
- Expels heat through an HVAC system
- Higher energy cost than evaporative
- Higher PFAS risk because liquid cooling equipment frequently contains PFAS-bearing components

**Key tradeoffs:**
- Closed-loop uses less daily water volume but more energy
- Evaporative uses more water volume but less energy, and poses lower PFAS risk from the cooling medium itself
- Newer, larger facilities increasingly use closed-loop for energy efficiency -- which shifts the chemical exposure profile
- Both types eventually discharge water; the difference is volume, frequency, and chemical content

## Relevance

When Frederick evaluates any data center permit application or text amendment provision, the cooling system type determines what the discharge profile will look like. A facility claiming minimal water use may be using closed-loop liquid cooling -- lower volume, but higher chemical risk. A facility claiming to use evaporative cooling 4% of the year (like Amazon in Louisa County) needs to be evaluated on what that 4% actually discharges.

Permit conditions for Frederick data centers should specify cooling system type, require disclosure of all chemicals used in treatment and cooling, and require periodic disclosure of discharge volume and content. The Virginia model (testing for metals and chlorine but not PFAS) is the current regulatory floor -- and it is inadequate.
