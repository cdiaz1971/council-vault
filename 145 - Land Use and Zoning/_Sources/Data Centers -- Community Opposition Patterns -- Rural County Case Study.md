# Data Centers -- Community Opposition Patterns -- Rural County Case Study

## Summary

A Washington Post story surfaced in the April 13 COG Regional Roundup describes a rural county that was promised a park on a piece of land, and now fears that land will be overtaken by data center development instead. The framing of a "freight train" reflects the speed and scale of data center pressure felt at the local government level, and the sense that community input is being steamrolled.

## Key Points

The story illustrates a pattern appearing in multiple jurisdictions: communities are promised one kind of land use, data center demand changes the calculus, and residents feel they have no meaningful ability to influence the outcome. The "freight train" metaphor signals that opposition is forming not just around the facilities themselves but around the process -- specifically, the lack of community benefit agreements, the speed of approvals, and the gap between what was promised and what gets built.

This is distinct from urban opposition to data centers, which tends to center on water consumption, noise, and industrial character in residential zones. Rural opposition often adds a land use betrayal dimension: the community was told something else was coming.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick is not a rural county, but the dynamic of community trust and land use promises is directly relevant to the zoning text amendment under review. If Frederick approves by-right data center siting in M1/M2 zones without community benefit agreements, it creates conditions for the same kind of opposition to emerge after the fact -- when it is harder to address. The rural case is a cautionary example for why community benefit agreements and meaningful public engagement should be conditions of approval, not afterthoughts.
