# Data Center Resource and Community Impacts

## Purpose

This page sets out what data centers draw from and impose on a host community: power, water, noise, air, land, public money, and risk over the facility's life. It supports evaluating applications, permit conditions, and fiscal claims. Regulatory tools are in [[Data Center Regulation and Local Authority]].

## Power and the grid

- **Scale.** A single large data center can draw a gigawatt, comparable to 800,000 homes ([[Data Center Energy Demand as a Land Use and Infrastructure Problem]]).
- **Timing.** A data center can be built in under a year; transmission lines take seven to ten years, large transformers one to two years, and gas turbines up to five years in the PJM region ([[Data Centers Are Easy to Build Powering Them Is Not]]).
- **Maryland.** Maryland imports about 40% of its power. Power reaching the City runs through the Doubs substation, and new transmission for significant new load could take up to ten years ([[Data Centers -- Power and Infrastructure Constraints in Frederick]]).
- **Ratepayer costs.** The Brandon Shores coal plant will run until 2031 under a reliability agreement costing Maryland ratepayers about $180 million a year, because transmission upgrades delayed by data center load growth are not ready; PJM and the Office of People's Counsel attribute the load growth to data centers, mostly outside Maryland ([[Brandon Shores Extension Reveals Data Center Ratepayer Cost Is Not Theoretical]]). People's Counsel testified in June 2026 that data centers already add $10 to $21 a month to Maryland residential bills through PJM capacity costs (110 bill notes). PJM's senior manager of long-term transmission planning stated in a June 2026 FERC affidavit that Brandon Shores-related transmission upgrades have been delayed "in some instances by several years," and People's Counsel David Lapp told FERC that PJM has unlawfully assigned Maryland ratepayers $2 billion in transmission upgrade costs caused by load growth that is "almost entirely outside the state of Maryland" ([[Data Center Demand Is the Named Cause of Maryland Grid Failures]]). Separately, the Maryland Energy Administration, Office of People's Counsel, and Public Service Commission jointly filed a FERC complaint on July 2, 2026 to end a utility surcharge for PJM grid participation that the Utility RELIEF Act made mandatory rather than voluntary, projecting $20 million in annual ratepayer savings if they prevail; BGE opposed the change and separately proposed an $8-a-month residential rate increase, pending before the PSC through January 2027 ([[Maryland Agencies File FERC Complaint to Recover 20M in Annual Ratepayer Savings]]). The same grid pressure is adding political support for reopening the retired Warrior Run coal plant in Western Maryland with federal backing, even though PJM's independent market monitor and clean-energy advocates say the plant is unlikely to be economically competitive and running it would cut against the state's legally required 60% emissions cut by 2031 ([[Trump Warrior Run Coal Plant Reopening -- Federal Intervention in Maryland Energy Policy]]).
- **Clean energy.** In Virginia, data center demand led the largest utility to plan eight gigawatts of new gas capacity despite a 2020 clean energy law, and Microsoft is reconsidering its 24/7 clean energy pledge ([[Data Center Demand Is Forcing Fossil Fuel Expansion in a State That Passed a Clean Economy Law]]; [[Microsoft Clean Energy Reversal Exposes Voluntary Corporate Commitments as Unenforceable]]). Maryland's Department of Labor proposed deleting the 2024 national energy code's on-site renewable requirement (0.75 watts per square foot for large new buildings, including data centers) and allowing double-counted efficiency credits; legislators who flagged this as potentially inconsistent with state law, which bars adopting a less stringent code than the national standard, won an AELR hold in August 2026, but no final resolution had been reported as of this writing: verify current status ([[Maryland Weakens Commercial Building Energy Code Deletes Solar Requirement]]).
- **On-site generation.** Gaps in grid supply are being filled with gas turbines and repurposed jet engines, which add noise and air emissions locally ([[Data Centers -- Noise, Water, and Community Impact]]). At the extreme, Utah's approved Stratos campus in Box Elder County would draw 9 gigawatts, more than double the state's current electricity use, entirely from a private natural gas pipeline outside public utility oversight, and was projected to raise the state's total carbon emissions by roughly 50% ([[Kevin OLeary Utah Data Center -- Community Opposition and Carbon Impact]]).
- **Emergency grid measures.** A U.S. Department of Energy emergency order, in effect during an October 2026 mid-Atlantic heat stretch, let PJM delay seasonal transmission maintenance and ordered large-load customers such as data centers onto backup power, with temporary relief from some environmental restrictions; PJM named mid-Atlantic data center growth as a contributing stress factor. The mechanism matters for permit conditions: ordering data centers onto backup power means on-site diesel or gas generators running near neighborhoods, which bears on the emission and noise conditions above. [[DOE Emergency Order Keeps PJM Grid Online During October Heat]]; The Baltimore Banner, October 2026.

## Construction-phase impacts

Impacts begin before a facility opens. Frederick County Sheriff Chuck Jenkins reached a new agreement with the developers of the Adamstown-area data center campus to reroute construction traffic through New Design Road after truck traffic caused a tie-up in Buckeystown; Jenkins said the volume runs to "hundreds, even maybe thousands of truck trips a day," a scale most residents do not see. This is a direct, named Frederick County example of a physical community impact distinct from the water, power, and ratepayer effects above, and a concrete case for addressing construction-phase traffic routing in permit conditions or a text amendment rather than assuming it will be handled informally. [[Frederick County Sheriff Reroutes Adamstown Data Center Construction Traffic After Buckeystown Tie-Up]]; Frederick News-Post, October 17, 2026.

## Water

- **Regional supply.** The Potomac supplies about 75% of drinking water for some five million people. Washington-area data centers use about 4 million gallons a day on average and 15 million at summer peak, when river flows are lowest; the Interstate Commission on the Potomac River Basin projects 22 million average and over 80 million at peak by 2050 and names Frederick County as an area of rapid expansion ([ICPRB](https://www.potomacriver.org/focus-areas/water-resources-and-drinking-water/waterresources/planning/data-centers-and-water-use-in-the-potomac-river-basin/); [[Data Centers -- Potomac River Basin Water Impact Projections]]).
- **Cooling choices.** Evaporative cooling consumes most of its water, with 70% to 80% lost to the air. Closed-loop liquid cooling uses far less water but puts fluids in contact with equipment ([[Data Centers -- Advanced Cooling Technology Options]]; [[Evaporative Cooling vs Closed Loop -- Data Center Water Use Patterns]]).
- **Discharge.** Virginia data centers discharge up to 460,000 gallons a day under permits that do not test for PFAS ([[Data Center Water Discharge and PFAS Risk -- The Virginia Regulatory Gap]]). Chemically altered cooling water can disrupt biological treatment at a municipal plant; the City operates its own wastewater system ([[Data Centers -- Water Use and Environmental Risks]]; [[Frederick Water, Sewer, and Stormwater]]).
- **Indirect use.** Power plants serving a data center also consume water, so on-site efficiency does not remove the full water footprint.
- **Local comparison.** City golf course irrigation used 16 to 31 million gallons a year from 2020 to 2025, concentrated June through October, as a reference point against claims that golf courses use comparable water ([[Frederick Golf Course Effluent Irrigation Data -- Water Use Comparison for Data Center Debate]]).

## Noise, air, and health

- Data centers run around the clock at levels near or above federal recommendations. A February 2026 peer-reviewed review of Virginia's Data Center Alley found direct health research sparse and named noise, fossil-fuel air pollution, and water use as the main risk pathways; it recommends siting as the main preventive tool (George Mason University, *Frontiers in Climate*; [[Data Center Health Impacts Research Gap and Site Selection Recommendations]]).
- Residents near facilities in Vineland, New Jersey and Sterling, Virginia report constant humming and high-pitched whine ([[Data Centers -- Noise, Water, and Community Impact]]).

## Land and lifecycle

- Counting power and water infrastructure, the footprint can be up to 100 times the building ([[Data Centers -- Infrastructure Footprint and Grid Strain]]).
- Data centers resemble nuclear plants as host-community assets: large, tax-generating, immovable, and eventually closed. Former nuclear sites have seen little redevelopment; decommissioning terms belong in approvals from the start ([[Data Center Host Communities Should Plan for Full Lifecycle Before First Shovel]]).
- Land and funds meant for other public uses have been diverted: donated parkland in Taylor, Texas became a data center site, and Wiscasset, Maine lost $240,000 in housing funds after pivoting a housing site to a data center ([[Taylor Texas -- Donated Parkland Converted to Data Center]]; [[Data Center Displaces Housing Funds -- Wiscasset Maine Case Study]]).

## Fiscal effects

- **Claims.** A County official has cited over $200 million a year in tax revenue from one project ([[Data Centers -- Frederick County Tax Revenue Estimates and Legal Conflict]]). The City's only project-specific analysis was commissioned by the developer.
- **Revenue source.** In Loudoun County, business personal property tax on equipment makes up about two-thirds of data center revenue. Frederick County levies no personal property tax on ordinary business property ([Maryland Commerce](https://commerce.maryland.gov/Documents/ResearchDocument/Frederickbef.pdf)). The City Charter makes tangible personal property in the City taxable for municipal purposes (Art. VII, § 10(a)), and the City levied $1.55 per $100 on personal property in 2022-23 ([SDAT](https://dat.maryland.gov/Documents/statistics/TaxRates_2022-2023.pdf)); current rate: verify. See [[Revenue, Taxes, and Debt]].
- **Incentives.** Virginia's sales tax exemption costs $1.9 billion a year; Ohio's exemptions cost about $2.5 billion from 2017 to 2024 ([[Virginia Data Center Capital Tax Breaks Energy Costs and Community Backlash]]; [[Data Centers -- Ohio Communities Use Moratoriums and Zoning to Reclaim Local Control]]). Maryland's 2020 exemption is slated for a repeal attempt in 2027.
- **Competition.** Localities have weakened noise, water, and land use rules to attract the revenue ([[Data Centers -- Race to the Bottom on Local Regulation]]).
- **Jobs.** Data centers employ few people once built.

## Equity and security

- Data centers are disproportionately proposed in communities with industrial history, disinvestment, and large Black populations; the NAACP has issued a Frontline Framework for these fights ([[Women-Led Community Opposition Is Reshaping the Data Center Political Landscape]]).
- Data centers are now treated as national security and physical attack targets ([[Data Centers as Critical Infrastructure Physical and Cyber Attack Surface in the AI Era]]; [[Data Centers -- National Security Concerns in Local Land Use Decisions]]).

## What to require in an application

| Impact | Information to require | Possible condition |
|---|---|---|
| Power | Peak demand, committed supply, interconnection status | No approval without proof of supply; on-site generation limits |
| Water | Cooling type, daily and peak use, source | Caps; closed-loop or air cooling; summer limits |
| Wastewater | Discharge volume and chemistry, PFAS testing | Pretreatment; escrow for plant upgrades |
| Noise | Modeled levels at property lines, generator testing schedule | Limits and monitoring |
| Air | Backup and on-site generation type and hours | Emission limits |
| Fiscal | Independent revenue and cost analysis | Third-party review at applicant's cost |
| Lifecycle | Decommissioning plan | Bond or surety |
| Construction traffic | Routing plan, estimated daily truck trips, affected roads | Designated haul routes; hours of operation; road-condition bond |

## Metrics

- Peak megawatts and gallons per day, requested and actual.
- Noise complaints and measured levels.
- Discharge test results.
- Tax revenue received versus projected.
- Local jobs, by wage level.
