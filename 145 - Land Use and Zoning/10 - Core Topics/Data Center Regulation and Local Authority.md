# Data Center Regulation and Local Authority

## Purpose

This page supports Council decisions about whether, where, and on what terms data centers may be built in the City: zoning text amendments, moratoriums, permit conditions, development agreements, and annexations. Physical and fiscal impacts are covered in [[Data Center Resource and Community Impacts]]. Bill work on PC25-626ZTA lives in `110 - Legislation/Data Center ZTA`.

## Definitions

| Term | Meaning | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| Critical Digital Infrastructure (CDI) | The LMC and County term for data centers and related facilities | The use category a text amendment would add |
| Hyperscale | Large campuses, often hundreds of megawatts | Drives grid and water impacts; most moratoriums target this scale |
| Permitted (by-right) use | Allowed if zoning standards are met, without discretionary review | Removes Council and Planning Commission judgment from individual projects |
| Floating zone / overlay | A district applied to a site by rezoning, with its own standards | Requires a separate decision for each site |
| Moratorium | A temporary pause on applications or approvals while rules are written | Most local actions nationally take this form |
| Development agreement (DRRA) | A contract trading developer commitments for limits on future zoning changes | Can freeze the City's ability to regulate for years |
| Large load | A customer whose power demand triggers special utility treatment | State thresholds (25 MW in the executive order) set which projects are covered |

## Frederick baseline

### City

- **PC25-626ZTA.** A text amendment submitted in December 2025 by counsel for Trammell Crow would add data centers as a permitted use in the M1 and M2 industrial zones, without special permits or discretionary review if standards are met. Four City properties meet the proposed standards; Trammell Crow owns land on Gas House Pike. At a September 23, 2026 workshop, Planning Commission members suggested a floating-zone approach requiring Council rezoning for each project, and residents raised environmental and fiscal objections. The Planning Commission has not issued its recommendation ([Frederick News-Post, Sept. 2026](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/continuing_coverage/data_centers/frederick-planning-commission-residents-critique-data-center-amendment/article_24184c5e-4861-5ae9-91ba-b861041ccac7.html)).
- **Moratorium.** A draft ordinance led by Council Member Shackelford would bar the City from accepting, processing, or approving any application to amend the code to allow data centers for two years, including applications already filed, which would freeze PC25-626ZTA. It would fund a study of environmental, infrastructure, economic, and community impacts and lets Council repeal or extend the pause ([Baltimore Sun](https://www.baltimoresun.com/2026/10/07/2-year-data-center-moratorium-proposed-in-city-of-frederick/); [[City of Frederick Two-Year Data Center Moratorium Proposed]]). At the October 8 discussion, Council Member Adkins called two years excessive, another member floated two to three years, and Trammell Crow asked Council not to proceed, arguing a moratorium is unnecessary for review. No vote date has been set ([Frederick News-Post](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/continuing_coverage/data_centers/city-residents-weigh-in-on-length-of-data-center-legislation-moratorium/article_1c387274-19d0-5bd0-abca-9a0cc9025d42.html)). The County's pause does not cover land inside City limits. Citizens for Responsible Growth is the most visible outside supporter.
- **Record.** Council opened its record at a June 11, 2026 workshop with presentations from Loudoun County (cautionary case), the Maryland Office of People's Counsel (ratepayer costs), and the City's Department of Economic Development (fiscal case). The only economic analysis is Sage Policy Group's, commissioned by the developer.
- **Recusal.** Council Member Nash is under an Ethics Commission order to cease participating in data center and energy matters ([[Ethics, Transparency, and Accountability]]).

### Frederick County

- The County Council adopted a Critical Digital Infrastructure overlay of about 2,600 acres north of Adamstown in December 2025 ([WYPR](https://www.wypr.org/wypr-news/2025-12-24/frederick-county-council-passes-map-for-data-center-development)). A referendum petition with over 20,000 signatures was rejected by the courts because zoning is not referable under the County Charter ([WYPR, June 2026](https://www.wypr.org/wypr-news/2026-06-19/frederick-county-judge-rules-against-data-center-referendum)).
- County Executive Fitzwater paused new data center projects on July 1, 2026, exempting projects approved before June 2025 such as Quantum Frederick, and required disclosure of power needs and sources ([WYPR, July 2026](https://www.wypr.org/wypr-news/2026-07-02/frederick-county-will-limit-new-data-center-development)).
- On September 15, 2026 she rejected Quantum's development agreement for its 2,100-acre campus, which offered $110 million in community benefits in exchange for an eight-year freeze on adverse zoning changes, and extended the pause to July 1, 2027 ([WYPR, Sept. 2026](https://www.wypr.org/wypr-news/2026-09-15/frederick-county-rejects-agreement-with-data-center-developer-amidst-public-outcry)).
- Quantum Maryland LLC, the Catellus-managed developer of the Frederick Digital Campus, sued the County, County Executive Fitzwater, and the Planning Commission in Frederick County Circuit Court. Its central claim is separation of powers: the County Charter gives zoning authority to the County Council, which adopted the overlay, so the Executive cannot suspend it by executive order. The complaint also alleges a political motive, seeks damages, and claims a land transaction worth more than $500 million was lost. Fitzwater called the suit false and said the County will fight it ([[Quantum Maryland Sues Frederick County Over Data Center Moratorium]]). The suit tests the vehicle, not the policy. A City moratorium adopted by Council itself, over a code that has no data center use to suspend, does not share the separation-of-powers defect, but its reach to already-filed applications is the point a court would examine most closely (verify with City legal).

### State

- **Executive order (September 23, 2026).** Creates a Data Center Accountability Task Force that rates projects of 25 MW or more against five principles: ratepayer and grid protection, economic benefit, community voice, environmental protection, and transparency. A project denied a required local approval cannot be found aligned. Unaligned projects get no State incentives, and State agencies hold permit review until local approvals are in hand. It bans NDAs between State agencies and developers and directs Maryland Planning to publish a model zoning guide for counties and towns ([[145 - Land Use and Zoning/_Sources/Maryland Data Center Executive Order Brief|Maryland Data Center Executive Order Brief]]). The Governor said final siting stays local, that he will seek repeal of the 2020 data center sales tax exemption in 2027, and that he would sign a statewide moratorium ([[Moore Data Center Order Defers to a Local No]]).
- **2026 session.** Of about 17 data center bills, two passed, including the Utility RELIEF Act, which sets tariff rules for large loads ([[Data Centers -- Maryland 2026 Legislative Session Outcomes]]; [[Maryland Utility RELIEF Act -- Energy Cost Relief and Data Center Tariff Policy]]).
- **State study.** A data center impact study under SB 116 (2025) was due in September 2026 ([fiscal note](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2025RS/fnotes/bil_0006/sb0116.pdf)). The study has been delayed until December 2026 ([Frederick News-Post](https://www.fredericknewspost.com/news/continuing_coverage/data_centers/city-residents-weigh-in-on-length-of-data-center-legislation-moratorium/article_1c387274-19d0-5bd0-abca-9a0cc9025d42.html)).

### Other Maryland jurisdictions

- Montgomery County: 18-month moratorium on hyperscale data centers, passed unanimously in July 2026 ([WTOP](https://wtop.com/montgomery-county/2026/07/montgomery-county-council-approves-18-month-moratorium-on-data-centers/)).
- Prince George's County: two-year moratorium extended July 8, 2026 ([[Prince Georges County Extends Two-Year Moratorium on Hyperscale Data Centers]]).
- Howard County: moratorium and task force ([CCAN](https://ccanactionfund.org/howard-county-approves-data-center-moratorium-and-task-force-legislation/)).
- Anne Arundel County: moratorium approved 7-0 on October 5, 2026, through January 31, 2028 or until permanent legislation is adopted, with a work group to recommend rules on energy, water, utilities, noise, air, and economic development. The bill began as an outright ban and was narrowed at the County Executive's request; projects already in development can generally continue ([WMAR](https://www.wmar2news.com/news/region/anne-arundel-county/anne-arundel-county-approves-moratorium-on-new-data-centers-through-2028); [[Anne Arundel County Downgrades Data Center Ban to 15-Month Moratorium]]).
- Calvert County: Amazon withdrew a 2.4 million square foot proposal after voters removed three commissioners in the June 2026 primary ([[Amazon Withdraws from Calvert Cliffs and Maryland Data Center Political Tide Shifts]]).

## Local authority under State law

- **Zoning.** The City zones land within its limits through the Land Management Code. A text amendment adds or changes a use; a moratorium pauses applications or approvals while rules are written.
- **Annexation.** When the City annexes land, it gains exclusive planning and zoning jurisdiction. For five years it may not allow a use substantially different from the County zoning or a density more than 50% higher, unless the County expressly approves ([Local Government § 4-416](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Laws/StatuteText?article=glg&section=4-416&enactments=false)).
- **Utilities.** Electric rates and large-load tariffs are set by the Public Service Commission, PJM, and FERC, not the City. The City controls its own water and sewer capacity allocation and connection terms.
- **State incentives.** Tax exemptions and grants are State decisions; under the executive order, a local denial removes State support.
- **Federal.** Bills in Congress would protect ratepayers (the Hawley-Blumenthal GRID Act; the Energy Bills Relief Act, with 148 House co-sponsors; the Power for the People Act, directing FERC to require data centers to pay for the transmission upgrades they trigger), bar federal agencies from overriding local data center zoning, and bar federal lawmakers from signing data center NDAs ([[Federal Bill Would Ban Lawmaker NDAs on Data Center Details]]). Sens. Sanders and Ocasio-Cortez introduced a bill (S. 4214, March 2026) to bar new AI data center construction until Congress sets safety, labor, and environmental standards; it was referred to committee and has not advanced ([Axios](https://www.axios.com/2026/03/25/sanders-aoc-data-center-moratorium-bill); [[Sanders and AOC Propose Federal Data Center Moratorium]]). None of these bills has passed. The data center and AI sector reported over $226 million in federal lobbying in 2025, EPA proposed in May 2026 to let data centers begin construction before receiving federal clean air permits, and the White House's March 2026 Ratepayer Protection Pledge, under which signatory tech companies commit to building or buying new generation rather than drawing on the existing grid, is voluntary and not enforceable ([[Data Center Federal Legislative Landscape -- Congress Debates Rate Protection and Moratorium]]; [[Federal Bill Would Bar Agencies From Overriding Local Data Center Zoning Authority]]).

## What other localities have done

- **Moratoriums.** National League of Cities research counts at least 120 communities that adopted data center moratoria and 60 that passed new zoning rules in 2026, up from 76 in its earlier tracker. Tucson moved to disclosure rules for energy, renewables, and water after rejecting a hyperscale proposal, and Cedar Rapids negotiated development agreements that fund a community fund; NLC and AAAS published a key-questions checklist for local officials ([NLC](https://www.nlc.org/post/2026/09/22/as-data-centers-expand-cities-weigh-growth-against-water-energy-and-community-concerns/); [[NLC Finds 120 Local Data Center Moratoria and 60 New Zoning Rules in 2026]]). Earlier tracking found most moratorium or ban attempts succeeded; Marshall County, Indiana, Monterey Park, California, and St. Charles, Missouri enacted permanent bans ([[National League of Cities Data Center Moratorium Tracker Finds 76 Localities]]). Denver paused permits for one year to write standards on water, energy, and noise ([[Denver One-Year Data Center Moratorium -- Peer City Model]]). Maine's legislature passed a 20 MW moratorium (LD 307) in April 2026; Governor Mills vetoed it to protect a mill-site project in Jay, the House fell five votes short on an override (72-65), and Mills signed a narrower bill (LD 713) barring data centers from state business tax incentives instead ([Maine Morning Star](https://mainemorningstar.com/2026/04/29/despite-initial-support-legislature-fails-to-override-mills-veto-of-landmark-data-center-ban/); [[Data Centers -- Maine Statewide Moratorium LD 307 (2026)]]). Sanford, Westbrook, and Brunswick, Maine acted locally after the veto ([[Local Moratorium as the Post-Veto Strategy for Data Center Regulation]]). New York adopted the first statewide one-year moratorium ([[New York Data Center Moratorium and Community Toolkit Resources]]). Charlotte's council initially saw a fast-tracked moratorium fail on the mayor's tie-breaking vote, then passed a 150-day moratorium unanimously (11-0) on June 8, 2026, running through November 5, 2026 ([WBTV](https://www.wbtv.com/2026/06/09/charlotte-city-council-approves-150-day-moratorium-new-data-centers/); [[Charlotte Data Center Moratorium -- Mayor Casts Deciding Vote Against]]). At least 34 New Jersey municipalities, including Jersey City (industrial zones, August 2026) and Red Bank, which also called for a statewide pause, have banned data centers locally, several after contentious meetings with no benefit-agreement framework in place ([6sqft](https://www.6sqft.com/jersey-city-bans-data-centers-on-industrial-land/); [[New Jersey Town Bans All Data Centers After Chaotic Meeting]]). Colleton County, South Carolina passed a six-month moratorium with extension options ahead of an 800-acre wetlands-adjacent proposal ([[Anti-Data Center Legislation -- State and Local Moratoriums Tracker]]). Roughly 1,500 square miles of Michigan, across 51 cities and townships, are covered by local moratoriums; Michigan zoning law limits an outright local ban absent a demonstrated lack of need, and a state moratorium bill (HB 5594-5596) is pending ([GovTech](https://www.govtech.com/artificial-intelligence/1-500-square-miles-of-michigan-off-limits-for-data-centers); [[Michigan Data Center Land Use Restriction -- 1500 Square Miles Off Limits]]). Bills to pause approvals were pending in mid-2026 in Georgia (until 2027), Oklahoma (until 2029, pending water and rate studies), and Virginia (until power needs are met); Maryland had a similar bill referred to committee that did not pass in the 2026 session ([[Anti-Data Center Legislation -- State and Local Moratoriums Tracker]]).
- **Development agreements.** St. Louis negotiated about $15 million in benefits covering digital inclusion, environmental justice, transportation, and hiring; Festus, Joliet, and Memphis used similar agreements ([[Comprehensive Development Agreements as an Alternative to Data Center Moratoriums]]). Frederick County rejected the same tool when it came with a zoning freeze. Negotiating leverage runs toward localities more than developers suggest: data centers were the sole driver of nonresidential construction planning growth in early 2026, with every other commercial sector easing back, and the five largest technology companies announced over $710 billion in combined 2026 AI capital spending, meaning hyperscale operators need specific sites more than any one city needs a specific project ([[Data Centers -- Construction Market Concentration -- 2026 Data]]; [[Cities Should Negotiate Data Center Deals From Leverage Not Desperation]]). A recommended negotiating sequence: require full disclosure of power and water needs, subsidy requests, and job counts before any vote; end NDAs and shell-LLC site acquisition; tie benefits to actual project costs; and set job-quality standards for construction and operations.
- **State models.** Pennsylvania ties tax benefits to bringing new power supply, community engagement, and workforce commitments ([[Pennsylvania GRID Standards -- A State Framework for Responsible Data Center Development]]); its House separately passed legislation shifting the cost of data-center-triggered grid upgrades onto the data centers rather than spreading them across all ratepayers, while Colorado continues to debate the same cost-allocation question without resolution ([[Pennsylvania Data Center Construction Rules -- Power Cost Legislation]]). Ohio's bill would cut the sales tax exemption, cap local abatements, set water reporting, create a rate class, and require surety bonds ([[Ohio Data Center Bill Is the Most Comprehensive State Policy Model to Date]]).
- **Courts.** Minnesota courts paused Google's Pine Island project over an inadequate environmental review ([[Minnesota Courts Used Environmental Review to Pause Google Data Center]]).
- **Public works framework.** The American Public Works Association's Data Center Development Toolbox, built by its technology, engineering, sustainability, and water resources committees, takes no position for or against data centers but gives public works staff a structured set of early questions on scale, location, water and power, transportation, stormwater, zoning, incentives, community benefits, and full lifecycle, including decommissioning ([[Data Center Review Should Start With Public Works Questions]]).

## Process and public trust

- NDAs between local officials and developers were present in about 80% of 31 Virginia localities reviewed ([[Data Center Opposition -- Community Trust and Permitting Obstacles]]).
- In Maryland, a UMBC Institute of Politics poll of 776 likely voters (September 22 to 27, margin of error 3.5 points) found 78% opposed new data centers anywhere in the state and 77% opposed one in their own community, with 80% expecting data centers to raise household electricity costs. Opposition does not soften when the project is somewhere else ([WMAR](https://www.wmar2news.com/news/national-politics/america-votes/maryland-voters-see-higher-electric-bills-other-downsides-from-data-centers-in-new-umbc-poll); [[UMBC Poll Finds Nearly 80 Percent of Maryland Voters Oppose Data Centers]]).
- Opposition polls around 70% nationally and crosses party lines; officials who backed projects have lost elections in Calvert County, Warrenton, Virginia, Festus, Missouri (voters replaced half the city council in an April 2026 backlash), and elsewhere ([[AI Data Center Backlash Becomes Bipartisan Kitchen-Table Politics]]; [[Data Centers -- Transparency and Community Accountability as Counterweights]]; [[Data Centers -- Opposition Impacts Local Elections in Missouri City]]; [[Data Centers -- Maine Statewide Moratorium LD 307 (2026)]]).
- Two cities arrested residents during public comment on data center items, and one then ended public comment for those meetings ([[Darren Blanchard Arrested at Data Center Public Meeting -- Bodycam Footage and the Chilling Effect on Public Comment]]; [[Emporia Kansas Ends Public Comment After Data Center Meeting Arrest]]).
- In Maryland, a Landover Mall-site petition gathered over 20,000 signatures within two weeks in April 2026; a Frederick County farmer described the pressure on agricultural land as an attack on farming communities, and Sen. Charles Sydnor (D-Baltimore County) said constituents in his Woodlawn-area district responded with "deep concern, fear and even outrage" ([[Data Centers -- Community Opposition Patterns and Tactics]]).
- Tactics that have stopped or delayed projects elsewhere: sustained presence at planning meetings (preceded Prologis withdrawing a Washington Township, Michigan application), petition campaigns (2,000 signatures defeated a 244-acre Microsoft project in Caledonia, Wisconsin), and pairing litigation with legislative pressure (Colleton County, South Carolina) ([[Community Opposition to Data Centers -- Coalition Patterns and Tactics]]).
- In Box Elder County, Utah, county commissioners and a state development authority approved Kevin O'Leary's 40,000-acre, 9-gigawatt off-grid Stratos campus with no environmental impact study and no public-comment vote, despite roughly 1,100 residents attending to oppose it and 1,800 written objections filed; the developer characterized opponents as outside agitators, and a resident referendum effort followed ([[Kevin OLeary Utah Data Center -- Community Opposition and Carbon Impact]]; [[Utah Stratos Data Center -- Community Resistance and Environmental Concerns]]).
- In Dowagiac, Michigan, a developer announced a 340-megawatt expansion solely through press releases, acquired property without filing permits, and claimed a power agreement the serving utility denied existed; the mayor had to demand formal plans by open letter because the city had no disclosure requirement to rely on ([[Small-Town Mayor Presses AI Company for Data Center Clarity]]).
- Where city halls and state legislatures have not acted, litigation is emerging as a third lever: Minnesota opponents moved from council meetings to the state legislature to the courts, winning a procedural injunction but facing a funding disadvantage against developers that can treat litigation costs as a write-off ([[Courts as Data Center Opposition Lever When Officials and Legislatures Wont Act]]).

## Authority map

| Lever | Who controls it | Council role |
|---|---|---|
| Adding or removing data centers as a use | City, by LMC text amendment | Direct |
| Moratorium on applications | City, by ordinance | Direct |
| Site-specific approval (floating zone, overlay) | City, by map amendment | Direct |
| Permit conditions: power proof, cooling disclosure, water caps, noise, decommissioning bonds | City, within State law | Direct, through LMC standards |
| Water and sewer capacity | City utility | Direct, by policy and agreement |
| Development agreements | City and developer | Direct; can limit future Council action |
| Annexed land within five years | City, with County approval for major changes | Shared |
| Electric rates and grid costs | PSC, PJM, FERC | None |
| State incentives and permit sequencing | State, under the executive order | Indirect: a local denial removes State support |

## Policy options

| Option | Addresses | Tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|
| Deny PC25-626ZTA | By-right siting without review | Leaves the use unaddressed; a new application could return |
| Moratorium while standards are written, tied to the State study and model zoning guide | Rules lag the industry | Time-limited; must be renewed or replaced |
| Floating zone or overlay requiring a Council rezoning for each site | Loss of site-by-site review | Slower; each case is a political fight |
| Formal Department of Public Works review before Planning Commission review, documenting water, sewer, and stormwater capacity rather than assuming it, per the APWA Data Center Development Toolbox | Infrastructure questions raised after a text amendment has already opened the door | A professional-association framework, not a local standard; still needs LMC language to bind staff |
| Performance standards in the LMC: proven power supply, cooling and chemical disclosure, water limits, noise limits, decommissioning bonds, escrow for treatment upgrades | Physical impacts | Requires technical capacity to enforce |
| Scale categories: allow server rooms and small facilities, restrict hyperscale | Mismatch between Frederick's density and hyperscale needs | Thresholds need defensible numbers |
| Ban NDAs for City officials on development projects | Secrecy and distrust | Developers may resist early disclosure |
| Community benefit agreement without a zoning freeze | Capturing value if a project proceeds | Benefits are only as good as enforcement |
| Permanent prohibition | Ends the question locally | Forecloses revenue and conditioned approval |

## Decision framework

- Does the City have independent fiscal, grid, and water analysis, or only the applicant's?
- Has the State study been delivered, and what does Maryland Planning's model guide say?
- Would approval be by right, or does Council keep a site-by-site decision?
- Can the City enforce the conditions it writes?
- Does any agreement limit future Council action, and for how long?
- How will residents be heard, and are officials free to discuss what they know?

## Metrics

- Applications received, by scale and zone.
- Megawatts and water demand requested versus approved.
- Conditions imposed and compliance reports received.
- Benefits committed versus delivered under any agreement.

## Council checklist

- [ ] Is the item a use change, a site rezoning, an annexation, or a moratorium?
- [ ] Is there independent analysis on the record?
- [ ] Are power supply, water, noise, and decommissioning addressed in enforceable terms?
- [ ] Does any agreement freeze zoning?
- [ ] Is any member subject to recusal?
- [ ] Have State actions (study, model guide, executive order status) been checked?
