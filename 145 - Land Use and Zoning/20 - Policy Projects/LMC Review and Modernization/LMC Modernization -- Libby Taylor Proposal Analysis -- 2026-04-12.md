# LMC Modernization -- Libby Taylor Proposal Analysis

## Summary

Libby Taylor's March 16, 2026 proposal outlines a four-phase strategy to replace the current Land Management Code with a fully redesigned document, governed by a dedicated Legislative Project Manager (LPM) reporting to the Council President and a new Land Use Commission (LUC). Total rough-order-of-magnitude cost over three years is approximately $1.39 million, with a CIP request of $276,650 in FY2027. The proposal is architecturally sophisticated but equity-light -- it says almost nothing about affordability outcomes, displacement risk, or how the new code is expected to serve low-income or ALICE-level households.

## Key Points

**The Core Architecture**

The proposal's structural innovation is the Legislative Project Manager -- a full-time, term-limited position (three years maximum) answering directly to the Council President, not to the Mayor or Planning Department. The LPM heads the code-writing effort, manages the consultant team, and coordinates with the Land Use Commission. This governance design explicitly separates long-range LMC work from the Planning Commission's active project review load, which is a real operational problem in many jurisdictions. It concentrates LMC authority in the council rather than the executive branch, which has significant political implications.

**Four Phases**

Phase 1 runs concurrently on two tracks: policy alignment (reaffirming the Comprehensive Plan as the primary land use document, identifying and resolving 5-10 code-versus-Comp-Plan contradictions via immediate text amendments) and institutional setup (appointing the Land Use Commission, hiring the LPM). Phase 2 has the LPM and LUC analyze regulatory instrument options -- Euclidean, form-based, incentive zoning, C/R zoning, standard/optional methods -- and bring a recommendation to council for formal adoption by resolution before any consultant procurement begins. Phase 3 is the design and drafting phase: the consultant team authors the Staff Draft with intensive public engagement, the LUC endorses it, and a 270-day development moratorium is triggered upon transmission to council. Phase 4 is legislative adoption -- council revises the Staff Draft into a Council Draft, holds public hearings, and adopts. Moratorium lifts upon adoption.

**The Development Moratorium**

The moratorium is the most politically volatile element. It suspends new annexations, new master plans, preliminary subdivision plats, and private text amendments from the moment the LUC-endorsed Staff Draft goes to council until adoption or 270 days, whichever comes first. Ministerial approvals -- final site plans, building permits, forest conservation plans, zoning certificates, HPC approvals -- remain exempt. The proposal frames this as protecting code integrity during the final legislative push. Opponents will frame it as halting development during a housing shortage. César should expect significant developer and builder opposition to this element specifically, and needs to decide whether to embrace it, modify it, or hold it at arm's length until the resolution passes.

**Three-Year Cost Estimate**

Year One (FY2027 CIP request: $276,650): $239,500 for the LPM (salary $155K plus 50% benefits, startup, supplies), $4,000 for community engagement, $8,000 for Land Use Commission stipends and supplies. No consultant cost in Year One. Year Two (FY2028 CIP request: $613,044): adds the consultant team at $304,000 (labor plus reimbursables), LPM continues at $240,313, LUC at $8,000, community engagement at $5,000. Year Three (FY2029 CIP request: $501,897): consultant at $202,000, LPM at $245,270, LUC at $8,000, community engagement at $1,000. Each year includes a 10% contingency. The LPM salary at $155K/year (plus 50% benefits) is the dominant cost driver across all three years.

**Division II Strategy**

The proposal includes a longer-horizon goal: transition Frederick from Division I to Division II under the Maryland Land Use Article. Division I requires a mandated 10-year Comprehensive Plan update cycle with a static long-term snapshot structure. Division II allows continuous updates without a fixed deadline, using a dynamic sector-based model. The proposal argues that a well-functioning SAP process demonstrates the "perpetual planning" capacity required to qualify for Division II status. This is aspirational -- it requires engagement with the Maryland Department of Planning and is not something the council can unilaterally accomplish.

**What the Proposal Does Not Address**

The proposal contains no affordability metrics, no AMI targets, no analysis of displacement risk during the moratorium period, no mention of ALICE households, no tenant protections, and no equity evaluation framework. It treats the LMC modernization as a technical and governance challenge, not a distributive one. The "continuous improvement" framing and Division II strategy are forward-looking but say nothing about who the improved code is intended to serve. This is the gap César needs to fill if he chooses to advance this proposal's elements alongside or within the commission resolution.

## Relevance

This proposal functions as the detailed implementation blueprint that sits behind the draft commission resolution. The LPM position reporting to the Council President is notable -- if adopted, it gives César direct operational control over the code-writing process, not just appointment authority over the advisory commission. The three-year cost structure needs to be reconciled with the FY2027 budget cycle underway now; the $276,650 Year One request would need to be identified as a CIP addition with an offsetting cut. The moratorium proposal intersects with César's housing production targets -- a moratorium during code rewrite risks slowing supply during a shortage and needs equity analysis before César can take a position.
