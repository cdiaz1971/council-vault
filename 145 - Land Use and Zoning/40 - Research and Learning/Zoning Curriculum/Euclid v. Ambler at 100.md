# Euclid v. Ambler at 100

## Summary
Aaron Lubeck's essay, part of Southern Urbanism's "American Land Use at 250" series, revisits the 1926 Supreme Court decision that established zoning as a legitimate municipal police power, arguing the ruling's real legacy is a Progressive-era faith in expert administration over market and individual judgment.

## Key Points
In 1922 the village of Euclid, Ohio adopted a comprehensive zoning ordinance dividing land into use, height, and area districts. Ambler Realty, which owned about 68 acres along Euclid Avenue and the Nickel Plate railroad, sued, and the case reached the Supreme Court in 1926, which upheld zoning as a valid exercise of police power.

Lubeck's argument centers on Ambler's attorney, who mocked the planning movement's confidence that credentialed experts, planners, sanitarians, and civic commissions could out-order what markets and individual owners were doing on their own. Lubeck traces that confidence to the same early twentieth century currents that produced scientific management and, in some quarters, eugenic thinking about improving the population through social separation.

He frames Euclid as one of several twentieth century judicial missteps that constrained the freedom to build and drove up housing costs, part of a five essay series in the same collection that approaches the same problem from different angles: constitutional design, planning as a stunted form of imagination, and the old quarrel between city, farm, and suburb.

## Relevance
This gives the founding legal and ideological backstory for Euclidean zoning, directly useful for the self-directed urban planning curriculum's Week 1 work on zoning typologies and the Euclidean, performance-based, and form-based distinctions. It also supplies historical ammunition for the LMC rewrite's move toward form-based code, since it names the specific intellectual assumptions (credentialed expertise over market judgment) that the rewrite is arguing against.
