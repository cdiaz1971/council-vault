# Zoning's Racial and Exclusionary Origins

## Before comprehensive zoning: explicit racial ordinances
Land use regulation in American cities predates 1916. Los Angeles passed ordinances as early as 1904 restricting where "wash houses" (laundries, mostly Chinese-owned) could operate, and by 1908 was zoning some areas explicitly to enforce racial segregation. Other cities passed ordinances barring Black residents from moving onto blocks that were majority white.

## Buchanan v. Warley and the pivot to facially neutral tools
In 1917, the U.S. Supreme Court struck down explicit racial zoning in Buchanan v. Warley, ruling a Louisville ordinance that barred Black residents from certain blocks unconstitutional. This did not end exclusion. It changed its form. After 1917, localities increasingly turned to tools that never mentioned race directly: minimum lot sizes, single-family-only districts, use separation, all of which could produce similar segregation outcomes while surviving legal challenge because they were, on paper, about land use rather than about people.

## Class motives inside the 1916 ordinance itself
Even NYC's founding ordinance, the one this curriculum treats as the origin point, was not purely a technical response to light and air. Historian Seymour Toll, in his 1969 book Zoned America, traced the ordinance's push to Fifth Avenue merchants whose high-end shops were being crowded by garment manufacturers and the immigrant workforce that came with them. Protecting property values and social exclusivity was part of the motivation from the start, not a later corruption of a neutral tool.

## Why this note exists in week 1, not week 9
The main curriculum note on exclusionary zoning's full history sits in Module 3 (week 9), closer to the housing supply material it connects to. But putting the caveat here, in week one, matters because the intro note's account (light, air, fire safety, use conflict) is true and also incomplete on its own. Holding both threads from the start, that zoning solved real problems and that it was a tool of exclusion from its founding, is the more accurate picture, and it's the one worth carrying into every ordinance you evaluate as council president.
