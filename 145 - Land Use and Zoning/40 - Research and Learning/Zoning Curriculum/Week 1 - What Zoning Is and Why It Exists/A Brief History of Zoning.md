# A Brief History of Zoning

## Summary
A recap video tracing zoning's evolution from Los Angeles's 1908 use-separation code through the 1916 NYC ordinance, the 1924 Standard State Zoning Enabling Act, and Euclid v. Ambler, then laying out the three major zoning approaches that followed: Euclidean, performance, and form-based. The historical ground (1916, SZEA, exclusionary origins) is covered in more depth in the existing Week 1 notes; this note's main contribution is the typology framework and its concrete illustrations of how form-based zoning differs from Euclidean and performance zoning in practice.

## Key Points

**Historical timeline (brief, see linked notes for depth).** Los Angeles's 1908 code was the first in the US, a mapless two-use split between residential and industrial that also functioned to segregate Chinese-owned laundries, classified as industrial, into separate districts. New York's 1916 resolution followed with the first mapped use, height, and area zones, producing the stepped-back "wedding cake" form to preserve light and air. The 1924 Standard State Zoning Enabling Act, sponsored by the Department of Commerce, gave states model legislation to delegate zoning authority to cities, and its language (health/safety/welfare, variances, boards of zoning appeals) still underlies most zoning codes today. Euclid v. Ambler (1926) settled zoning's constitutionality as a police-power exercise, framed around separating noxious uses, though the opinion's concern about apartments encroaching on single-family districts shows exclusionary motives were present alongside the stated public-health rationale.

**Euclidean zoning** separates land into use-based districts (Atlanta's 1929 map, for example, had just four: dwellings, apartments, industrial, business). It successfully separates incompatible uses and stabilizes property values, but it spreads uses apart in ways that reduce walkability, pushes development toward auto-orientation, and, by hardening into single-family-only versus multifamily-only zones over time, eliminated missing-middle housing types and contributed to income segregation and reduced affordability.

**Performance zoning** layers numeric standards, parking ratios, floor area ratio, open space requirements, onto the same district structure, emerging from a mid-20th-century view of cities as systems that could be optimized through metrics. It adds real context-sensitivity (different ratios for urban versus suburban districts) but the metrics were mostly observational rather than scientific, and stacking many of them together tends to produce unpredictable, inflexible outcomes.

**Form-based zoning**, the approach that has gained traction over the last 10-15 years, prioritizes a building's physical form and its relationship to the street over the use inside it. It responds to existing development patterns or an adopted community vision rather than imposing a use-first framework, and it trades some of Euclidean zoning's use-based predictability for flexibility, though it still needs use and bulk standards to be right-sized or it can create its own barriers to small-scale infill.

**How form-based zoning changes specific elements**, illustrated in the video:
- Building placement: required proximity to the sidewalk/street rather than just a minimum setback
- Parking location: pushed to the rear or interior of a site rather than left to face the street
- Building height: regulated transitions between adjacent buildings so height changes gradually rather than abruptly
- Windows and doors: active-facade requirements aimed at pedestrian interest, which use-based codes don't typically address
- Public space: prioritizes genuinely usable, accessible public space rather than a bare minimum open-space requirement
- Uses: still regulated, but for impact rather than as a flat allow/prohibit list

**Present-day codes are a blend of all three.** Atlanta's original 1920s zoning was a short pamphlet; its current code is a large binder incorporating Euclidean, performance, and form-based elements layered over time, which the video frames as typical of most US cities, Frederick included.
