# The 1916 NYC Zoning Resolution

## The popular story, and why it's incomplete
The standard telling says the Equitable Building, a 40-story tower that rose straight up from the sidewalk at 120 Broadway in 1915 and threw a seven-acre shadow, so alarmed the city that officials rushed to pass zoning. That building was real, and it mattered as a symbol. But the formal push for zoning began by at least February 1913, driven mainly by the Fifth Avenue Association, a group of merchants and hotel owners fighting to keep garment manufacturers and their loft buildings out of the fashionable retail stretch of Fifth Avenue. Research into the 299-page Final Report of the Commission on Building Districts and Restrictions found the Equitable Building mentioned on only four of its pages. It was one example among many, not the trigger.

## What the ordinance actually did
Adopted July 25, 1916, by the Board of Estimate and Apportionment, the resolution was "comprehensive" because every parcel in the city fell under it. It worked on two axes at once.

**Use districts.** The city was divided into three categories: residential, commercial (offices, retail, light industrial), and unrestricted (heavy industry, utilities, port facilities). About two-fifths of Manhattan and two-thirds of the whole city was zoned residential.

**Height and bulk, the "envelope."** Rather than a flat height cap, the ordinance set a ratio of setback to height that varied by district. A building could rise straight up to a certain point, then had to step back along an angled line as it went higher. This produced the "wedding cake" massing you can still see across Manhattan, most famously 120 Wall Street, built in 1930. Lot coverage was also capped by district letter, from as little as 50 percent ground-floor coverage in the most restricted "E" districts up to nearly 100 percent in the least restricted "A" districts.

## Who wrote it
The chief authors were George McAneny, then Manhattan Borough President, and Edward M. Bassett, a lawyer who became the central figure in American zoning law for the next two decades and later helped write the Standard State Zoning Enabling Act. Bassett's name will come back throughout this curriculum.

## Why this matters for the curriculum
This ordinance is the ancestor of every zoning code you'll touch in Frederick, including the underlying logic of use districts and bulk envelopes. But treating it as a clean, single-motive story, dark streets in, sensible regulation out, misses the class interests baked into it from day one.
