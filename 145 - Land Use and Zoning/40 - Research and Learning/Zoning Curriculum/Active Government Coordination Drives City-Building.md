# Active Government Coordination Drives City-Building

## Summary
A review of Benjamin Schneider's "The Unfinished Metropolis" argues that American cities have largely abandoned an era of active, government-led city-building in favor of reactive permitting, and that reviving deliberate coordination, consensus-building, and public funding at municipal scale, not a return to top-down urban renewal, is what other countries are doing better.

## Key Points
Schneider's core argument is that city-building drove past eras of American development but is "profoundly absent" today, replaced by a reactive posture where government responds to private proposals rather than setting direction.

His prescription differs from mid-century urban renewal specifically by casting government's role as coordination, consensus-building, and funding at a scale only government can provide, not direct execution.

The reviewer frames cities as potential "laboratories that pilot the larger-scale changes" ultimately needed nationally, an argument for municipal-level experimentation rather than waiting on federal or state action.

## Relevance
Speaks directly to the governance posture question underneath several active Frederick threads, particularly Golden Mile Small Area Plan implementation and Inclusionary Zoning, where the recurring tension is whether the city drives redevelopment proactively or waits on private applications. Worth folding into the zoning and urban planning curriculum as a framing text alongside the comparative international examples in [[Comparative International Models for City-Building]].
