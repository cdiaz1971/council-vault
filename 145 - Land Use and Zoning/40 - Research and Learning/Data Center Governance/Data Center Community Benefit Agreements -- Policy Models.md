# Data Center Community Benefit Agreements -- Policy Models

## Summary

Community benefit agreements (CBAs) have emerged as the primary mechanism for ensuring data center development produces local returns rather than just extracting local resources. Several cities have negotiated meaningful agreements with measurable commitments on jobs, wages, taxes, and grid costs. The quality of negotiation and enforceability of conditions determines whether CBAs are real protections or public relations.

## Key Points

Cleveland has used CBAs for economic development projects since 2013. It distinguishes between standard agreements (projects under $20M) and expanded agreements for larger investments. Developers must specify hiring targets, meet low-income employment goals, and create mentorships, apprenticeships, and internships.

Lancaster, Pennsylvania negotiated a CBA with Chirisa Technology Parks for a 450,000-square-foot data center backed by a $6 billion CoreWeave commitment. The agreement was structured after local officials worked directly with the company rather than simply approving the permit.

Cedar Rapids, Iowa negotiated a 20-year, 70% tax exemption with Google and QTS contingent on maintaining at least 31 full-time jobs at a minimum wage of $26.20/hour, plus $400,000 annually to the city from Google and $18 million over 18 years from QTS.

West Des Moines negotiated with Microsoft to require 100% renewable energy use and secured over $2 billion in projected tax revenues over the life of the agreement.

Key design considerations: tax abatement length (agreements range from 10 to 50 years), trigger conditions for full incentive payment, enforceability of community benefit commitments, and public access to projected tax revenue modeling so residents can evaluate whether projections are realistic.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick's data center policy work needs to go beyond permitting standards to address economic terms. What should a data center owe the community in exchange for local approval, tax incentives, and grid infrastructure investment? These peer examples give Frederick concrete reference points for negotiating commitments on jobs, wages, renewables, and tax contribution -- and for structuring agreements that are enforceable rather than aspirational.
