# Tech Growth Without Democratic Infrastructure Is Extraction

## Summary

Tech companies arriving in cities with promises of jobs and shared prosperity default to extraction -- of land, water, subsidies, and democratic control -- when community engagement is treated as optional compliance rather than structural infrastructure. Frederick's active data center legislation is an opportunity to institutionalize the relational model before extraction becomes the default.

## The Pattern

Three pieces landed in the inbox today from Next City's "In the Shadow of the Server" series, and they all name the same underlying problem from different angles.

The reading list ("Cities in the Shadow of the Server") traces the historical pattern: for decades, technology companies have arrived in cities with grand promises and extracted far more than they delivered. Land, water, public subsidies, data, democratic control over local decisions. The tools and scale have changed; the pattern has not. Google's failed Toronto waterfront project is the template. The current wave of AI data center buildout is the same dynamic at unprecedented speed and scale.

The "Municipal Tech Rescue Cities" essay names what's at stake when cities fail to treat digital infrastructure as public: surveillance capitalism, gig work precarity, essential services placed behind monopolies and paywalls. Barcelona, Chattanooga, and Philadelphia show what it looks like when a city actually treats data and connectivity as public systems -- but those outcomes required deliberate political choices, not just vendor procurement.

The "Local Leaders Turn Tech Growth Into Shared Prosperity" piece gives the practical mechanism for why the extractive default happens: community engagement is treated as something that comes after the deal, not as infrastructure embedded in the deal itself. Approvals based on financials and projected economic impact, with community investment figured out later. The result is episodic engagement and promises that don't materialize.

## The Structural Fix

The "Community Circle" model in the third piece points to what structural engagement actually requires: named accountability (who is responsible for ongoing community relationships?), trusted intermediaries (organizations residents already know, not ones the company selects), co-design rather than consultation, and performance metrics tied to incentives rather than promises made at groundbreaking.

Barcelona's procurement rules show the more ambitious version: mandate open standards, interoperability, and local-first contracting. Tie data governance to public benefit. Make community engagement a legal requirement, not a corporate social responsibility line item.

## The Frederick Implication

Frederick has active data center legislation in process. The vault's existing notes -- [[Data Center Community Benefit Agreements -- Policy Models]], [[Data Centers -- Legislative Approach for Frederick]], [[Data Centers -- Transparency and Community Accountability as Counterweights]] -- show the groundwork is laid.

The synthesis from today's inbox: the legislation should require the relational model, not invite it. That means:

- Named community liaison with ongoing accountability, not a one-time community meeting.
- Community benefit metrics tied to tax abatements and land use approvals, revisited annually.
- Trusted community organizations built into the partnership structure from day one -- Centro Hispano, Golden Mile Alliance, AACF -- not consulted after the deal is signed.
- Data governance requirements: what data is the facility collecting, who has access, and under what conditions?

The reading list, the municipal tech essay, and the CBA piece together make the case that this is not about being anti-development. It is about whether Frederick captures the value of development for its residents or watches it flow to shareholders. The two outcomes are not inevitable -- they are the product of structural choices made at the permitting stage.
