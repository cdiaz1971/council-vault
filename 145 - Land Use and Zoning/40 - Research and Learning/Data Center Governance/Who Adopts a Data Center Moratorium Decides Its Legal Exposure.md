# Who Adopts a Data Center Moratorium Decides Its Legal Exposure

## Summary
Quantum Maryland's suit against Frederick County attacks the moratorium's vehicle (an executive order suspending council-adopted zoning) and its motive, not the policy of pausing. A council-adopted City moratorium avoids the vehicle problem, but Shackelford's draft takes on different risks through its two-year length, its extension clause, and its reach to the pending Trammell Crow text amendment.

## The Pattern
Two Frederick moratoria are moving at once, and they look alike from a distance. Up close they carry almost opposite legal profiles.

**The County moratorium** was imposed by County Executive Fitzwater through executive orders. It suspended application processing under a Critical Digital Infrastructure Overlay that the County Council had already adopted, and that the Maryland Supreme Court had just shielded from referendum. It was then extended from December 31, 2026 to July 1, 2027, right after Fitzwater rejected a $110 million community benefits agreement. Quantum's complaint attacks three things. The first is authority: the Charter gives zoning to the Council, so an executive cannot suspend adopted zoning. The second is motive, backed by an affidavit quoting the chief of staff saying Fitzwater needed to "get past the election." The third is reliance, a $500 million-plus land deal built on the overlay.

**The City moratorium** would be adopted by the City Council as an ordinance. It freezes something different. The City's LMC has no data center use at all, so there is no adopted entitlement to suspend. What gets frozen is the legislative process to *create* one, chiefly Trammell Crow's pending text amendment (PC25-626ZTA).

The lesson is that the vulnerability in the County case comes almost entirely from *who* acted and *what* they suspended. Neither carries over to the City. The City draft has its own exposure, and the County fight shows exactly where it is.

## Where the City Draft Is Stronger
**Vehicle.** The legislative body is acting on its own zoning authority. That is the posture Maryland courts upheld in *Friel v. Triangle Oil* and *Ungar v. State*, and the one *Tahoe-Sierra* treats as ordinary planning practice. Quantum's central theory, an executive overriding a legislature, has no City analogue.

**Reliance.** No one holds City zoning that permits a data center. Trammell Crow has spent close to two years on its text amendment, but asking a legislature to change the law creates no vested right to the change. A takings or vested-rights claim is much weaker than Quantum's, whose land deal rested on an adopted overlay.

**State posture.** [[Moore Data Center Order Defers to a Local No]] makes a local decision a gate on state incentives and permitting, and Moore has called moratoria a local matter he would support. A Council-adopted pause fits that framework cleanly.

## Where the City Draft Takes On Risk
**Length and the extension clause.** The May brief ([[Data Center Moratorium Policy Brief -- 2026-05-21]]) recommended twelve months or less, automatic termination when permanent rules are adopted, and a concrete staff work plan, because courts are most hostile to rolling moratoria that extend without visible progress. Shackelford's draft runs two years and lets the Council extend it. Adkins called two years excessive at the October 8 work session. *Tahoe-Sierra* upheld 32 months, so two years is not fatal by itself. But an open-ended extension power is the County's exposure in miniature. The County's extension came right after a rejected deal, which is what lets Quantum frame it as political. A City extension that lacks a documented reason invites the same story.

**Reach to the pending application.** The May brief advised exempting applications already in the pipeline. The draft does the opposite on purpose, because capturing the Trammell Crow amendment is the point. The defensible version of that choice rests on the distinction above: a text amendment application asks the Council to legislate, and it is not a development application. The open question is whether the Maryland Housing Certainty Act regime the City adopted in G-26-11 treats a text amendment filing as a "complete application" that vests anything. If it does not, retroactive reach is low-risk. If it arguably does, the draft needs to address that head-on.

**The record.** Quantum's strongest material is not legal doctrine. It is the affidavit and the donor conversation. A City moratorium's protection is a record that ties the pause and its length to specific findings. Those are the state impact study (now delayed to December), the Doubs transmission constraint, the ICPRB Potomac water figures, and the absence of any LMC definition. Public statements framing the pause as a way to stop a developer or win a political fight work against that record, even when they are sincere.

**The veto question.** This is still unresolved from May. If the mayor can veto the ordinance, the Charlotte outcome ([[Charlotte Data Center Moratorium -- Mayor Casts Deciding Vote Against]]) is the City's equivalent of the County's vehicle problem. The answer turns on the charter and the override threshold, not on the policy.

## The Benefits Agreement Lesson
The rejected County deal shows what a hyperscale developer asks for in return for community benefits: protection from future local law. Fitzwater's stated reason for saying no ("an industry changing this fast should not be shielded from future local laws") is the same logic behind the City's housing concern about Housing Certainty Act vesting. Any data center framework the City writes during a moratorium should settle in advance whether vesting protection is ever on the table. That decision is far easier to make before a specific offer arrives than after.

## Implications
A City moratorium is very likely the stronger of the two legally, *if* it is built to the May brief's specifications rather than as a mirror of the County's posture. The changes that would close most of the gap are modest. Tie the duration to defined deliverables, with automatic termination when permanent rules are adopted. Require a public hearing and written findings for any extension. Write findings that rest on the state study and local infrastructure facts. Address the text amendment and vesting question explicitly. The vote math also points toward a tighter draft. Adkins has questioned the length, Brehm has been silent, and Taylor is on leave, so a shorter, cleaner version may be what reaches a majority.
