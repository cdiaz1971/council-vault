# Data Centers -- State Legislative Models for Cooling Mandates

In 2025 alone, more than 200 data center-related bills were introduced across all 50 states, with more than 40 enacted. The models below span transparency requirements, technology mandates, and incentive-based approaches. All are relevant to what Frederick can pursue at the local ordinance level.

## Transparency and Reporting

Public disclosure of projected and actual water use is foundational. Non-disclosure agreements between data center operators and government officials are common and should be prohibited.

Georgia Senate Bill 421 would prohibit local governments from signing NDAs with data center operators that shield energy and water usage data from public disclosure. Virginia requires large water users to report withdrawals to DEQ above established thresholds; Loudoun County requires detailed water use disclosures during permitting. Arizona requires industrial facilities in regulated groundwater areas to file annual withdrawal reports. Washington state requires public disclosure of anticipated water consumption under the State Environmental Policy Act before project approval. Michigan, Iowa, New York, Oregon, Indiana, Illinois, and Utah have introduced analogous bills; Utah's targets facilities of 50,000 square feet or larger.

## Closed-Loop Technology Mandates

The strongest available models require new data centers to use closed-loop cooling systems, effectively prohibiting ongoing groundwater or municipal water use for cooling.

South Carolina House Bill 4583 would require all new data centers to use closed-loop cooling systems. Kansas Senate Bill 400 contains similar closed-loop mandates and authorizes municipalities, local district attorneys, and the state attorney general to sue non-compliant facilities -- a useful enforcement mechanism. Both bills align directly with commercially available immersion cooling and direct-to-chip technologies.

## Incentive-Based Approaches

Virginia Senate Bill 417 would condition access to the state's Cloud Computing Cluster Infrastructure Grant Fund on the use of treated wastewater in cooling systems. Virginia Senate Bill 30 would condition eligibility for the data center sales tax exemption on meeting environmental management and energy efficiency standards. Colorado Senate Bill 25-280 (Data Center Development and Grid Modernization Act) creates an incentive certification program tying sales tax exemptions and utility benefits to water stewardship strategies and a requirement that at least 50% of energy come from renewable sources.

## Application to Frederick

Frederick City cannot replicate state tax incentive structures, but local permitting authority is a direct analogue. Disclosure requirements, closed-loop mandates as permit conditions, and escrow or bonding requirements for treatment facility impacts are all within reach of a local ordinance. The South Carolina and Kansas models are the strongest templates for permit-level technology mandates. See [[Data Centers -- Advanced Cooling Technology Options]] for the technical basis for these requirements and [[Data Centers -- Legislative Approach for Frederick]] for the ordinance framework in development.
