# Data Center Health Impacts: Research Gap and Site Selection Recommendations

## Summary

A February 2026 peer-reviewed analysis of Virginia's Data Center Alley finds that direct health research on data centers is sparse and still emerging, but identifies noise pollution, fossil-fuel-driven air quality degradation, and water consumption as the most significant risk pathways. The core public health argument: siting decisions made now determine whether these impacts can be prevented or only mitigated later.

## Key Points

**The research gap itself is an argument.** The study was authored by researchers at George Mason University and published in Frontiers in Climate. Its central finding is that "rapid data center expansion is happening now while the research is still emerging." For local governments approving projects today, this is a material fact: the full health consequences are unknown, and the facilities being approved now will be operating for decades.

**Noise pollution is the most immediate and direct impact.** Data centers operate 24/7 at decibel levels near or exceeding federal recommendations. Short-term effects: sleep disruption and chronic annoyance. Long-term risks: heart disease, permanent hearing loss, chronic stress. The most effective intervention is siting -- building further from residential areas -- rather than engineering fixes after the fact.

**The broader health risk is the fossil fuel system, not the facility in isolation.** The larger health consequences stem from the electricity demand data centers place on grids still dependent on fossil fuels, adding to air pollution and greenhouse gas emissions. A data center in a state with high renewable penetration has a different health profile than one in a state dependent on coal or natural gas. Virginia's grid is a mixed case; Maryland's is similar.

**Diesel backup generators are a direct local air quality concern.** Many data centers use diesel generators as backup power. During the July 2026 heatwave, PJM pushed large customers -- including data centers -- to use these backup generators to prevent blackouts. The industry trade association claims generator use is "minimal" and represents 7% of permitted emissions; but that use is concentrated, local, and spikes during the conditions (heat waves) when air quality is already stressed.

**Water consumption grows with scale.** Some data centers use water volumes comparable to the country of Denmark annually. The industry argues per-facility use is comparable to other large commercial users, but growth trajectories mean the cumulative demand will increase substantially.

**Site selection is the primary preventive tool.** Once built, the options are mitigation (buffer zones, quieter engineering, water recycling). Before construction, the option is location. The researchers' recommendation: require health impact assessment from the start of siting, not as a post-approval condition.

## Relevance

This is a new harm vector for the ZTA opposition record that was not previously in the file. The existing opposition case is built around four vectors: ratepayer cost, clean energy commitment reversal, housing opportunity cost, and civic participation suppression. Public health is a fifth vector with peer-reviewed support.

For the consolidated opposition brief (the outstanding next action in Active Threads): the siting argument maps directly onto the M1/M2 industrial zone question in PC25-626ZTA. The ZTA would authorize data centers in industrial zones that are adjacent to residential corridors -- exactly the configuration the research identifies as highest-risk for noise and air quality impacts.

The research gap argument is also usable in testimony: the Planning Commission is being asked to approve a permanent zoning change while the health evidence base is still being built. That is a one-way door. Reversing it later will be far harder than requiring health impact assessment now.
