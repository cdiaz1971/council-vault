# Data Centers -- Potomac River Basin Water Impact Projections

The Potomac River supplies 75% of regional drinking water for approximately 5 million people through Fairfax Water, WSSC Water, and the Washington Aqueduct, and is the sole drinking water source for Washington D.C. and Arlington County. The basin is also home to the world's largest concentration of data centers, with more than 290 individual buildings currently operating, representing approximately 5,400 MW of power demand. Frederick County is explicitly named in the March 2026 ICPRB analysis as a site of rapid expansion.

## Current Consumption Figures

ICPRB reports that data centers in the Washington Metropolitan Area (WMA) currently consume approximately 4 million gallons per day (MGD) on average, surging to 15 MGD at summer peak -- nearly three times the annual average -- precisely when river flows are lowest and other demands are highest. Despite representing only 1% of total withdrawals in the WMA, data centers account for 9% of annual consumptive use and up to 12% in summer months. Data centers in the basin are served by public water utilities and currently hold no direct withdrawal permits, placing them outside existing consumptive use regulations.

## 2050 Projections

With more than 100 million square feet of additional data center development planned in the region, ICPRB projects WMA data center water demand could reach 22 MGD average and more than 80 MGD at peak by 2050. Upstream consumptive use is projected to reach 5 MGD annually and up to 17 MGD at peak. ICPRB concludes this trajectory could create significant regional water supply reliability challenges, particularly during drought and low-flow periods.

## Indirect Water Footprint

Even if a data center minimizes on-site water use, the upstream water intensity of its electricity supply contributes meaningfully to regional water stress. Thermoelectric power plants (coal, natural gas, nuclear) withdraw and consume large volumes of water for cooling, so a data center's grid-draw creates an indirect water impact that on-site efficiency measures do not eliminate.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick County is named in the ICPRB analysis. Any data center permitted in the City of Frederick draws from the same basin supply that serves millions of downstream users. The regulatory gap -- no direct withdrawal permits required, no state consumptive use rules applying -- is the political and legal opening to impose city-level permit conditions the state framework currently does not. The ICPRB figures give concrete, citable numbers for the council record: current 9% of consumptive use despite 1% of withdrawals, summer peak tripling, and 2050 projections that could stress regional water reliability.
