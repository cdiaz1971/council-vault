# Local Progress

## Summary

Local Progress is a national network of progressive local elected officials advancing a racial and economic justice agenda through all levels of local government. Founded in 2012, it operates as two affiliated entities: a 501(c)(4) advocacy arm (Local Progress Policy Action) and a 501(c)(3) educational and training arm (Local Progress Impact Lab). The network has grown to nearly 1,800 members across 47 states and Washington, D.C., including mayors, city councilmembers, county commissioners, district attorneys, and school board members. Baltimore City Councilmember Kristerfer Burnett serves on the LP board of directors as Co-Chair, representing Maryland's presence in the network's national leadership.

## Organizational Structure

Local Progress operates as two legally distinct but closely coordinated organizations sharing staff and leadership.

**Local Progress Policy Action** (501(c)(4))
EIN: 87-2098657. Tax-exempt since April 2022. The advocacy and lobbying arm. Dues and donations are not tax-deductible. This entity holds the member network, does organizing, and carries out direct policy advocacy.

**Local Progress Policy Institute d/b/a Local Progress Impact Lab** (501(c)(3))
EIN: 86-3590543. Tax-exempt since November 2021. The charitable and educational arm. Donations are tax-deductible. Runs training, research, policy development, and the Progressive Governance Academy.

Both entities share the same executive director, senior staff, and board of directors. The two-entity structure is common among advocacy organizations operating in both the lobbying and charitable spaces.

**Origin:** Local Progress was founded in 2012 as a project of the Center for Popular Democracy (CPD). In 2022, it was spun off from CPD as its own independent dual-entity structure.

## Programs and Member Resources

**Progressive Governance Academy (PGA)** — Flagship leadership development program co-hosted with re:power and the State Innovation Exchange. Designed for progressive state and local elected officials who have been elected but lack governing-specific skills: policy analysis, constituent outreach, coalition management, parliamentary procedure. Trainers are exclusively current and former state and local elected officials.

**Policy Help Desk** — On-demand policy, strategy, and communications support for LP members. Members submit questions to helpdesk@localprogress.org and receive staff expertise, peer connections, and partner referrals.

**Policy Library and Toolkits** — Searchable resource library of policy memos, model ordinances, and issue briefs covering housing, immigrant justice, public safety, worker protections, education, and more. Sortable by state, issue, and resource type.

**Policy & Strategy Primers** — Learning series for new members and newly elected officials covering foundational governance and advocacy skills.

**Steering Committees** — Issue-area leadership bodies shaped by member elected officials. Areas include housing, immigrant justice, public safety, education and school boards, and worker protections. Membership involves monthly or quarterly virtual meetings, media representation, and strategic input.

**Identity Caucuses** — Member community spaces organized by shared identity. Current caucuses: Black Caucus, Women's Caucus, Nuestro Caucus (Latino members, with a focus on immigration enforcement impacts and the Puerto Rico diaspora), and Pride Caucus. The Women's Caucus lost a founding member in April 2026 with the death of Coral Springs Vice Mayor Nancy Metayer, the first Haitian American and Black woman elected to that role and the creator of Coral Springs' first Office of Sustainability.

**Communications Cohort** — A nine-session virtual training program for local elected officials on communications theory, skills, and press relationships, including an in-person DC trip. The second cohort (January-April 2026) drew 18 members from 16 states. Its core lesson for under-resourced officials: pick one platform and do it with discipline rather than spreading thin across all of them.

**State Chapters** — Fully certified state-level chapters with formal leadership structures and hired staff. Current chapters: New York (first chapter, since 2018), Texas, North Carolina, and Minnesota (since 2022). Florida organizing is in development. Maryland does not currently have a certified LP chapter.

**Municipal Membership** — Cities and counties with at least one LP member can join as institutional members on a population-based fee structure. Provides tailored training, policy briefings, and staff support to the full governing body.

**Annual National Convening** — Yearly in-person gathering for members, partners, and movement allies covering strategy, peer learning, workshops, and site visits.

## Issue Areas

Local Progress works across a broad progressive agenda anchored in racial and economic justice:

- **Housing and Tenant Protections** — Renter protections, affordable housing production, anti-displacement, short-term rental regulation, algorithmic rent-fixing bans.
- **Immigrant Justice** — Sanctuary policies, ICE non-cooperation, municipal ID programs, know-your-rights infrastructure, immigrant community investment.
- **Worker Protections** — Minimum wage, workplace safety, paid leave, gig worker rights.
- **Public Safety** — Community-centered safety investment, reducing reliance on policing and criminalization.
- **Home Rule and Anti-Preemption** — Defending local authority against state preemption laws that block progressive municipal policy.
- **Democracy and Governance** — Voting access, transparency, collaborative governance models.
- **Education** — Public school funding, school board organizing, student supports.
- **Economic Justice** — Direct cash assistance, participatory budgeting, anti-poverty programs.

## Maryland Connection

Maryland does not have a certified Local Progress state chapter. However, the state has meaningful representation in the network's leadership and membership.

**Kristerfer Burnett**, Baltimore City Councilmember representing District 8, serves as Co-Chair of the LP 501(c)(4) board. His work with LP includes immigrant justice, housing, and reparations policy. In 2023, he introduced reparations legislation in Baltimore through the LP network, making Baltimore the first Maryland community to formally initiate that process.

Maryland-based elected officials can join LP as individual members or initiate municipal membership for their city or county government. As of early 2026, LP is not known to be actively organizing a Maryland state chapter, though the presence of a board co-chair from Baltimore suggests some organizing infrastructure exists in the state.

**Key observations:**

The organization has scaled dramatically since its 2021 launch as an independent entity — from $2.8M in inaugural revenue to nearly $9.6M in the 501(c)(3) alone in 2024. Growth has been consistent year over year with no deficit years, reflecting strong funder relationships. Net assets in the 501(c)(3) now exceed $10.5M, suggesting a well-capitalized organization with significant operational runway.

The near-total reliance on contributions (97%+) across both entities is worth noting. There is no earned revenue from member dues at a scale that shows up as material in the filings. This means LP's programmatic capacity is entirely dependent on continued philanthropic and institutional support. The 501(c)(4) carries higher liabilities relative to assets ($425k liabilities against $1.76M assets) compared to the 501(c)(3), which is typical for a 501(c)(4) operating with more advocacy-related expenses and less grant restriction flexibility.

## Why This Matters for César

Local Progress is the most directly relevant national network for César's work as Frederick City Council President. It is built for exactly his profile: a progressive municipal elected official working on housing affordability, immigrant protections, and community investment in a mid-size city that is not a major metro.

**As a resource network:** LP's policy library, help desk, and steering committees give César access to model ordinances, peer-tested policy frameworks, and staff researchers on his core issue areas. For active work like the [[Municipal ID]] program, [[City Values Act]], and housing production targets, LP's immigration justice and housing toolkits are directly applicable. Their anti-preemption work is also relevant given Maryland's evolving posture on local housing authority.

**As a peer community:** LP's membership explicitly prioritizes supporting officials who are "firsts" in their governing bodies and those working in communities that are not major metros. The network's racial and economic justice framing aligns with Frederick's growing immigrant population and César's policy priorities.

**Maryland dimension:** The LP board Co-Chair is a Baltimore City Councilmember. While Maryland has no state chapter yet, that Baltimore connection creates a natural entry point for state-level LP organizing that César could potentially help seed or participate in. If LP ever builds a Maryland chapter, Frederick's participation would add meaningful geographic and demographic diversity.

**Progressive Governance Academy:** The PGA is directly applicable for César's own leadership development and potentially for peer councilmembers interested in governance training. It addresses exactly the gap between campaign skills and governing skills.

**Communications Cohort:** LP's focus-over-completeness lesson (pick the channel that connects best with your community and do it well) matches César's existing single-channel Bluesky discipline, and the curriculum on reaching non-English-speaking audiences maps onto Frederick's multilingual constituency.

**Policy corner relevance:** LP's steering committees have flagged two items with direct Frederick application: San Marcos stopping a proposed data center over utility cost, water strain, and public health risk (LP ties data center siting to environmental and racial justice, citing AI Now as a resource), and St. Paul's extension of its pre-eviction notice period, framed by LP as a racial and gender justice issue. Both are relevant to active Frederick work on the data center text amendment and tenant protections.

Individual LP membership is free for local elected officials. Municipal membership for the City of Frederick would carry a population-based fee and would extend LP resources to all councilmembers as an institutional benefit.
