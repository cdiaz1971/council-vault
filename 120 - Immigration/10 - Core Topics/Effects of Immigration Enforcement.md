# Effects of Immigration Enforcement

## Purpose

Use this page when considering service funding, outreach, interagency coordination, or oversight after immigration enforcement activity. The decision is how to preserve residents' access to essential services and household stability, and how to tell whether the response is working.

Separate **direct disruption after detention or removal**, **avoidance driven by fear**, and **changes in legal eligibility**. They call for different responses. A decline in service use can mean residents are afraid to seek help; rising demand at a trusted organization can occur at the same time. Frederick's March 2026 reporting describes both patterns. [WYPR, March 19, 2026](https://www.wypr.org/wypr-news/2026-03-19/immigration-enforcement-driving-fear-in-frederick-county).

For incident chronology, use [[ICE Enforcement in Maryland and Frederick]]. For the delivery and funding of assistance, use [[Immigrant Legal Services and Local Support]].

## Definitions and measurement

| Term | Working meaning | Decision consequence |
|---|---|---|
| Direct household disruption | Loss of income, caregiving, mobility, or family contact associated with detention or removal | Assess immediate housing, food, childcare, and legal needs separately |
| Chilling effect | Avoidance of an activity or service because of perceived immigration consequences | Service eligibility alone does not demonstrate practical access |
| Mixed-status household | Household members have different citizenship or immigration statuses | One member's status does not determine every member's eligibility |
| Enrollment, attendance, and graduation | Registration stock, participation over time, and completion of a defined student cohort | These measures cannot be substituted for one another |
| Public charge | Federal immigration admissibility assessment, distinct from a benefit program's eligibility rules | Benefits navigation and individual immigration advice are different services |
| Provider report, estimate, and measured outcome | An account of experience, an assumption-based calculation, and an observed result | Label each before using it to justify an appropriation |

Hispanic ethnicity, foreign birth, English-learner classification, and noncitizenship are different characteristics. None is a valid substitute for identifying undocumented residents. Do not construct a local enforcement-affected population estimate by treating them as interchangeable.

## Frederick baseline: what the record establishes

| Evidence | What it supports | What it does not establish |
|---|---|---|
| March 2026 joint City–County meeting coverage: Claudia Hernandez reported a decline of more than 50% in people accessing services beginning June 2025; Centro Hispano reported sharply increased demand | Dated local accounts of withdrawal and demand shifting toward particular providers | A measured 50% decline across all Frederick services, a City-only rate, or a causal estimate. The reporting supplies no common denominator or provider-level series |
| September family accounts in Bolts: lost income, disrupted schooling, curtailed movement, and reluctance to contact police after enforcement | Concrete pathways through which enforcement can destabilize a household | Population prevalence, total local wage loss, or the effectiveness of any intervention |
| Taskers Chance coverage and statements compiled in the September 9 press brief | Residents and officials reported fear of leaving home during the operation | A representative survey or a count of every affected household |

Sources: [WYPR](https://www.wypr.org/wypr-news/2026-03-19/immigration-enforcement-driving-fear-in-frederick-county), [[Immigration Enforcement -- Frederick County WYPR]]; [[Frederick County Immigration Enforcement -- Jenkins, Community Harm, and the Limits of Reform]] (a dated review of Bolts reporting); [[ICE Raid Press Coverage Brief -- 2026-09-09]] (press and statement compilation). The last two remain qualitative local evidence, not administrative datasets.

**The actionable data gap:** the material reviewed does not establish a City-wide rate of enforcement-related service avoidance or a count of households needing assistance. A useful baseline would combine monthly provider intake, completed services, turnaways, waiting times, and voluntary reasons for missed appointments, using consistent definitions. Request aggregate series from participating providers; do not create a registry of residents' immigration status.

Frederick County Public Schools states that children have a right to public education regardless of citizenship or residency status and identifies pupil personnel services as a route for enrollment and attendance assistance. That establishes an access principle and an existing partner, not proof that families feel able to attend. [FCPS, Pupil Personnel Services](https://www.fcps.org/families_students/student_services/pupil_personnel_services).

## State and national evidence

### Schools and childcare

Maryland Matters' August 24, 2026 analysis reports declining Hispanic enrollment in 2025–26 and interviews linking some families' withdrawal to enforcement fears. It also describes Arco Iris, a Laurel childcare provider, operating at roughly half capacity amid both enforcement fears and scholarship constraints. These are **state data interpreted by journalism and a provider account**, respectively; neither measures Frederick's effect. [Maryland Matters](https://marylandmatters.org/2026/08/24/marylands-hispanic-student-enrollment-declines-amid-immigration-fears/); [[Immigration Enforcement Fear Driving Hispanic School Enrollment Decline in Maryland]]; [[Immigration Enforcement Fear Compounding Maryland Childcare Access Crisis]].

For a local decision, obtain FCPS enrollment, attendance, and withdrawal series, and use the [MSDE student publications](https://marylandpublicschools.org/about/Pages/DCAA/SSP/index.aspx) for consistent definitions and comparison years. Population movement, incoming enrollment, household costs, and school changes can also affect participation. Neither correlation with an enforcement period nor ethnicity alone identifies the cause.

The earlier page's graduation-rate changes are not carried forward as evidence of an enforcement effect. They require cohort verification and clear distinction between percent change and percentage-point change. Childcare enrollment also does not measure licensed capacity or unmet demand. See [[Childcare and the Care Economy]].

### Health care and benefits avoidance

The September 2026 AAP study, *Pediatricians' Perceptions Regarding Immigrant Families Accessing Services*, is identified by [DOI 10.1542/peds.2026-078069](https://doi.org/10.1542/peds.2026-078069). Contemporary reporting describes approximately **40% of pediatricians reporting families avoiding medical care**, **31% reporting avoidance of government-program applications**, and **21% reporting avoidance of school**. These are shares of responding clinicians reporting an experience, **not percentages of children avoiding those services**. [HealthDay report via Medical Xpress](https://medicalxpress.com/news/2026-09-pediatricians-immigrant-families-children.html); [[Immigration Enforcement Drives Immigrant Families Away From Pediatric Care]].

The primary article's full methods could not be retrieved during this review. The percentages therefore remain secondary-reported national findings; the earlier page's sample-size and field-date claims are not treated here as independently verified. Provider observation also misses families who never reach a practice. Do not apply the national percentages to Frederick's population or infer a neighborhood rate from the urban–rural comparison.

A separate federal data-sharing policy adds a benefits-specific driver of avoidance distinct from public charge. A new Administration for Children and Families policy would let DHS cross-reference TANF recipient data, including immigration status, with USCIS; Maryland and 24 other jurisdictions sued to block it before its August 11, 2026 effective date. [[Maryland AG Sues Trump Administration on TANF Data Sharing and Tariffs]]; [Maryland Matters, Aug. 4, 2026](https://marylandmatters.org/2026/08/04/maryland-joins-two-more-lawsuits-against-the-trump-administration/). No ruling is documented in the reviewed material. For mixed-status households, a policy of this kind can extend avoidance beyond the person whose status is at issue to other household members who fear a relative's TANF enrollment could be used against them, the same mechanism already documented for Medicaid, CHIP, and schools above. This is reported litigation, not a measured Frederick TANF enrollment effect; it belongs with the City's data-exposure review in [[ICE Surveillance and Data]] as well as here.

### Direct eligibility loss: H.R. 1 Medicaid provisions

Separate from chilling effects and public charge, H.R. 1 directly removed Medicaid eligibility, effective October 1, 2026, for refugees, asylees, humanitarian parolees, and trafficking victims nationally, while green card holders, Cuban and Haitian entrants, and Compact of Free Association migrants keep eligibility. The State estimates up to 4,500 affected Marylanders, including people in active treatment for chronic conditions; the same populations lose subsidized ACA marketplace coverage in January 2027. A parallel provision shifted SNAP administrative costs from an even federal-state split to 75 percent State and 25 percent federal, adding roughly $57 million to Maryland's budget with up to $240 million more possible in 2027. [Maryland Matters, Oct. 1, 2026](https://marylandmatters.org/2026/10/01/thousands-of-immigrants-in-maryland-to-lose-medicaid-coverage-under-h-r-1/); [[H.R. 1 Ends Medicaid for Refugees and Asylees in Maryland]].

This is an enacted statutory change, not a modeled scenario, and it reaches people lawfully admitted on humanitarian grounds. It compounds the avoidance patterns above: a family that loses coverage and also fears enforcement contact is less likely to seek care at all. Frederick's refugee and asylee households are affected, and the loss of state budget capacity from the SNAP cost shift is a reason to expect less, not more, state support for local safety-net programs over the next two years. Confirm with the City's Housing and Human Services referral network whether local sliding-fee providers can be identified for residents losing Medicaid mid-treatment. See [[Federal Immigration Policy]] for the statutory mechanism and [[Food Access and SNAP]] for the cost-shift detail.

### Housing, work, and civic participation

The Minnesota and Memphis accounts in [[ICE Enforcement Chilling Effect -- Economic and Housing Disruption]] identify a plausible chain: disrupted work and movement, reduced earnings, rent arrears, and increased requests for help. Their wage-loss, eviction, and rental-assistance numbers are outside-jurisdiction reporting and estimates; this review does not independently validate them or use them to size a Frederick appropriation.

Labor effects are now being estimated directly. One study estimated Minnesota's Operation Metro Surge cost about 4,600 hospitality jobs and $71 million in wages over three months ([Minnesota Reformer](https://minnesotareformer.com/2026/06/02/study-operation-metro-surge-cost-minnesota-an-estimated-4600-hospitality-jobs-in-3-months/)). A larger figure of 32,000 fewer jobs, attributed to the Immigration Research Initiative in a news roundup, could not be traced to the underlying study (verify). Reporting also links enforcement and an aging workforce to skilled-trades shortages ([[Immigration Crackdown Driving Skilled Trades Labor Shortage]]). For Frederick the relevant point is that housing production and capital projects depend on trades labor, so enforcement-driven shortages can raise local construction costs and delay projects. That is a mechanism to monitor, not a measured local effect.

[[ICE at Military Graduations -- Chilling Effect on Immigrant Families]] preserves a reported example of avoiding a civic milestone. It illustrates a mechanism, not a measured national attendance effect.

**Council implication:** follow the whole household. Counting arrests alone cannot capture school continuity, housing retention, access to care, or the burden transferred to relatives and local organizations. This is an analytical conclusion from the evidence above, not a new estimate of local harm.

## Public charge: current rule and service-access implications

**Rule reviewed September 29, 2026.** DHS's July 20 final rule rescinds the 2022 public-charge regulations. It took effect September 18 and applies to admission applications made on or after that date and adjustment-of-status applications postmarked or electronically submitted on or after it. Benefits received before September 18 are considered consistently with the 2022 rule. [Federal Register, 91 FR 45324, final rule 2026-14539](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-07-20/pdf/2026-14539.pdf).

The revised framework permits broader consideration of means-tested assistance. It is not an automatic denial for anyone receiving assistance and does not itself change benefit-program eligibility. Public charge does not apply to every immigration pathway; humanitarian exemptions and individual circumstances matter. [Washington DSHS, updated September 18, 2026](https://www.dshs.wa.gov/family-food-and-housing-services/office-refugee-and-immigrant-assistance/public-charge-information). That source explains federal policy; Washington-specific benefit rules are not Maryland guidance.

**Do not give blanket assurances about family benefits.** The fact that a child is a citizen and eligible for a program does not, by itself, answer how a parent's immigration application will be assessed under the new guidance. Arrange individual advice before residents make decisions to enroll or withdraw. [Massachusetts government public-charge guidance, September 2026](https://www.mass.gov/doc/the-federal-public-charge-rule-information-for-immigrants/download). This distinction corrects the earlier page's incomplete treatment of eligibility and immigration consequences.

KFF's July 20, 2026 analysis uses the **2024 ACS** and assumed disenrollment rates of **10–30%** among Medicaid/CHIP enrollees in households with a noncitizen. It estimates **1.4–4.1 million** potential disenrollments, including approximately **560,000–1.7 million citizen children**. These are national scenarios, not observed losses, a forecast interval, or a Frederick caseload estimate. [KFF analysis and methods](https://www.kff.org/medicaid/potential-chilling-effects-of-public-charge-and-other-immigration-policies-on-medicaid-and-chip-enrollment/).

The rule is under challenge: a multistate suit was filed September 14. A lawsuit is not an injunction; recheck controlling orders and current guidance before issuing public materials. [New York Attorney General's filing announcement](https://ag.ny.gov/press-release/2026/attorney-general-james-leads-major-challenge-trump-administrations-attack). The review located current government implementation guidance but did not complete an independent docket audit.

## Authority and responsibility

| Level or institution | Relevant responsibility | Practical City role |
|---|---|---|
| City | Its employees, facilities, services, contracts, and appropriations | Implement the [[City Values Act]], improve access to City services, and consider properly authorized provider funding |
| County and community providers | County service navigation and advisory work; provider intake and assistance | Agree on referral ownership and aggregate reporting; City funding does not establish County operational control |
| FCPS / Board of Education | School enrollment, attendance response, and school procedures | Partner and request aggregate information; City Council does not direct school operations |
| Maryland and administering agencies | State benefit and childcare programs and relevant state law | Seek program-specific guidance and coordinate assistance |
| Federal government and courts | Immigration status, adjudication, enforcement, and public-charge rules | The City cannot grant lawful status or guarantee the absence of federal enforcement |

Authority anchors: [[City Values Act -- Enacted Ordinance G-26-1]]; [Maryland municipal general authority, Local Government §5-202](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/2026RS/Statute_Web/glg/5-202.pdf); [County Immigrant Affairs Commission](https://www.frederickcountymd.gov/8282/Immigrant-Affairs-Commission); [FCPS pupil personnel services](https://www.fcps.org/families_students/student_services/pupil_personnel_services); the federal rule above. Specific spending and program designs require City legal and fiscal review; see [[Local Authority and Federal Preemption]].

A local cooperation restriction should be described by its actual scope. It does not guarantee that an encounter cannot occur near a school, clinic, or City facility. Avoid public messaging that promises safety beyond the government's control.

## Response options

These are decision options, not adopted programs or new legislative commitments.

| Option and best use | Delivery and operating needs | Risk / evidence limit | Result to assess |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trusted navigation when eligible residents are avoiding services | Fund trained navigators, interpretation, current information, and follow-through with providers | Outreach may increase demand beyond provider capacity; local effectiveness must be evaluated | Share of consented referrals resulting in completed assistance; waiting time |
| Household stabilization after income or caregiving disruption | Partner on housing, food, childcare, and counseling; budget assistance plus case management | Eligibility and public-charge implications depend on the program; one-time relief may not resolve sustained income loss | Housing and care continuity at agreed follow-up intervals; unmet need |
| Legal screening and benefits counseling when families face immigration questions | Qualified providers; dedicated consultation capacity; separation from general navigation | Consultation is not ongoing representation, and no legal outcome can be promised | Time to qualified advice; accepted referrals and continuity of representation |
| School and healthcare access coordination | FCPS and provider-led appointment or attendance follow-up, language access, family support | Do not place identifiable education or health records in a City dashboard | Re-engagement and service completion using partner-held data |
| Clear City service protocols and communication | Staff training, translated explanations, designated update owners, complaint handling | Rules may be implemented correctly while fear persists | Protocol completion plus resident-reported barriers and complaint resolution |

Evidence supports taking the barriers seriously; it does not establish a Frederick-specific effect size or cost-effectiveness ranking for these interventions. Request provider costs and capacity before selecting a scale.

## Decision framework and minimum dashboard

Before appropriating funds, identify the target barrier, responsible operator, eligible population, intake route, and service capacity. Include recurring interpretation, supervision, administration, and follow-up costs. Build a referral pathway before promoting it.

| Indicator | Definition and owner | Interpretation safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Completed access | Completed services divided by consented referrals with sufficient follow-up; provider reports monthly | Report pending, declined, unreachable, and ineligible separately |
| Timeliness and unmet demand | Median and upper-range waiting time; requests turned away by reason | Rising intake may reflect trust or worsening need; a shorter queue may reflect withdrawal |
| Household continuity | Assisted households retaining housing or reconnecting to care/school at agreed intervals | Report follow-up coverage and missing observations; avoid attributing all change to the intervention |
| Distribution | City residency and language/access barriers, collected only as necessary | County totals are not City totals; suppress small groups and avoid status-based identification |
| Cost and capacity | Spending, funded staff capacity, completed service type, continuing obligations | A consultation and a multiyear legal case are not comparable units |
| Trust and access experience | Optional anonymous feedback on barriers, clarity, and successful access | Participation is voluntary and not representative without an appropriate sampling design |

These are proposed reporting specifications. Establish the baseline and reporting agreement before setting numerical targets. Collect only necessary data, keep case records with responsible providers, and obtain legal review of retention and disclosure rules. Do not promise absolute confidentiality.

- [ ] Evidence identifies date, geography, population, denominator, and source type.

## Evidence notes and retained research trail

Law and official program documents control legal and eligibility questions. Administrative records describe their covered populations. Research methods determine how far findings can be generalized. Meeting testimony and journalism establish attributed experiences; they do not establish population causality.
