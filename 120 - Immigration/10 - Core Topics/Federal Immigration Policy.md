# Federal Immigration Policy

Federal rules determine admission, citizenship, immigration status and eligibility for particular federal programs. Frederick's practical decisions concern service continuity, legal referrals, communication, workforce and business effects, and the lawful use of municipal resources. A City resolution cannot confer immigration status or replace a federal adjudication.

**Review date: October 3, 2026.** This is a policy reference, not an individual eligibility determination. Country-specific TPS notices, court orders and program rules require a fresh check when used.

## Decision frame and authority

| Decision | Responsible authority | Frederick's role |
|---|---|---|
| Citizenship, admission, removal and immigration benefits | Federal law, agencies and courts | Accurate referrals and advocacy; no local status determination |
| Federal work authorization and lending eligibility | Relevant federal agency, subject to law and court orders | Identify service and business impacts; avoid promising eligibility |
| City-funded services, communications and contracts | City within applicable state and federal constraints | Budget, procurement, language access and service design |
| State/local cooperation with enforcement | Applicable federal and Maryland law and local policy | Read alongside [[Maryland Community Trust Act]] and [[ICE Enforcement in Maryland and Frederick]] |

Keep citizenship, lawful permanent residence, temporary protection, parole, work authorization and statistical population classifications separate. An employment authorization document is not permanent residence. Loss of one protection does not establish that every affected person lacks another basis to remain.

## National baseline: useful context, not a Frederick count

MPI estimates **15.8 million unauthorized immigrants in mid-2024**. That is a dated national estimate, not a September 2026 count or a local population estimate. The underlying category can include people with temporary protection or permission to remain; it should not be translated into a claim that everyone counted entered unlawfully or lacks work authorization. Two retained notes summarize the same article, so they are not independent corroboration. The note titled “From 2026 Peak” should be read with the corrected **2024** reference year. [MPI estimate](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/press-release/us-unauthorized-immigrant-population-reaches-new-levels-and-diversifies-mpi-estimates).

The ACLU's review of mass deportation enforcement describes more than 2,000 federal, state, and local agencies participating, over 25,000 federal agents plus 287(g)-deputized officers, about $240 billion in congressional enforcement funding, roughly 6.1 million people losing legal status, and a reported 75% drop in the naturalization rate over two years. These are advocacy-organization figures with recommendations attached (a path to citizenship, replacing ICE, ending functional immunity for agents); use them as attributed claims and check each figure against the underlying federal data before citing it as fact (verify) ([ACLU](https://www.aclu.org/news/immigrants-rights/we-launched-the-first-comprehensive-civil-rights-review-of-mass-deportations-heres-how-its-impacting-democracy); [[ACLU Report Finds Mass Deportation Enforcement Is Eroding Democratic Safeguards]]).

Support for work authorization is not confined to the left. More than 250 Latino leaders, including a former chair of the Republican National Hispanic Assembly, signed a Comite de 100 letter asking the President and Congress for work permits and legal protections for long-term undocumented workers, citing polling that 75% of Latino voters would favor a candidate who backs work permits ([[Comite de 100 Letter Shows Bipartisan Latino Demand for Work Permits]]). The polling is the coalition's own citation, not an independent survey reviewed here.

A more current and more credible count than the ACLU figures above comes from the Migration Policy Institute, a nonpartisan research organization: roughly 980,000 ICE and CBP deportations from January 20, 2025 through July 31, 2026, about 580,000 formal removals and about 410,000 voluntary departures or withdrawn admission applications, built on a new methodology because official federal reporting has slowed. Most of those deported had no criminal conviction, and many had two or more years of US residence; among people first arrested at the border, 62% had been in the country more than a year. This count is well below DHS's own public claim of more than 3 million combined deportations and self-departures, a gap MPI attributes to DHS counting unverified self-departures. *verify:* MPI's page could not be fetched directly during this review; the figures come from MPI's published excerpts and secondary reporting, and MPI's total self-departure estimate was not independently retrieved. [MPI fact sheet, October 2026](https://www.migrationpolicy.org/publication/us-deportations-second-trump); [[MPI Estimates 980,000 Deportations in First 18 Months of Second Trump Term]].

For Frederick, request aggregated service-demand indicators from trusted providers rather than attempting to enumerate residents by immigration status. National changes can affect mixed-status households, employers and schools, but do not establish the size or direction of a particular local effect.

## Temporary Protected Status and parole

**TPS** is a country-specific temporary protection; **parole** is a separate discretionary mechanism. Their duration, employment consequences and litigation differ. Do not describe all Afghan, Ukrainian or CHNV parole grants as a single program with one termination date, or describe a later termination as making an earlier authorized entry retroactively unlawful.

**El Salvador needs a current-status check.** The retained September reporting described September 9, 2026 as an approaching deadline. A September 3 E-Verify bulletin also described termination on that date, but this review could not retrieve the current USCIS country page to reconcile subsequent reports of continuing protection. Accordingly, this page does **not** certify that TPS or work authorization ended on September 9. Before publishing resident guidance, obtain the current country notice, applicable Federal Register action, litigation posture and document-specific extension rules. [Dated E-Verify bulletin](https://content.govdelivery.com/accounts/USDHSCISEVERIFY/bulletins/42855a4); [USCIS El Salvador TPS page](https://www.uscis.gov/humanitarian/temporary-protected-status/temporary-protected-status-designated-country-el-salvador).

The reported national and Washington-region Salvadoran population figures are not a Frederick caseload. Proposed congressional extensions are not enacted protections. Individual harm described by families is important qualitative evidence, but does not by itself prove a medical or statistical causal relationship.

**Local response:** fund qualified legal screening and multilingual referral capacity; distinguish an announcement from its effective date and from its application to a particular person. See [[Immigrant Legal Services and Local Support]].

## Refugee admissions: ceiling, actual arrivals and local capacity

The FY2026 ceiling was initially 7,500 and was raised to **17,500** by the May 2026 presidential determination. A ceiling authorizes a maximum; it is not an admission count or a funding commitment to Frederick. [May determination](https://www.govinfo.gov/content/pkg/FR-2026-05-27/pdf/2026-10598.pdf).

The retained August article reports 10,258 admissions during the first ten months of FY2026. Comparing that partial year directly with complete FY2024 or FY2025 totals exaggerates the comparability of the figures. Its account of Afrikaner prioritization describes the administration's selection policy; contested assertions about persecution should remain attributed.

For budget purposes, ask resettlement partners about actual local placements, family reunification, grant changes, housing demand and staffing. Do not derive local arrivals from the national ceiling. Any FY2027 announcement requires its own final determination and effective-period check.

## Birthright citizenship: replace the pending-case description

On **June 30, 2026**, the Supreme Court decided *Trump v. Barbara*, holding that children born in the United States to parents unlawfully or temporarily present are citizens at birth under the Fourteenth Amendment. It affirmed the judgment below. The earlier essay in this collection predates that decision; its hypothetical ancestry-documentation consequences are not current requirements. [Supreme Court opinion, syllabus and majority opinion](https://www.supremecourt.gov/opinions/25pdf/25-365_4hdj.pdf).

The operational question for the City is whether its own forms or contractors impose unnecessary citizenship documentation, not how to implement the rejected executive-order theory. Counsel should review any proposed eligibility requirement against the actual program authority.

## H.R. 1: Medicaid eligibility and SNAP cost shift

H.R. 1 removed Medicaid eligibility, effective **October 1, 2026**, for refugees, asylees, humanitarian parolees, and trafficking victims. Green card holders, Cuban and Haitian entrants, and Compact of Free Association migrants keep eligibility; the affected groups are populations the federal government itself admitted on humanitarian grounds. Maryland estimates up to 4,500 residents affected. Subsidized ACA marketplace coverage for the same groups ends in January 2027. [Maryland Matters, Oct. 1, 2026](https://marylandmatters.org/2026/10/01/thousands-of-immigrants-in-maryland-to-lose-medicaid-coverage-under-h-r-1/); [[H.R. 1 Ends Medicaid for Refugees and Asylees in Maryland]].

The same effective date shifted SNAP administrative costs from a 50-50 federal-state split to 75 percent State, 25 percent federal, adding an estimated $57 million to Maryland's budget on top of the $115 million it already spends, with up to $240 million more possible in 2027. This is an enacted statutory change, not a proposal; it reduces state fiscal capacity for safety-net programs generally, not only those serving immigrants. For the local service-access and household-harm consequences, see [[Effects of Immigration Enforcement]]; for the food-assistance budget mechanics, see [[Food Access and SNAP]].

## SBA lending restrictions and business support

SBA's 7(a)/504 notice imposes revised ownership, citizenship and residency requirements effective **March 1, 2026**. Its application rules distinguish processing stages; staff should use the notice rather than assume that every existing loan or pending application is affected identically. A separate microloan policy notice is effective **April 1, 2026**. These are program-specific restrictions, not a ban on immigrant business ownership or all commercial credit. [7(a)/504 procedural notice](https://legacy.sba.gov/document/procedural-notice-5000-876626-revised-applicant-ownership-citizenship-residency-requirements-7a-504-loans); [microloan policy notice](https://legacy.sba.gov/document/policy-notice-5000-877232-update-sop-52-00-b-microloan-program-citizenship-requirements).

Economic Development can map affected financing pathways, connect owners to lenders and technical assistance, and identify gaps in otherwise available local programs. Any City-funded alternative needs explicit eligibility, underwriting, administration and loss-reserve costs; it should not be advertised as an automatic substitute for federal guarantees.

## Council options and tradeoffs

| Option | Benefit | Cost, limit or safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Maintain a dated referral and policy-update protocol | Reduces misinformation and repeated staff research | Assign an owner and update triggers; providers make individual legal assessments |
| Expand legal screening and navigation | Helps residents identify actual options and deadlines | Attorney capacity and recurring funding; track service access without case files |
| Assess affected small-business financing | Makes economic impacts visible | Aggregated lender/provider data; avoid collecting unnecessary status information |
| Prepare service-continuity contingencies | Anticipates interruptions in household income and provider grants | Separate documented demand from scenarios; identify funding source and sunset |
| Advocate to federal/state partners | Communicates local costs and priorities | Advocacy is not a change in federal eligibility |

These are policy options, not newly introduced measures. Track any legislative or budget initiative in [[Immigration - In-Progress Plans]] and verify live status against the Legislative Roadmap.

## Questions before action

- What exact program, population, effective date and controlling document are involved?
- Does a court order change implementation, and for whom?
- Is the figure national, regional or local; an estimate, ceiling, application or completed admission?
- Which City decision is actually required, and what authority supports it?
- What is the recurring cost, provider capacity, funding restriction and review date?
- Can the City measure access without retaining immigration files?

## Measures and maintenance

Track referral wait time, completed consultations, unmet provider demand, language coverage, business-assistance uptake and aggregate service interruptions. Report time periods and denominators. Do not equate a referral with a successful immigration outcome.
