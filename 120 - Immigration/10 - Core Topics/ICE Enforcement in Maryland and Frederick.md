# ICE Enforcement in Maryland and Frederick

## Decision frame

Frederick can govern its own employees, facilities, spending, records, and public-safety response. It cannot direct ICE operations or determine an individual's immigration case. The immediate Council questions are whether City personnel follow enacted policy, whether residents and observers receive an appropriate public-safety response, and whether evidence and costs are documented well enough to support independent accountability.

This page distinguishes an event, a reported allegation, an official request for investigation, and an adjudicated finding. An official's letter verifies that the request was made; it does not establish every allegation in it. Review date: **October 3, 2026**. No completed investigation or comprehensive City arrest dataset was established in this review.

[September 17 delegation letter](https://www.vanhollen.senate.gov/news/press-releases/maryland-delegation-members-call-for-answers-investigation-following-alarming-ice-operation-in-frederick).

Follow-up reporting adds the local response and the competing accounts of police authority ([Baltimore Banner](https://www.thebanner.com/community/local-news/ice-agents-clash-frederick-protestors-next-steps-PLMNNZV2AVCABNG7NGE2MUISL4/); [[Frederick After the ICE Clash Police Role and City Response]]):

- **FPD's stated role.** Chief Meyer said officers cannot interfere with a federal operation, were present to deter excessive force, and kept body cameras running. ICE would not tell FPD whether it held a warrant. A retired Howard County police colonel noted federal agencies normally notify local police before operating in a jurisdiction.
- **Mayor's position.** Mayor O'Connor said officers who observe a rights violation must decide what level of intervention is needed to stop it, a broader view than the Chief's, and said he would review the bodycam footage with the Chief. The difference matters for any written FPD guidance and for the City's request to the Attorney General.
- **Sheriff's position.** Sheriff Jenkins, a plaintiff in the sheriffs' suit against the state non-cooperation laws, attributes the surge to those laws. That is a causal claim the record does not establish.
- **Community infrastructure.** Clergy trained as ICE observers after watching the Minneapolis surge, and volunteers were already walking children home from school, delivering groceries to families with a detained relative, and circulating neighborhood alerts before the clash. These networks are a resource for rapid response and for documenting incidents.

The detailed local chronology and competing accounts remain in [[ICE Raid Press Coverage Brief -- 2026-09-09]] and the original concept snapshot. Do not count several articles reproducing the same video or statement as independent corroboration. Preserve original files, timestamps, provenance, and the distinction between firsthand observations and subsequent summaries.

## City, County, state, and federal roles

| Actor | Relevant responsibility | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| City Council | City policy and appropriations; oversight of implementation; requests for information and intergovernmental action | Cannot command federal agents or the independently elected County sheriff |
| Mayor, Legal Department, and City departments | Implement G-26-1, designate supervisors, review requests, preserve records, document incidents and costs | Follow the enacted text and applicable law; do not improvise new powers |
| Frederick Police Department | City public safety, emergency response, evidence, and applicable departmental duties | Presence at an incident is not by itself proof of assisting immigration enforcement; record the specific activity |
| County sheriff and detention center | County custody and sheriff operations | Their historical practices are not FPD practices |
| Maryland Attorney General and courts | Litigation, state legal guidance, and judicial review within their jurisdiction | A complaint or request does not guarantee relief |
| Federal agencies | Federal immigration investigation, arrest, detention, and removal functions | Their authority remains subject to statutory and constitutional limits |

Frederick County's jail-based 287(g) participation began in 2008. Maryland enacted SB245/HB444 on February 17, 2026 to prohibit such deputization agreements, effective immediately. AP reported the resulting halt in Frederick's program. This change concerns delegated local enforcement; it does not end independent federal operations. Claims that ending 287(g) caused a particular later street operation require evidence beyond timing. [Governor's enactment announcement](https://governor.maryland.gov/news/press-releases/governor-moore-signs-legislation-prohibit-maryland-jurisdictions-deputizing-officers-federal-civil); [AP reporting](https://apnews.com/article/72929a61953c3da5ed5d49cab2dcc611). See [[Frederick County Immigration Enforcement -- Jenkins, Community Harm, and the Limits of Reform]] for history.

The February 287(g) law, the [[Maryland Community Trust Act]], and the [[City Values Act]] are distinct measures. Check each law and relevant court orders rather than treating them as a single statewide prohibition.

## Definitions that change the analysis

- **Judicial warrant versus administrative warrant:** identify issuer, legal authority, target, scope, and requested action. A document issued within an immigration agency is not a warrant signed by a judge. Whether an arrest is authorized and whether entry into a particular place is authorized are separate questions.
- **Warrantless civil immigration arrest:** the relevant interior-arrest provision of 8 U.S.C. §1357(a)(2) requires reason to believe the person is unlawfully present and likely to escape before a warrant can be obtained. The Maryland litigation challenges compliance with those requirements; reasonable suspicion for a brief stop is not interchangeable with the arrest standard. [Statutory text](https://uscode.house.gov/view.xhtml?edition=prelim&num=0&req=granuleid%3AUSC-prelim-title8-section1357).
- **Collateral arrest:** a person arrested during an operation who was not its original target. That classification alone establishes neither the person's criminal history nor that the arrest was lawful or unlawful.
- **No conviction, no pending charge, and no criminal record:** different categories. None alone determines immigration status or whether an arrest complied with law.
- **Arrest, detention, and removal:** different events. Arrest totals over a period cannot be substituted for the number detained on one date or the number removed.

These distinctions are for evaluating policy and records, not a substitute for case-specific advice to residents.

## Maryland litigation and evidence

In September 2026, CASA, Amica Center, ACLU of Maryland, and partners announced litigation alleging unlawful warrantless arrests, including the detention of a U.S. citizen. The announcement is a primary source for the plaintiffs' allegations and requested relief, not a judicial finding. Obtain the operative complaint and latest order before asserting class scope, an injunction, or a binding rule resulting from that case. [Coalition announcement](https://www.aclu-md.org/press-releases/maryland-immigrant-rights-coalition-sues-ice-over-warrantless-arrest-first-ask-questions-later-policy/).

Maryland's SB 1 police mask ban, barring officers including federal agents from wearing face coverings on duty, took effect October 1, 2026. DOJ sued the same day to block it as applied to federal officers, naming the State, Prince George's County, and Montgomery County, on an intergovernmental-immunity theory. [Maryland Matters, Oct. 1, 2026](https://marylandmatters.org/2026/10/01/trump-administration-challenges-marylands-ban-on-police-using-masks/); [[DOJ Sues to Block Maryland Police Mask Ban]]. See [[Local Authority and Federal Preemption]] for the full preemption analysis. The relevant distinction for Frederick: a mask requirement for Frederick Police and other City personnel sits on the City's own authority over its officers and is not the exposed version; a rule purporting to bind federal agents during an operation like Taskers Chance is the version DOJ is actively litigating against. If a local unmask ordinance is proposed following Taskers Chance, it should track the defensible version.

The older source collection includes Maryland/Virginia surge numbers, national detention snapshots, collateral-arrest analysis, and demographic breakdowns. Those describe different populations and dates. They are not a Frederick arrest rate. Demographic disparities can warrant investigation, but proving an unlawful selection practice requires examination of the comparison population, stop circumstances, agency records, and legal standard.

Before citing a number, record **source dataset, geography, date range, unit counted, denominator, criminal-history definition, and missing-data treatment**. ## The federal accountability gap

Frederick's September 21 request to the Attorney General for guidance on officers' duty to intervene against federal force ([[AG Investigation and Guidance Request Letter]]) sits inside a larger, national pattern: local police can be sued for misconduct under 42 U.S.C. §1983, but federal agents are largely immune, so a city cannot discipline or be sued over ICE conduct and non-cooperation policy is the main lever it has. Two recent shootings illustrate the gap and an emerging litigation strategy around it. In Minneapolis, an ICE officer shot and killed Renee Good, a U.S. citizen with no criminal record, during a January 2026 operation; the officer was not indicted or fired and remains active. Her family filed two federal suits: a Federal Tort Claims Act wrongful-death claim against the United States, and a Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871 (42 U.S.C. §§1985(3), 1986) civil-rights-conspiracy claim naming senior officials, a route chosen because the Supreme Court has sharply narrowed *Bivens* as a path to sue federal officers directly. Days after that filing, plainclothes ICE officers in Manhattan's Marble Hill neighborhood shot and wounded Oscar Belgal within seconds of surrounding his car, with his 5-year-old son in the back seat. [[Renee Good Family Lawsuits Test Federal Immunity for ICE Shootings]]; American Immigration Council, October 2026.

This is out-of-state litigation, not a Maryland case, but it is the same accountability asymmetry behind the Mayor's and Chief's differing views on officer intervention during Taskers Chance, above, and it is relevant background for the pending AG guidance request: whatever the AG advises on FPD's duty to intervene will operate against a backdrop where victims of federal enforcement violence have very limited direct recourse against the officers involved.

## Council options and tradeoffs

| Option | Practical value | Cost, risk, or limitation |
|---|---|---|
| Audit existing CVA implementation | Establishes whether supervisors, facility designations, legal review, and required reports work in practice | Staff and legal time; a compliance audit cannot measure all federal activity |
| Establish a consistent incident and evidence workflow | Supports reliable public explanation, referrals, and legal review | Secure storage, records management, interpretation, and privacy safeguards; avoid a resident immigration-status registry |
| Obtain written state guidance and track investigation requests | Clarifies contested duties and preserves accountability channels | Depends on other institutions; receipt of a request is not a promised investigation |
| Coordinate legal and household-support referrals | Addresses immediate consequences of detention without waiting for litigation | Provider capacity and recurring funding; confirm current intake arrangements |
| Track response costs under §33-13 | Makes the City's resource burden reviewable | Documented costs are not guaranteed federal reimbursement |
| Consider additional legislation after the implementation review | Can address a demonstrated gap | Counsel must assess authority, preemption, administrative burden, and duplication |

The enacted ordinance already supplies the first implementation duties. Under §33-11, department reports are due within 30 days of quarter-end and the consolidated Council/public report within 45 days. For Q3 2026, those dates are **October 30 and November 14**; the annual report is due March 31. Reports must protect identifying information. See [[City Values Act -- Enacted Ordinance G-26-1]] for controlling text.

Family disruption, lost wages, school impacts, and vehicle recovery belong in [[Effects of Immigration Enforcement]] and [[Immigrant Legal Services and Local Support]]. The “ghost cars” reporting concerns other Maryland jurisdictions and does not establish Frederick's caseload or a locally applicable tow-release rule. Individual detention cases retained below illustrate issues; recheck their current procedural status before public use.

## Oversight measures and next-decision checklist

Track City interactions, not an unsupported estimate of all ICE activity:

- Number and type of requests received; department, legal authority presented, response, and disposition.
- Timeliness and completeness of quarterly reports, training, and facility inventories.
- Evidence-preservation requests and responses; separate referrals, opened investigations, findings, and remedies.
- City staff hours, overtime, outside costs, amounts invoiced, and amounts actually recovered.
- Aggregate referral completion and unmet need, with provider consent and minimal personal information.

Before a hearing or new measure, obtain the latest federal/state responses, applicable orders, City incident records, counsel's explanation of disputed duties, and an implementation cost estimate. A zero in a City interaction report means no reportable interaction was recorded; it does not prove no federal enforcement occurred.
