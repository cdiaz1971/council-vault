# Warrant-Based ICE Restrictions Create Legal Exposure, Not Prevention

## Summary
Two notes filed August 12, one on a Howard County immigration facility permitting dispute and one on an ICE arrest at a Baltimore school, describe the same underlying dynamic from two different angles. Legal restrictions on ICE, whether built around a permitting ban or a warrant requirement, create real friction and legal exposure for the government, but they do not stop a facility from being built or an encounter from happening in the moment. That has direct bearing on how the City Values Act should be understood and communicated.

## The Pattern
In Howard County, the county revoked a permit and passed emergency legislation banning private contractors from holding detention-facility permits. A federal judge sided with the developer's financial-harm argument and ordered construction to resume, regardless of the county's land use and public safety objections. The restriction did not stop the facility. It generated a lawsuit the county lost.

In Baltimore, state and city laws require a judicial warrant before ICE can access school property. None of that stopped agents from pulling a family from their car in a school parking lot during drop-off, because the restriction governs what school and local officials can do to assist ICE, not what federal agents can do once they encounter someone in a public-adjacent space. The man was released two weeks later because a judge found no probable cause, not because the sensitive-location law prevented the arrest from happening.

In both cases, the restriction wins after the fact, in court, rather than before the fact, on the ground.

## Implications for the City Values Act
CVA is built on the same architecture as the Baltimore and Maryland school laws: a warrant requirement for access to the private areas of city buildings and city property, and a no-cooperation posture for city employees. That structure is more legally defensible than Howard County's approach, since CVA conditions access rather than attempting to ban a category of federal activity outright, the way Howard County tried to ban private-contractor detention facilities entirely. But the Baltimore case shows warrant-based structures share the same practical ceiling regardless of how well-drafted they are: they create legal exposure for agents who violate the rule and a path to remedy afterward, but they do not physically prevent an encounter in a parking lot, a sidewalk, or any space outside the specific areas CVA actually covers.

## What This Changes
Public messaging on CVA should be precise about what it does. It creates legal exposure and a path to remedy if the rule is violated. It does not make an encounter impossible, and the Baltimore incident is a concrete example of exactly that gap playing out under a comparable legal structure. Overpromising prevention risks trust with the community if an encounter ever happens in Frederick the way it happened in Baltimore.

Know-your-rights guidance from the Frederick Rapid Response Network and Centro Hispano needs to explicitly cover public-adjacent spaces, parking lots, sidewalks, and drop-off zones, since that is where the actual gap in a warrant-based protection shows up. CVA's coverage of building interiors and private areas is already solid; the exposure is at the perimeter.

## Next Actions
1. Raise the accuracy-of-messaging point with comms before any CVA anniversary or public education material goes out, so materials describe legal exposure and remedy rather than implying prevention.
2. Confirm with Rachel Gray whether CVA's conditional-access structure is in fact more defensible than Howard County's contractor-type ban if CVA is ever challenged.
3. Flag the parking-lot and adjacent-space gap to the Frederick Rapid Response Network for know-your-rights material updates.
