# Municipal Sovereignty Against Federal Enforcement Infrastructure -- Data, Land, and Technology as the Three Fronts

## Summary

The City Values Act (Ordinance G-26-1, passed 7-0 April 2, 2026) established Frederick's non-cooperation posture as a matter of city staff conduct and city property. It was the right first move. But vault research across three separate threads now reveals that federal enforcement infrastructure has expanded well beyond the domain the CVA was designed to address. The federal government is building enforcement capacity through data acquisition, physical facility siting, and technology procurement -- three channels that local non-cooperation policies alone cannot counter. This note maps each front, inventories the local authority tools available, and names the gaps.

---

## The Three Fronts

### Front 1 -- Data

Federal enforcement is acquiring and combining personal data through channels that bypass local cooperation entirely.

The SAVE database (ruled illegal June 2026) was built by the Trump administration combining SSA citizenship data with other federal records to create a national voter verification system -- a purpose Congress explicitly prohibited. States ran voter rolls through it, wrongly flagging citizens as non-citizens and canceling their registrations. The court ordered it dismantled, but the pattern it represents -- executive branch data consolidation that exceeds statutory authority -- remains active.

The ICE/ITIN data broker loophole (documented in [[ICE Buying Immigrant Tax Identifiers -- Data Broker Loophole in Action]]) shows the commercial channel: federal agencies purchasing financial identity data from private brokers to identify and locate undocumented immigrants. This requires no local cooperation, no subpoena, no public process. It happens in the market.

**What the CVA covers:** City staff are prohibited from sharing information about residents' immigration status with federal enforcement agencies. City databases and city-held data are protected.

**What the CVA does not cover:** Federal agencies acquiring data commercially or combining federal databases to build enforcement infrastructure. Frederick cannot prevent ICE from buying ITIN records or SSA data from brokers. The CVA protects what city employees do; it does not protect what federal agencies can buy.

**Gap:** Frederick has no data governance policy that addresses the commercial data broker channel. A city data privacy ordinance that limits what data the city collects (reducing the surface area of potential subpoenas) and that takes a public position on data broker sales is the logical next step. Maryland's Data Privacy Act (HB 711, became law May 2026) prohibits selling personal data for immigration enforcement -- that is a state-level partial answer, but it does not bind federal agencies purchasing through federal appropriations.

---

### Front 2 -- Land

Federal enforcement is converting industrial warehouses into detention infrastructure at scale, with near-absolute authority over federally owned property.

The Trump administration's "Big Beautiful Bill" allocated $45 billion to ICE for new detention centers. ICE has already purchased warehouses in at least eight states including a $102M acquisition in Maryland, an $84M purchase in Pennsylvania, and a $70M purchase in Arizona. Detention capacity has grown from roughly 37,000 beds in early 2025 to over 72,000 by January 2026, with a stated goal of 100,000.

The Planetizen/Reyes analysis (documented in [[Zoning as Resistance -- Cities Using Land Use Law to Block ICE Detention Centers]]) is clear: local governments have almost no tools to block facilities on federally owned property. Federal sovereign authority overrides local zoning for government-owned land. The only windows for local intervention are (1) before federal acquisition, through community pressure on private sellers; (2) zoning restrictions on privately operated detention facilities under contract to the federal government; (3) permit revocation before construction is complete on non-federally-owned property; and (4) utility service leverage in some jurisdictions.

**What the CVA covers:** City property cannot be used for immigration enforcement. City staff cannot participate in enforcement actions.

**What the CVA does not cover:** Federal acquisition of private property within or near Frederick's jurisdiction for conversion to detention infrastructure. The CVA provides no tool against a federally owned facility. Frederick does not currently face a known ICE facility siting threat, but the Maryland acquisition is real and the pattern is active.

**Gap:** Frederick does not currently have a zoning amendment restricting where privately operated detention facilities can be located within city limits. San Antonio adopted such an amendment after concluding it lacked authority to block the federally owned facility. That is the available local tool for the privately operated channel -- and it is available now, before any siting threat emerges, which is when it is most useful.

---

### Front 3 -- Technology

Federal enforcement is entering local jurisdictions through technology procurement channels that city councils can influence but not always see in advance.

The Flock Safety ALPR system (documented in [[Flock ALPR Tracks Bluetooth Devices -- Police Stalking Cases and Surveillance Creep]]) has expanded its capability beyond license plate reading to Bluetooth device tracking, meaningfully expanding its surveillance footprint. Flock entered numerous jurisdictions through procurement processes that did not anticipate this capability expansion. The surveillance vendor capture pattern (documented in [[Surveillance Vendor Capture -- How Technology Gets Into Local Government Against Community Will]]) shows how technology gets into local government before community consent or council oversight catches it.

The BusPatrol notes document the child safety Trojan horse mechanism -- surveillance technology entering schools and transit through emotionally compelling frames that bypass the deliberative process.

**What the CVA covers:** City staff non-cooperation posture on immigration enforcement.

**What the CVA does not cover:** Surveillance technology that FPD or other city departments procure independently, that federal agencies access through data-sharing agreements, or that private vendors deploy on city infrastructure under contracts the council did not specifically authorize with enforcement access in mind.

**Gap:** Frederick has no surveillance technology procurement governance ordinance. The active next step -- developing contract language based on [[Municipal Tech Consent Architecture -- Contract Language, Audit Standards, and Resident Notification]] and determining whether any Flock or ALPR contracts are currently active in Frederick -- is documented in the Surveillance Technology Procurement Governance thread but not yet acted on.

## What a More Complete Municipal Sovereignty Posture Requires

The CVA is the foundation. The three-front analysis points to what needs to be built on top of it:

**On data:** A city data minimization policy and a public position on data broker sales. Coordinate with state-level implementation of Maryland's Data Privacy Act. Consider a resolution naming the commercial data broker channel as an enforcement threat and calling on the state to strengthen enforcement of HB 711.

**On land:** A zoning amendment restricting private detention facility siting -- drafted proactively, before any specific siting threat, when the political environment is calmer and the drafting can be deliberate. San Antonio's model is the closest precedent.

**On technology:** A surveillance technology procurement governance ordinance with mandatory council approval, community notification requirements, and contract language standards that limit federal enforcement data access. This is the most actionable near-term gap given the active Flock research already in the vault.

**Cross-cutting:** The [[Immigration - In-Progress Plans#Institutional Solidarity Pact|Institutional Solidarity Pact planning]] thread names regional civic coordination as the infrastructure that makes all three fronts more defensible. No single city's data policy, zoning amendment, or procurement ordinance is sufficient against federal enforcement operating at national scale. The coalition is the multiplier.

## Connection to the Infrastructure of Democratic Survival

This three-front analysis maps directly onto the Layer 3 argument in the Infrastructure of Democratic Survival synthesis: civic institutions and local government as the physical and legal infrastructure of democratic survival. The CVA was Layer 1 (individual political presence -- the city declaring a posture). The zoning and procurement work is Layer 3 (institutional infrastructure -- the rules and systems that make the posture durable). The coalition work is the connection between them.

Federal enforcement infrastructure -- data, land, technology -- is an authoritarian project at the physical layer. The local response has to operate at the same layer.
