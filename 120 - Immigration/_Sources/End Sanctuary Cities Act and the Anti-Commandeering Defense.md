# End Sanctuary Cities Act and the Anti-Commandeering Defense

## Summary

S. 3805 would criminalize local officials who decline to honor ICE detainers. Legal analysis argues the bill is unconstitutional under the Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering doctrine -- courts have consistently held that detainer compliance is voluntary.

## Key Points

Senator Lindsey Graham introduced the End Sanctuary Cities Act of 2026 in January 2026, updated in February, with a House companion from Congressman Lance Gooden. The bill would make it a criminal offense for state and local officials to willfully interfere with federal immigration enforcement -- including declining to honor ICE detainers, withholding immigration records, or releasing detained individuals when ICE has requested they be held.

The constitutional objection is grounded in the Tenth Amendment and the anti-commandeering doctrine developed from it. The federal government cannot compel state or local officials to implement or enforce a federal regulatory program. Courts have applied this doctrine consistently to immigration detainers: compliance is voluntary, not mandatory. This is settled law.

DHS's own enforcement data undermines the public safety framing: 73 percent of those detained in recent operations had no criminal conviction, and DHS classified 73 percent of nationwide detainees as presenting no threat. The Major City Chiefs Association -- representing police chiefs in the largest US cities -- has stated that decisions about local law enforcement resource priorities must remain with local governments. Research supports this: sanctuary policies reduce deportations of people without criminal records while leaving deportations of those with violent records unchanged.

The bill will almost certainly be litigated if it passes. The historical pattern of anti-commandeering cases suggests at least partial invalidation. The more immediate risk is not the bill passing but the political pressure it generates and the compliance costs it imposes during litigation.

## Relevance

Frederick officials are not legally required to honor ICE detainers under current law -- and that would remain true even if this bill passed, during the period of almost-certain legal challenge. Any council communication on this should be grounded in that legal reality rather than framed as a policy preference.

The argument to make publicly: local law enforcement resource allocation is a local prerogative, detainer compliance is voluntary as a matter of constitutional law, and the Major City Chiefs Association agrees. Cite DHS's own data on threat classification to avoid being framed as shielding criminals. The policy is about constitutional limits and local priorities, not about protecting anyone from criminal enforcement.

[[City Values Act -- Frederick Ordinance G-26-1]] is Frederick's existing legislative commitment in this space. This federal bill is the hostile federal environment in which that ordinance operates.
