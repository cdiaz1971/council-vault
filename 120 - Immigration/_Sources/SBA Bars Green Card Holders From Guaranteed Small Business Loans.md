# SBA Bars Green Card Holders From Guaranteed Small Business Loans

## Summary

A February 2026 SBA policy notice barred green card holders from owning any share of a business applying for SBA-guaranteed loans, reversing a decades-old rule that allowed lawful permanent residents to own up to 100 percent, cutting off a historically important low-cost financing source for immigrant-owned small businesses and already visibly affecting commercial corridors.

## Key Points

Starting March 1, 2026, the SBA's flagship 7(a) and 504 loan programs require 100 percent of a business's owners to be U.S. citizens or U.S. nationals residing in the U.S. or its territories. Under the prior long-standing rule, a business could qualify with as little as 51 percent ownership by U.S. citizens, nationals, or lawful permanent residents, meaning up to 49 percent could be held by a foreign national of any status. A March 9, 2026 follow-up notice expanded the same 100 percent citizenship requirement to SBA's microloan and Surety Bond Guarantee programs effective April 1.

The rule applies across direct and indirect ownership, so even a small minority stake (as little as 1 percent, per some coverage) held by a green card holder disqualifies the whole business. SBA framed the change as prioritizing "American job creators" under finite lending authority; advocacy groups and immigrant business networks (CAMEO Network, NCRC, Small Business Majority) argue immigrants start businesses at roughly twice the rate of native-born citizens and that the change removes access to loans that are typically lower-interest and more available to owners without an established U.S. credit history.

Existing SBA loans are unaffected, but businesses with financing applications still pending as of March 1 needed full approval before that date to remain eligible, and any future change in ownership that adds a non-citizen owner can jeopardize an existing loan's compliance going forward. Commercial corridors that depend on immigrant-owned storefronts are already reporting effects from reduced access to this financing channel.

## Relevance

Directly relevant to the immigrant business community served by Centro Hispano and SSCM, and a concrete example of federal policy narrowing economic pathways for lawfully present immigrants even absent any change in local enforcement posture, the same pattern of municipal-level actors needing to fill gaps left by federal retrenchment tracked in [[Cities as the Pragmatic Immigration Policy Actors When National Governments Fail]].
