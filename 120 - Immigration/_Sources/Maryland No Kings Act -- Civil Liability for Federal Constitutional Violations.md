# Maryland No Kings Act -- Civil Liability for Federal Constitutional Violations

## Summary

Maryland enacted the No Kings Act (Senate Bill 346 / House Bill 351) on May 13, 2026, giving Marylanders and the state attorney general the ability to pursue civil litigation against any federal official who violates someone's constitutional rights under color of law. The law takes effect October 1, 2026.

## Key Points

The law was described by sponsors as a civil remedy for "federal lawlessness" -- a structured pathway to challenge federal conduct that violates constitutional rights without relying on federal courts or federal enforcement mechanisms. It does not apply to persons acting under a joint task force that primarily enforces federal law, which carves out ICE task force operations as a partial limitation.

The sponsors, Montgomery County Democrats Sen. Jeff Waldstreicher and House Majority Leader David Moon, framed it alongside dozens of other Maryland bills passed this session that collectively constitute a broad state-level pushback against the Trump administration. Del. Lorig Charkoudian, whose prior legislation provided the "color of law" language, described the act as part of "many strategies" the legislature deployed this session.

It was signed alongside Mason's Law (requiring municipal storm drain inspection and repair -- see [[Masons Law -- Maryland Storm Drain Inspection Requirements]]) and roughly 200 other bills in a single ceremony.

## Relevance

As Council President, this matters in two ways. First, Frederick residents who have constitutional rights violated by federal officials -- including during immigration enforcement -- now have an explicit state-law civil remedy available to them starting October 1. Second, the act strengthens Maryland's legal infrastructure for democratic resistance, which is directly relevant to the anti-authoritarian organizing track and to the framing work César is doing around the City Values Act and municipal ID. This is the state-level scaffolding that local organizing plugs into.

The carve-out for joint task forces is worth watching. It limits the act's reach against ICE operations conducted through federal-local task forces, which is one of the primary enforcement vectors in Maryland.
