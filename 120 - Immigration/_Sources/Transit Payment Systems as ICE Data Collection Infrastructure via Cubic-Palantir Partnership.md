# Transit Payment Systems as ICE Data Collection Infrastructure via Cubic-Palantir Partnership

## Summary

Cubic Corporation, a defense contractor whose executives come from major weapons manufacturers, operates tap-to-pay fare systems in multiple US transit agencies and signed a late-2025 partnership with Palantir, the company that built ImmigrationOS for ICE. Civil rights and immigrant advocates warn that credit card, phone, and fare card data captured by Cubic's cloud database could be shared with ICE.

## Key Points

Cubic describes its analytics product as offering "de-identification" of personal data that turns rider information into "an analytics ready dataset that can be securely consumed for research, monetization schemes, and other internal and external purposes" and includes "personalized actionable information" sent directly to riders' phones.

The de-identification claim does not resolve the concern. Palantir's ImmigrationOS is specifically designed to re-identify individuals across fragmented data sources. A dataset that has been "de-identified" in transit may be re-identified when combined with other sources in a Palantir environment.

The partnership is not a contract with ICE itself. It is a corporate relationship between two companies that creates a potential data pipeline. No transit agency has publicly consented to their data being used for immigration enforcement, but the architecture makes it possible without any additional agreement.

## Relevance

Frederick does not currently use Cubic or a Palantir-connected transit payment system, but this is the pattern to watch: infrastructure that communities depend on being quietly connected to enforcement networks through corporate partnerships rather than government contracts. This is the same vendor capture dynamic documented in [[Surveillance Vendor Capture -- How Technology Gets Into Local Government Against Community Will]] applied to transit rather than ALPR.

For Frederick's surveillance governance work, this is an argument for requiring any technology procurement to include data use agreements that expressly prohibit sale, sharing, or partnership-based transfer of rider or user data to immigration enforcement. The Frederick data center ZTA opposition argues that infrastructure decisions have long tails. So does transit technology procurement.

The Community Trust Act protects against direct law enforcement cooperation with ICE. It does not protect against data flowing through corporate intermediaries. That gap is where the next enforcement infrastructure will be built.
