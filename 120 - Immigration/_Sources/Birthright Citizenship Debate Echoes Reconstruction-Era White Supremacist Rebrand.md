# Birthright Citizenship Debate Echoes Reconstruction-Era White Supremacist Rebrand

## Summary
Next City argues that today's birthright citizenship debate is not new but a repeat of the argument white America made after Reconstruction, revived through D.W. Griffith's 1915 film *The Birth of a Nation* and now through Executive Order 14160. The Supreme Court ruled 5-4 on June 30, 2026 against the order, relying heavily on historical scholarship about the 14th Amendment's original scope.

## Key Points
Executive Order 14160, "Protecting the Meaning and Value of American Citizenship," signed January 20, 2025, directed federal agencies to stop recognizing citizenship for children born in the U.S. to foreign-born parents. The administration argued the 14th Amendment's citizenship clause ("All persons born or naturalized in the United States, and subject to the jurisdiction thereof...") was written only for formerly enslaved Black people, not for anyone born on U.S. soil.

The 14th Amendment (1866) was drafted in response to the 1857 *Dred Scott* decision, which held no Black person -- free or enslaved -- had citizenship rights. The article frames the amendment's soil-based citizenship principle as the central obstacle to a late-19th-century white-supremacist "rebrand" because it rendered whiteness ordinary rather than a precondition for citizenship. D.W. Griffith's 1915 film *The Birth of a Nation* (adapted from Thomas Dixon Jr.'s novel *The Clansman*) is presented as the most vivid form of that rebrand: it depicted Reconstruction as a threat to natural order, Black officeholders and freedmen as unfit for government, and the Ku Klux Klan as noble restorers of order. The article links the film's release to real violence: Black towns burned, elected officials removed by force, Black men hunted on rape accusations. Woodrow Wilson, a Johns Hopkins classmate of Dixon, gave the film an unprecedented White House screening and endorsed it, per the article.

On the legal merits, the Supreme Court's June 30, 2026 majority opinion relied heavily on Johns Hopkins historian Martha S. Jones's book *Birthright Citizens: A History of Race and Rights in Antebellum America*, and on *United States v. Wong Kim Ark* (1898), which held a man born in San Francisco to Chinese immigrant parents was a birthright citizen. Justice Clarence Thomas dissented in a 91-page opinion accepting the administration's narrow reading; the article notes the ruling was decided by a single vote. Historians Martha Jones and Kate Masur filed an amicus brief arguing the administration's account "omits, and thus misrepresents, the historical forces" behind the 14th Amendment, rejecting the claim that the clause's sole purpose was to cover freed slaves and their children.

Justice Ketanji Brown Jackson's concurrence added historical detail: 19th-century Black activists in the Colored Conventions Movement argued for universal birthright citizenship across races; Sen. Lyman Trumbull withdrew an early draft of the 1866 Civil Rights Act limited to African Americans and replaced it with language also covering children of Chinese and Romani immigrants; President Andrew Johnson vetoed the bill objecting to extending citizenship to "the Chinese of the Pacific States, Indians subject to taxation, the people called gypsies, as well as the entire race designated as blacks," and Congress overrode the veto.

The article ties this to other recent federal actions it frames as part of the same historical-rebranding pattern: Trump's February 2025 reversal of a 2020 law renaming nine Confederate-named Army bases, a March 27, 2025 executive order ("Restoring Truth and Sanity to American History") that led to removal of commemorations of Black historical figures from public sites including the Smithsonian's National Museum of African American History and Culture, and broader anti-DEI actions the article says cost "millions of dollars" in jobs, grants, loans, and research into racial disparities.

Note on sourcing: the article contains two internal inconsistencies -- it refers to the ruling as coming in "July" in one place and June 30 in another (June 30 matches the linked external reporting), and misspells Johns Hopkins as "John Hopkins" in one instance.

## Relevance
This is federal constitutional history and current litigation, not a Frederick-specific policy lever, but it is directly relevant background to César's immigration advocacy and the City Values Act work: it supplies the deeper historical frame (14th Amendment origins, Reconstruction-era backlash) for why birthright citizenship and immigrant-inclusion arguments matter, and it confirms the EO 14160 litigation outcome (struck down 5-4, June 30, 2026) that underlies the current federal posture toward immigrant families that the CVA and local non-cooperation policies respond to.
