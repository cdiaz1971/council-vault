# Art as Immigration Organizing Infrastructure During the 2026 World Cup

## Summary

Miami arts collective Artists for Artists: MIA joined the national No ICE in the Cup campaign during the 2026 FIFA World Cup, using poster art to distribute immigrant rights information, know-your-rights resources, and emergency hotlines to communities drawn to matches by diaspora loyalty rather than immigration awareness.

## Key Points

DHS Secretary Mullin publicly announced ICE would be "out there everyday" during World Cup events. Over 120 civil society organizations issued a travel advisory for international visitors. The campaign treated the World Cup as an organizing opportunity rather than a threat, using the international visibility to connect community members with protective information.

Johann munoz-tapasco, born in Colombia and formerly undocumented in the US, created a poster drawing on vintage Florida tourism postcard aesthetics, featuring an alligator tearing through a soccer ball. The image references the "Alligator Alcatraz" detention facility and attempts to reclaim the alligator symbol that right-wing media used to dehumanize migrants, reconnecting it to Florida's environmental and Indigenous histories.

The organizing model: distribute art that attracts attention on aesthetic grounds, embed within it emergency contacts and rights information, and use the cultural context of the event to reach people who would not otherwise engage with an immigration advocacy event. The campaign included an open call for South Florida artists to create additional posters.

Artist Paola Mendoza: "Artists can inspire people to organize against what they are afraid of. Art has the power to bring people together when fear pushes us into silos."

## Relevance

Frederick has Centro Hispano, SSCM, AACF, and other organizations doing community education work. The World Cup model is worth adapting: Frederick has a significant Central American population with soccer loyalty and diaspora ties. Cultural events connected to national teams create openings for reaching people who are not already connected to formal advocacy organizations. The Frederick Rapid Response Network's know-your-rights work could use similar aesthetic distribution strategies rather than relying on formal event settings.

The broader point the article makes is about political education through archiving: "so that people know how this came to be and how we prevent it from happening again." That is the same logic behind the Infrastructure of Democratic Survival synthesis note. Community memory of what happened, documented and accessible, is itself a form of civic infrastructure.
