# ICE Detention Infrastructure Fiscal Fallout When Federal Property Removes Local Tax Base

## Summary

After ICE appeared to abandon plans to convert two Pennsylvania warehouses into detention centers holding up to 7,500 and 1,500 people respectively, local taxing districts face a combined $1.6 million annual shortfall with no viable federal reimbursement mechanism. The case demonstrates how federal infrastructure imposition extracts fiscal value from local governments with no compensation.

## Key Points

When the federal government acquired the two warehouse properties, they became tax-exempt. Local school districts and county governments that previously collected property taxes on those parcels lost that revenue immediately. Federal PILT (Payment in Lieu of Taxes) programs generally cover entitlement lands administered by Interior, not DHS facilities. Neither Schuylkill nor Berks County expects PILT payments.

The federal government paid recording fees of $37,000 and $26,220 when the properties changed hands, plus real estate transfer taxes of $428,000 each to two local jurisdictions. Those one-time windfalls partially offset short-term losses but do not replace recurring annual tax revenue.

Local officials are caught between hoping for a sale that would restore the properties to the tax rolls and fearing the federal government will mothball the buildings indefinitely, leaving them as tax-exempt liabilities. ICE's legal challenges at the Pennsylvania Environmental Hearing Board are still active, suggesting the pivot is not fully formalized.

## Relevance

This is a useful case study in what happens when federal enforcement infrastructure arrives in a community without community consent and without a fiscal framework. The harm is not only to residents facing potential detention but to the fiscal base of local government itself. School districts lose funding. County budgets shrink. The federal government bears none of the cost.

This pattern is directly relevant to Frederick's data center ZTA analysis. The fiscal harm argument for data centers follows the same logic: large-footprint federal or corporate infrastructure can displace tax-generating uses, impose service costs, and leave communities without the tools to recoup. It also connects to the "costs without benefits" framing in [[Data Center Displaces Housing Funds -- Wiscasset Maine Case Study]].

For the Community Trust Act litigation context: Maryland counties that joined the sheriffs' suit against the CTA are in many cases the same counties that would face these fiscal dynamics if ICE infrastructure arrived. The interests of those county governments are not uniformly aligned with ICE, whatever their sheriffs' positions may be.
