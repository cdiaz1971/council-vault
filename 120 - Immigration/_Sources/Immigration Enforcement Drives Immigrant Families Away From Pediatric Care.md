# Immigration Enforcement Drives Immigrant Families Away From Pediatric Care

## Summary
An American Academy of Pediatrics survey of 1,600+ pediatricians found 40% saw immigrant families avoid medical care for their children over immigration status fears, with the effect sharpest in urban practices. It is hard evidence that enforcement reaches children who are often citizens.

## Key Points
AAP researchers surveyed more than 1,600 pediatricians between April and June 2026. On average about a quarter of their patients live in immigrant families. Asked about the past year, 40% reported families avoiding medical care for a child because of a household member's immigration status. 31% reported families avoiding applications for Medicaid, CHIP, or SNAP, and just over a fifth reported families keeping children home from school. The AAP considers these figures an undercount.

The avoidance is not evenly distributed. It reached 48% among urban and inner-city pediatricians and about 45% among subspecialists, compared with 32% among rural pediatricians. Clinicians interviewed by Stateline describe the downstream effect as kids arriving with more advanced illness, skipped preventive care, and forgone surgeries.

The policy driver is the January 2025 rescission of the protected-areas guidance that had generally kept immigration arrests away from hospitals, clinics, and schools. Nationally, a quarter of children have at least one foreign-born parent, which means the chilling effect lands heavily on citizen children in mixed-status households.

## Relevance
This is the healthcare leg of the pattern already documented in [[Immigration Enforcement Chilling Effect Compounds Across Schools Childcare and Legal Protections]]. Schools, childcare, and now pediatric care all show families retreating from services they are entitled to. It pairs with the public charge notes ([[New Public Charge Rule Expands to Non-Cash Benefits]], [[Public Charge Fear Could Push Millions Off Benefits Regardless of Eligibility]]), since the new rule compounds the same fear on the benefits side.

For Frederick, District 2's foreign-born share runs 1.4x the county rate, and the urban-versus-rural gap suggests city neighborhoods like the Golden Mile are where avoidance is likely concentrated. The data is directly usable in the September 29 URSP708 lecture on the City Values Act as an illustration of why local non-cooperation and trust-building matter. It also gives a concrete, non-partisan health framing for any response to the Tasker's Chance ICE confrontation. The most plausible local lever is partnership with Centro Hispano and community clinics on messaging that care sites are safe to use, since the city has no authority over federal enforcement locations.
