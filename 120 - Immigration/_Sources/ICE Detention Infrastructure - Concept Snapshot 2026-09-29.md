# ICE Detention Infrastructure

## The buildout

- **Scale.** Federal legislation allocated $45 billion to ICE for new detention. Detention capacity was reported at about 37,000 beds in early 2025 and more than 72,000 by January 2026, against a stated goal of 100,000 ([[Municipal Sovereignty Against Federal Enforcement Infrastructure -- Data, Land, and Technology as the Three Fronts]]).
- **Warehouse purchases.** The federal government has bought warehouses and industrial buildings in at least eight states for conversion, including **$102 million in Maryland**, $84 million in Pennsylvania, and $70 million in Arizona. Most local governments learned of the plans from news reports or organizers, not from DHS. On June 22, 2026, DHS was reported to be planning to sell or give away seven of eleven recently acquired warehouses. The Maryland property was not among them ([[Zoning as Resistance -- Cities Using Land Use Law to Block ICE Detention Centers]]).
- **Salt Lake City.** ICE bought an 830,000+ square-foot warehouse for $145.4 million in March 2026, for up to 10,000 people. Secretary Markwayne Mullin later paused new warehouse deals while reviewing Noem-era contracts ([[Utah Uproar Sues to Block ICE Detention Center Using Maryland Hagerstown as Model]]).

## Hagerstown and Maryland

- **Hagerstown (Washington County).** Maryland Attorney General Anthony Brown sued under the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA), arguing that DHS and ICE skipped required environmental review. In April 2026, U.S. District Judge Brendan Hurson granted a partial block that allowed only limited interior work. Opponents in Salt Lake City explicitly modeled their own NEPA litigation on the Maryland case ([[Utah Uproar Sues to Block ICE Detention Center Using Maryland Hagerstown as Model]]).
- **Howard County (Elkridge).** Howard County approved an office-to-detention conversion in 2025. In February 2026 it revoked the permit, saying it had been issued in error, and passed emergency legislation in under a week limiting detention-facility permits to government agencies. The developer, Genesis Strategic One, sued. In August 2026, U.S. District Judge Adam Abelson ordered construction to resume. He cited more than $21 million already invested, about $5,000 a day in interest, about $132,000 a month in forgone federal rent, and lender warnings. The county's safety and process objections didn't weigh the same way in the irreparable-harm analysis ([[Howard County Immigration Facility Case Shows Limits of Local Permitting Power Against ICE Infrastructure]]).
- **Frederick County.** An ICE office lease drew sustained public pressure at the County Immigrant Affairs Commission. The Commission sent County Executive Fitzwater a letter in April 2026, which was unanswered as of late May ([[IAC Brief 2026-05-27]]).
- **State legislation.** HB 1017, restricting and regulating private immigration detention in Maryland, advanced in the 2026 session alongside the Community Trust Act ([[Maryland Community Trust Act -- SB791 Passage and ICE Detainer Limits]]; see [[Maryland Community Trust Act]]).

## What local governments can and can't do

The core limit is that the federal government's authority over **its own property** effectively overrides local zoning. Local tools work mainly on **privately owned or privately operated** facilities, and in the window before acquisition or construction ([[Zoning as Resistance -- Cities Using Land Use Law to Block ICE Detention Centers]]):

- **Zoning restrictions on private operators.** After concluding it couldn't block a federally owned facility, San Antonio amended its zoning to sharply restrict where private detention centers can go.
- **Permit revocation before construction.** Howard County tried this and was overridden in court once the developer showed financial harm (above).
- **Utility service.** Utility denial has been used as a legal tool, in Social Circle, Georgia.
- **Pressure on private sellers.** In Kansas City, residents persuaded an owner not to sell to the federal government.
- **Certificates of occupancy and review demands (Romulus, Michigan).**
  - ICE planned to convert a warehouse near Detroit's airport into a facility for about 500 detainees.
  - The city council unanimously passed a resolution of opposition, and the mayor said he wouldn't issue a certificate of occupancy without a federal court order.
  - A coalition demanded technical and environmental review from five agencies and utilities.
  - Michigan's Attorney General cited the floodplain location and proximity to schools, and filed a FOIA request with DHS.
  - Coalition attorneys acknowledged that withholding permits was one of the few real tools available.

  ([[Local Government Resistance to ICE Detention Centers -- Romulus Michigan Case]])
- **NEPA litigation** has become the main legal vehicle against federal facilities, in Maryland and Utah (above).
- **State oversight of conditions.** Colorado expanded its health department's authority to inspect detention facilities, and a private prison operator is challenging the law ([[Courts Are Testing the Limits of State Authority Over Federal Immigration Enforcement]]).
- **What these restrictions actually do.** Like warrant rules, they tend to produce legal exposure and after-the-fact remedies rather than stopping a facility outright. Howard County is the example ([[Warrant-Based ICE Restrictions Create Legal Exposure Not Prevention]]).

## Fiscal cost to local governments

When the federal government buys a property, it comes off the tax rolls. In Pennsylvania, after ICE appeared to abandon plans to convert two warehouses (for up to 7,500 and 1,500 people), local taxing districts faced a combined **$1.6 million annual shortfall**. The PILT program generally covers Interior Department lands, not DHS facilities. One-time recording fees and $428,000 transfer taxes to each of two jurisdictions didn't replace recurring revenue ([[ICE Detention Infrastructure Fiscal Fallout When Federal Property Removes Local Tax Base]]).

## Who profits

Demos describes a "deportation-industrial complex":
1. Private operators such as CoreCivic and GEO Group hold ICE detention contracts.
2. Their profits grow as enforcement spending grows.
3. Campaign-finance loopholes let government contractors turn those profits into political influence over enforcement policy.

Demos's policy ask is closing those contractor loopholes ([[Private Prison Corporations and the Deportation Industrial Complex]]).

## Conditions and organizing outside the walls

- **Conditions.** Rodney Taylor, a double amputee held since January 2025, was repeatedly denied medical care and went six days without a shower because his facility lacked equipment for him, according to a letter from 21 members of Congress ([[Rodney Taylor -- Double Amputee Barber Detained by ICE Since January 2025]]).
- **Delaney Hall (Newark, New Jersey; privately operated).** In spring 2026, detained people ran hunger strikes, labor strikes, and public letters over about two weeks, including a hunger strike led by detained women. The ICE Out of New Jersey coalition of about ten organizations launched a hub site. Radio Cosecha and Radio Jornalera NJ documented the campaign, and families and volunteers held daily presence and vigils outside ([[NDLON Delaney Hall Update -- June 2026]]; [[Delaney Hall Resistance -- NDLON Update June 2026]]).
- **Adelanto (California).** Adelanto is one of the country's largest ICE detention centers, with a documented history of deaths in custody and inadequate medical care. On March 14, 2026, more than 300 people caravanned there for a serenata, a serenade in solidarity with detainees. Hunger strikes followed in May ([[NDLON -- A Song for Adelanto, Serenata at Immigration Detention]]; [[NDLON -- ICE Detention Hunger Strikes Adelanto and Delaney Hall]]).
- **Framing.** NDLON organizes around "solo el pueblo salva al pueblo," only the people save the people: community-led accompaniment, legal support, and amplifying detained people's own voices ([[NDLON -- ICE Detention Hunger Strikes Adelanto and Delaney Hall]]).
