# City Values Act

The City Values Act (CVA), Ordinance G-26-1, adds Chapter 33, Article II, "Immigration Enforcement Guidance," to the Frederick City Code. It sets out how City employees and City property interact with federal civil immigration enforcement: a judicial-warrant standard for non-public areas of City facilities, a prohibition on using City property for enforcement operations, a limit on City employees asking about immigration status, and a mechanism to seek cost recovery from federal agencies for enforcement-related expenses. Council President César Díaz introduced it on March 19, 2026. It passed 7-0 on April 2, 2026, was signed by Mayor O'Connor on April 6, and was certified for legal sufficiency by City Attorney Rachel Gray ([[City Values Act -- Enacted Ordinance G-26-1]]).

## What it does

Per the enacted text ([[City Values Act -- Enacted Ordinance G-26-1]]):

- **Facility access (§33-4).** A judicial warrant or exigent circumstances are required before a law enforcement officer may enter non-public areas of City facilities for an immigration enforcement operation. City employees may not assist access otherwise, and nothing in the section authorizes interfering with an officer once access is denied.
- **City property (§33-5).** City-owned, leased or managed property may not be used for immigration enforcement operations, meaning surveillance, staging, processing, detention or vehicle storage, without a judicial warrant or where required by law. This does not apply to temporary access needed for an immediate threat to public safety, or to any portion of a City facility already open to the general public.
- **Status inquiries (§33-6).** City employees may not ask about immigration status, citizenship, country of origin, place of birth or nationality unless legally required, including in benefit eligibility determinations.
- **FPD is covered, not carved out.** These restrictions apply to any "City employee," which includes Frederick Police Department officers. The scope limiter is purpose, not department: the restrictions bite only when the activity is for or in furtherance of an immigration enforcement operation, so FPD's ordinary criminal law enforcement work is unaffected because it isn't immigration enforcement to begin with, not because FPD is exempt.
- **Implementation duties (§33-7, §33-8, §33-12).**
  - The Legal Department issues staff guidance, including on the difference between judicial warrants and civil detainers.
  - The Mayor ensures each department director designates a supervisor liaison, identifies and signs non-public areas per §33-12 (a filed written inventory, signage at points of ingress and public/non-public transitions, with prescribed sign text), and sets any additional department-specific measures.
  - The Mayor may issue further regulations to carry out the ordinance's purposes.
- **Reporting (§33-11).** Department directors report incidents to the Legal Department within 30 days of each quarter's close. The Legal Department consolidates those into a quarterly report to Council within 45 days of quarter's close, posted publicly on submission, plus an annual report by March 31 covering patterns, protocol effectiveness, guidance issued, and recommended amendments. Identifying information about individuals or employees involved in an incident is redacted before submission. The first required report, for Q2 2026, reported no incidents (see Reporting record, below).
- **Cost recovery (§33-13).** The Mayor or designee may identify, document, and seek reimbursement from DHS, ICE and CBP for City costs arising from federal immigration enforcement activity within the City: personnel and overtime, public safety response, administrative and legal costs, detention/transportation/facility-use costs, and community-mitigation costs such as outreach and translation. This is enacted law, part of G-26-1 itself. The March 20 draft that first proposed it as a standalone amendment ([[Proposed City Values Act Amendment]]) was folded in before passage and is archived as a superseded source, not live legislation.
- **Safeguards (§33-9, §33-10).**
  - A federal-law compliance clause (§33-9) preserves information-sharing that federal or constitutional law requires.
  - Employee violations bring internal discipline only (§33-10): no criminal penalties, no citation or civil fine, no private right of action.
  - Definitions (§33-3) use ACLU-aligned terms such as "immigration enforcement operation" and "law enforcement officer."

The Equity Impact Statement (Lakisha Wade) supported the ordinance and named training, communication and legal-boundary clarity as implementation priorities. No fiscal impact was listed ([[Hearing Brief 2026-03-19]]).

The enacted text tracks the March 19 as-introduced provision list almost exactly; the one substantive addition between introduction and passage is §33-13, cost recovery.

## How it got here

- **February 16 draft.** The earliest draft framed the ordinance as "an administrative and procedural ordinance, not a sanctuary declaration" ([[City Values Act -- Legislative Development Note 2026-02-16]]). It included several elements that don't appear in the enacted text:
  - a centralized ICE-interaction log with required fields
  - a single annual public report due March 1
  - a translated plain-language public summary
  - a requirement that FPD not detain anyone solely on an ICE civil detainer

  It also set aside a "second layer" for later: data privacy limits, community notification, funding non-cooperation clauses, charter-level protection, and civilian oversight. None of these four discarded elements reappear in the enacted text; the closest surviving equivalent to the civil-detainer rule is the general judicial-warrant standard in §33-4.
- **March 5.** Council President Díaz's public statement on ICE activity near Hillcrest Elementary, about 200 yards from the school while children were walking to class, entered the official record. It became the direct context for introduction ([[Hearing Brief 2026-03-19]]). The Frederick News-Post's March 13 story on the ordinance also cited the incident ([[ICE Raid Press Coverage Brief -- 2026-09-09]]).
- **March 12.** Work session ([[topic_city_values_act]]).
- **March 19.** Introduction (First Read). Council Member Benford's surveillance-technology and FPD tracking/reporting provisions were not taken up at that meeting ([[Hearing Brief 2026-03-19]]). A drafting issue recorded in daily notes as the "CVA PP fix" was still unresolved as of March 20 ([[Immigration Policy Brief -- 2026-03-22]]); what it was and whether it was resolved isn't documented anywhere in the vault, including the enacted text.
- **March 20.** City staff drafted a cost-recovery amendment ([[Proposed City Values Act Amendment]]). Its categories match §33-13 of the enacted ordinance almost exactly. It was folded into G-26-1 before the April 2 vote rather than introduced as a standalone amendment; the note is archived as the historical draft it turned out to be, not tracked as pending legislation.
- **April 2.** Passed 7-0 ([[City Values Act -- Enacted Ordinance G-26-1]]). Díaz described the public hearing as one of the most significant moments of his term: City Hall was packed, and one speaker raised concerns while the rest of the room was supportive ([[Behind the Spires 41 - Cesar Diaz Interview]]). Council Member Shackelford voted yes after earlier public silence ([[Cesar Context -- Council]]).
- **April 6.** Signed by Mayor O'Connor and certified for legal sufficiency by City Attorney Rachel Gray ([[City Values Act -- Enacted Ordinance G-26-1]]).

## Stated purpose

Díaz has framed the ordinance around two problems ([[Behind the Spires 41 - Cesar Diaz Interview]]):
- **Operational.** Staff needed a clear, codified protocol for when enforcement shows up at a City facility: call a supervisor, request a warrant, and manage the encounter through the chain of command.
- **Perception.** Residents were avoiding pools, parks, recreation centers and in-person applications for City benefits out of fear.

The written rationale is service integrity. City services work only if all residents trust them enough to use them ([[topic_city_values_act]]). The 2026 State of the City speech summarized it as keeping immigration enforcement "out of the private areas of our buildings and off City property without a warrant signed by a judge," and City employees not asking "where you were born unless the law requires it" ([[State of the City Speech 2026]]).

The office's communications practice avoids "sanctuary city" and uses the ordinance's own language ([[topic_city_values_act]]).

## Applied: the Taskers Chance ICE operation

Full incident account and the wider enforcement picture: [[ICE Enforcement in Maryland and Frederick]].

ICE staked out the Taskers Chance neighborhood from September 3 to 7, 2026, and the operation ended in a physical confrontation with community observers on September 5. The Mayor and Council President issued a joint statement quoting the CVA's inquiry clause, "A City employee will make an inquiry only if required by law to do so." The statement confirmed that City employees and police officers would not ask residents about status unless legally required. Council has said it will review the incident ([[ICE Raid Press Coverage Brief -- 2026-09-09]]). Note: this September operation falls in Q3 2026, after the Q2 report described above, which reported no activity for the prior quarter.

## What it does not reach

- **Encounters outside covered spaces.** The CVA is warrant-based. Like the Maryland and Baltimore school-access laws, it creates legal exposure and a path to remedy afterward, but it does not physically prevent an encounter in a parking lot, sidewalk or drop-off zone. In Baltimore, agents made an arrest in a school parking lot despite a warrant requirement for school property ([[Warrant-Based ICE Restrictions Create Legal Exposure Not Prevention]]). That note argues public messaging should describe exposure and remedy, not prevention, and that know-your-rights material should cover public-adjacent spaces.
- **Data acquired outside City channels.** The CVA protects City-held data and staff conduct. It does not reach federal purchases of commercial data or federal database consolidation ([[Municipal Sovereignty Against Federal Enforcement Infrastructure -- Data, Land, and Technology as the Three Fronts]]).
- **Federal land.** It offers no tool against federal acquisition of private property for detention. The available local tool is a zoning restriction on privately operated detention facilities, which Frederick doesn't currently have (same source).
- **Technology procurement.** It doesn't govern surveillance systems that departments procure or that federal agencies access through data-sharing, such as ALPR networks (same source; see [[Flock ALPR]]).
- **The Sheriff.** The CVA governs City agencies only. The Frederick County Sheriff is an independently elected officer the City cannot direct ([[Community Trust Act -- CTA Sheriffs Lawsuit Synthesis]]).

## State and federal environment

Full treatment of the state law, its companion laws, and the litigation against it: [[Maryland Community Trust Act]].

Maryland built a layered framework during 2026 ([[Community Trust Act -- CTA Sheriffs Lawsuit Synthesis]], [[Maryland Community Trust Act -- Governor Allows Law Without Signature]]):
- the 287(g) ban
- the Community Trust Act, a judicial-warrant requirement that became law without the Governor's signature in late May
- the Data Privacy Act (HB 711), which restricts data sales for immigration enforcement

Seventeen sheriffs, including Frederick County's, sued to block the Community Trust Act (Gahler v. Moore, No. 8:26-cv-02057). DOJ separately sued Maryland on Supremacy Clause grounds ([[DOJ Files Suit Against Maryland Over Community Trust Act Claiming Supremacy Clause Preemption]]). Montgomery County has layered its own County Values Act on the state law ([[County-Level Trust Acts Proliferate as Community Trust Act Faces DOJ Preemption Challenge]]).

`confidence: low`, sources disagree on what a DOJ win would mean for the CVA. This is a question about the Community Trust Act's litigation, not about the CVA's own enacted text, so it is unaffected by the ordinance now in the vault:
- One note holds that the CVA "passed under local authority, not state law" and would remain in place if the Community Trust Act were enjoined ([[DOJ Files Suit Against Maryland Over Community Trust Act Claiming Supremacy Clause Preemption]]).
- Two others argue that the preemption reasoning "would likely extend" to the CVA because it uses the same warrant-based structure ([[County-Level Trust Acts Proliferate as Community Trust Act Faces DOJ Preemption Challenge]], [[Immigration Enforcement Chilling Effect Compounds Across Schools Childcare and Legal Protections]]).
- A review by City Attorney Rachel Gray is listed as an open action in three notes, and none records the answer.
