# ICE Enforcement at Schools Shows Limits of State and Local Sensitive-Location Restrictions

## Summary
On June 11, ICE agents followed a car into the parking lot of Commodore John Rodgers Elementary/Middle School in Baltimore, pulled the driver from the vehicle, and detained both parents in front of their two daughters. A federal judge later ordered the man released, ruling agents lacked probable cause and calling the incident a "stomach churner." Maryland and Baltimore both have laws meant to keep ICE away from schools, and neither one prevented the arrest.

## Key Points
More than a dozen states have passed laws restricting ICE presence at schools since the Trump administration reversed the 2011 "sensitive locations" policy in 2025. But these laws are structurally limited: they mostly restrict what state, local, and school officials can do to cooperate with or grant access to ICE, usually by requiring a judicial warrant before agents can enter school property. They do not restrict what federal agents can do in a school parking lot or on a public street adjacent to campus, which is exactly where the Baltimore arrest happened. At least 17 ICE encounters on school grounds have been tracked nationally since the policy reversal.

The politics cut both ways. Indiana passed a law this year barring school districts from restricting cooperation with ICE at all, and its attorney general sued Indianapolis Public Schools for resisting. Meanwhile the termination of Temporary Protected Status for more than 330,000 Haitians is expected to increase ICE activity near schools with large Haitian populations this coming school year, with Ohio districts specifically flagged as bracing for it.

## Relevance
This is the practical companion to [[Howard County Immigration Facility Case Shows Limits of Local Permitting Power Against ICE Infrastructure]]. Both show the same pattern: a warrant requirement and a "no cooperation" posture slow ICE down and create legal exposure for agents who overstep, but they do not prevent an encounter from happening. The City Values Act is built on the same warrant-based structure as the Maryland and Baltimore school laws referenced here. Worth flagging to the Frederick Rapid Response Network and to Centro Hispano as a real-world caution: know-your-rights guidance needs to cover parking lots and drop-off zones, not just the interior of a building, and needs to be clear that a warrant requirement protects against unlawful access, not against an agent encountering someone in a public or semi-public space nearby.
