# Renee Good Family Lawsuits Test Federal Immunity for ICE Shootings

## Summary
The family of Renee Good, a U.S. citizen killed by an ICE officer in Minneapolis in January 2026, filed twin federal suits: an FTCA wrongful-death claim against the United States and a Ku Klux Klan Act conspiracy claim naming Stephen Miller, Tom Homan, and Kristi Noem. The case tests whether senior officials can be held liable for field-level enforcement violence.

## Key Points
- **The shooting.** January 7, 2026, during "Operation Metro Surge" in Minneapolis, ICE officer Jonathan Ross fired three shots into Renee Good's car as she drove away; one struck her head. Good was 37, a mother of three, a U.S. citizen with no criminal record. The administration called her a "domestic terrorist"; the suit says video shows her steering away and using de-escalating language. Ross was not indicted or fired and remains an active ICE officer.
- **Context.** Days after the filing, plainclothes ICE officers in New York City fired into a car within seconds of surrounding it, wounding Oscar Belgal, 28, with his 5-year-old son in the back seat.
- **Suit 1: Federal Tort Claims Act.** United States as sole defendant; battery, assault, false imprisonment, negligence, intentional infliction of emotional distress causing wrongful death. The FTCA is Congress's waiver of sovereign immunity for torts by federal employees acting within the scope of employment. Focus is the mechanics of the shooting.
- **Suit 2: Ku Klux Klan Act of 1871, 42 U.S.C. 1985(3) and 1986.** Alleges a conspiracy to violate civil rights, naming Ross plus Stephen Miller, Tom Homan, and former DHS Secretary Kristi Noem. Alleges coordination with private actors: far-right online provocateurs whose claims about the Somali community prompted Metro Surge, and technology companies whose tools targeted neighborhoods and tracked observers and protesters. Section 1986 covers failure to prevent the conspiracy. The theory is that Good's death was the "predictable result" of a campaign driven by racial animus against Hispanic and Somali communities, with officers signaled they would face no consequences.
- **Why this route.** Sections 1985 and 1986 sidestep Bivens, which the Supreme Court has sharply narrowed as a path to sue federal officers for constitutional violations.
- **Stakes.** If it proceeds, it could set precedent for holding top officials liable for ICE field conduct. If dismissed, the remaining fix is congressional: a statutory cause of action against federal officers parallel to Section 1983 for local police.
- Becca Good, Renee's partner: "The whole world had to watch as our entire universe was destroyed. We were all robbed."

## Relevance
The accountability gap between local police (suable under 1983) and federal agents (largely immune) is the core asymmetry that makes local non-cooperation policy like the City Values Act matter: a city cannot discipline or be sued over ICE conduct, so limiting entanglement is the lever it has. The private-tech conspiracy allegation also connects to the ICE surveillance and data work in [[Effects of Immigration Enforcement]] and to Frederick's own surveillance procurement questions.
