# Immigrant Entrepreneurs as a Tool for Main Street Ownership Transitions

## Summary
Immigrant entrepreneurs are positioned as an underused solution to the coming wave of small business ownership transitions, with immigrants already accounting for 21 percent of business owners and 27 percent of Main Street business owners nationally.

## Key Points
Researchers call it the Great Ownership Transfer. By 2035, roughly 6 million small and mid-sized businesses are expected to change hands as baby boomer owners retire, with more than 1 million of those viable for sale and representing about 5 trillion dollars in enterprise value. When no buyer or successor is ready, the default outcome is closure rather than sale, and a city loses jobs, tax revenue, and foot traffic along with the business itself.

Immigrants already play an outsized role in exactly this segment. The Immigration Research Initiative puts immigrants at 21 percent of all business owners and 27 percent of Main Street business owners nationally, meaning attracting immigrant entrepreneurs is less about courting a new demographic and more about recognizing where ownership transitions are already succeeding. Many immigrant entrepreneurs are not looking to build a startup from scratch. They are looking to buy an existing business with customers, equipment, and a name people already trust, which is exactly what a retiring owner needs.

The article frames this as a corridor level and neighborhood level strategy rather than a purely economic development one. Federal investor pathways (E-2, L-1, EB-5) already exist and don't require new local legislation, meaning a city's role is less about creating new programs and more about being visibly open, practical, and affordable to entrepreneurs already exploring a move.

## Relevance
Golden Mile carries over 30 underutilized commercial parcels along the corridor, per [[Golden Mile Small Area Profile]], and the corridor's engagement phase is actively underway. This article's framing, that ownership transition and vacancy problems can be addressed through deliberate immigrant entrepreneur attraction rather than waiting for a developer or a subsidy, is a useful lens for the Small Area Plan's eventual policy recommendations, separate from the housing focused pipeline data already being tracked.
