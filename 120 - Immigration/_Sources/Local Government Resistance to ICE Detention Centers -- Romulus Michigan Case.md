# Local Government Resistance to ICE Detention Centers -- Romulus Michigan Case

## Summary

In early 2026, ICE moved to open a detention facility in Romulus, Michigan by converting a warehouse near Detroit Metropolitan Airport into a facility for approximately 500 detainees. The case became a detailed example of what tools local and state governments actually have to resist federal immigration enforcement infrastructure -- and where those tools hit their ceiling.

## Key Points

The Coalition to Shut the Camps formed within weeks of ICE's intentions becoming public in mid-February 2026. It brought together immigrant rights advocates, faith leaders, environmental justice organizations, and elected officials, holding weekly Saturday demonstrations outside the proposed facility at 7525 Cogswell Street. Within a month, the coalition sent formal demand letters to five public agencies -- the Great Lakes Water Authority, Wayne County, the City of Romulus, DTE Energy, and the Michigan Department of Environment -- requiring transparency and rigorous technical and environmental review before any utilities or administrative services were extended to the facility.

The Romulus City Council unanimously passed a resolution formally opposing the facility's establishment, construction, or operation. The mayor stated he would not issue a certificate of occupancy unless ordered to do so by a federal court. Michigan Attorney General Dana Nessel sent a demand letter to ICE citing the facility's placement in a 100-500 year floodplain, proximity to schools, and a lack of transparency. She also filed a FOIA request for DHS records on the warehouse purchase and conversion plans. Both U.S. senators wrote to DHS Secretary Kristi Noem with structural safety concerns about housing detainees in a warehouse not designed for that use.

The core legal constraint: federal law preempts state and local ordinances on immigration enforcement. Attorneys working with the coalition acknowledged that withholding permits and certificates of occupancy was one of the few real tools available, and that Romulus city ordinances may not currently permit that type of use for the building. Whether those mechanisms would survive a federal court challenge remained open. The facility's location near Detroit Metropolitan Airport was a specific concern -- it would give ICE ready airline access to move detainees in and out.

## Why This Matters for César

Frederick has not faced an analogous situation, but the Romulus case is a useful precedent study for the question of what local governments can actually do when federal immigration enforcement conflicts with local values. The tools used here -- resolutions of opposition, withholding certificates of occupancy, demanding environmental and technical review through regulatory bodies, multi-sector coalition-building -- are all potentially replicable at the local level.

This is also useful context for [[Municipal ID]] and the [[City Values Act]], both of which position Frederick as a welcoming city. Understanding the real limits of local authority on federal immigration enforcement matters is part of being credible on this issue. The coalition model -- rapid formation, multi-sector, sustained public presence -- is also worth noting for future organizing in partnership with [[Centro Hispano de Frederick]] or similar organizations.
