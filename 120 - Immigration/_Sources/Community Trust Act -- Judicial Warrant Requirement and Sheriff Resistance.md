# Community Trust Act -- Judicial Warrant Requirement and Sheriff Resistance

## Summary

The Community Trust Act (SB 791), passed in the final hours of the 2026 Maryland legislative session, prohibits local and state police from detaining individuals for ICE except in narrow circumstances, and requires federal officials to present a judicial warrant rather than an administrative detainer. As of May 18, 2026, Governor Moore had not yet signed it. Several sheriffs are consulting attorneys about legal challenges, framing the bill as forcing a conflict between federal and state law.

## Key Points

The bill's narrow exceptions for honoring ICE requests cover individuals convicted of felonies in the United States, registered sex offenders, people who served 12 to 18 months in a state prison, or people who committed offenses in another state and served at least five years in prison. Outside those categories, local law enforcement cannot hold someone based on immigration status or an ICE administrative detainer.

The judicial warrant requirement is the core enforcement mechanism. Administrative ICE detainers are requests, not legal compulsion -- the Community Trust Act closes the loophole that let local jails treat them as binding. This is the same principle the City Values Act applies to city facilities.

Harford County Sheriff Gahler posted a public video urging Moore to veto the bill, calling it a direct assault on public safety and arguing it conflicts with the federal constitutional oath. Washington County Sheriff Albert said his department has few people with ICE detainers currently, but confirmed they are consulting legal counsel.

Supporters including We Are CASA's Cathryn Jackson framed the bill's signing as career-defining for Moore and part of an essential legacy response to federal immigration pressure.

One additional pending bill: the Data Privacy Act (HB 711), which would prohibit selling personal data for immigration enforcement purposes.

## Relevance

The Community Trust Act is the structural complement to the 287(g) ban. The 287(g) ban stops formal program participation; the Community Trust Act addresses the informal compliance mechanism -- holding people on administrative detainers -- that could persist even after 287(g) termination. Together they represent the most comprehensive state-level limit on local-federal immigration enforcement cooperation yet attempted in Maryland.

Governor Moore's decision on the Community Trust Act before or on May 26 is the key watch item. If he signs, Frederick County law enforcement will be operating under the same judicial warrant standard the City Values Act established for city facilities. That alignment is worth naming publicly when the opportunity arises.

The sheriff legal challenge possibility is real but likely weak -- state law limiting local law enforcement cooperation with federal immigration authorities has survived constitutional scrutiny in other jurisdictions.
