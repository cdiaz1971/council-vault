# We Are Here Because America Was There

## Summary
Lao American organizer Vimala Phongsavanh argues refugee communities exist because of US foreign policy, not American generosity, and that aggregated AAPI data hides refugee poverty behind the model minority myth. Disaggregation is how policymakers see who needs resources.

## Key Points
The essay is part of Next City's "Not My Narrative" series, which takes apart myths used to justify neglect and the status quo. This installment targets the American origin story.

Phongsavanh grew up in the Fairmount neighborhood of Woonsocket, Rhode Island, a working-class immigrant neighborhood where Lao American families have lived for about 45 years. Her parents worked long factory shifts at minimum wage. She studied American history through college without ever encountering her own family's history, and believed into her early 20s that her family's story belonged to Laos rather than to the United States.

Her family came because of the CIA-backed Secret War. From 1964 to 1973 the US ran a covert bombing campaign in Laos, dropping more than two million tons of bombs, hidden from much of the public and from Congress. Laos became the most heavily bombed country per capita in history. Villages disappeared, farmland became unusable, and hundreds of thousands were displaced into refugee camps. Some, including her family, reached Rhode Island through the Refugee Act of 1980.

The consequences continue. Southeast Asian families who arrived as refugees and spent decades building families, businesses, and communities now face detention and deportation, fighting again for the right to remain.

She turned this history into an exhibit, The Heart of Wattayai, shown earlier in 2026 at Woonsocket's Museum of Work and Culture. It ties the Secret War, resettlement, mill labor, detention and deportation, and Woonsocket into one American story, centered on her grandmother.

Her core argument is that omitting these histories lets people frame refugee migration as pursuit of the American Dream or as government generosity, and treat immigrants as outsiders arriving rather than as people already shaped by American intervention abroad. That omission props up the model minority myth, the idea that Asian Americans are uniformly successful and need no public investment. The myth only works when distinct communities are collapsed into a single "Asian" category.

That is why she advocates data disaggregation. Aggregated data says Asian Americans are doing fine. Disaggregated data shows refugee communities with higher poverty, lower educational attainment, more limited English proficiency, and worse health outcomes. Without it, policymakers literally cannot see the need.

Her closing claim is about obligation. If refugee communities exist because of national decisions, their struggles are a shared civic responsibility, not someone else's story.

Author context: Phongsavanh is Managing Director of Coalition Mobilization at National CAPACD, a former elected member and chair of the Woonsocket School Committee, and Board Chair of the Laotian American National Alliance.

## Relevance
The disaggregation argument applies directly to Frederick. César leans on ALICE and TPMA data for housing and affordability work, and both report Asian residents as a single group, which can hide exactly the pattern this essay describes among refugee and recently arrived households. It is a concrete ask worth raising with Lakisha Wade on equity data, and worth considering for any data standard in the Affordable Housing Navigator or production dashboard Resolution 26-15 created.

The "we are here because America was there" framing is a strong public-communications line for City Values Act and immigrant-protection work, and pairs naturally with AACF, one of César's community partners. It also connects to the deportation exposure of long-settled refugee families, which sits alongside the Community Trust Act litigation and the record-low refugee admissions already in this folder. Phongsavanh's path from organizer to elected school board member is close to César's own.
