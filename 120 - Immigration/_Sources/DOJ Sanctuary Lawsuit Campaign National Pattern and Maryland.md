# DOJ Sanctuary Lawsuit Campaign: National Pattern and Maryland

## Summary

The Trump DOJ has filed 27 lawsuits against more than 20 sanctuary jurisdictions since January 2025, using Supremacy Clause arguments to block state and local policies that restrict ICE cooperation. Maryland is among the latest targets. Court results are mixed: a Trump-appointed judge blocked Philadelphia's agent-identification-masking policy, while a Biden-appointed judge tossed the New Jersey suits. The constitutional argument from sanctuary advocates is that the Supremacy Clause does not compel states to enforce federal immigration law -- it only prevents states from obstructing it.

## Key Points

**The scale of the campaign.** 27 lawsuits since January 2025 against more than 20 jurisdictions. Targets include Connecticut, Maryland, New Jersey, New York, a Michigan county, and Philadelphia. The pace has accelerated: at least half a dozen suits filed in the last three months alone. The National Immigration Law Center calls it "unprecedented."

**The legal theory on each side.** DOJ argues sanctuary laws violate the Supremacy Clause by obstructing lawful federal enforcement. Sanctuary defenders argue the Supremacy Clause prevents states from passing conflicting laws, but does not create an affirmative duty for states to assist federal enforcement. The Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering doctrine (from Printz v. United States) supports the sanctuary position: the federal government cannot compel state officers to implement federal programs.

**The Maryland suit.** Filed July 10, targeting the Community Trust Act. Maryland's law prohibits state and local correctional officers and law enforcement from inquiring about immigration status or detaining/transferring individuals to federal authorities without a warrant. The DOJ suit and the sheriffs' lawsuit (Gahler v. Moore) use nearly identical language in some sections -- coordinated strategy between federal DOJ and state-level plaintiffs.

**Philadelphia as a test case.** A Trump-appointed district judge issued a preliminary injunction blocking Philadelphia's policy barring ICE agents from wearing masks or using unmarked vehicles. The judge cited the Supremacy Clause. Six of the seven Philadelphia ordinances took effect; only the agent-identification policy is enjoined. The council member who sponsored the package framed this as a win: "We've got six laws on the books."

**The New Jersey counter-example.** A Biden-appointed judge dismissed the four New Jersey city suits, ruling the cities' policies did not impede ICE activities given existing state sanctuary law. Outcome depends heavily on which judge gets the case.

**The constitutional stress test.** These cases are establishing the boundary between state anti-commandeering rights and federal Supremacy Clause authority in real time. The precedents being set now will govern how much room state and local governments have to protect their communities from federal immigration enforcement for the foreseeable future.

## Relevance

This is the national context for the Community Trust Act injunction risk flagged in Active Threads. The Maryland suit is part of a deliberate coordinated campaign, not an isolated case. The Philadelphia outcome -- six of seven ordinances intact despite an injunction on one -- is an important template: a legal setback on one provision does not invalidate the broader protective architecture.

For Frederick: the City Values Act operates under local authority and does not depend on state law, which means it survives a CTA injunction. But the DOJ lawsuit campaign now establishes that even local ordinances restricting ICE cooperation (masking, unmarked vehicles, identity concealment) are litigation targets. Any future Frederick ordinances strengthening the CVA need to be designed with this litigation environment in mind.

Coalition partners need to understand the national pattern, not just the Maryland case. The briefing for Centro Hispano, SSCM, AACF, and Mobilize Frederick should frame the DOJ campaign as a coordinated national strategy, not a targeted response to Maryland specifically.
