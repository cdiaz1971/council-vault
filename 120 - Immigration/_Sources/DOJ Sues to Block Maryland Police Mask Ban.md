# DOJ Sues to Block Maryland Police Mask Ban

## Summary

On October 1, 2026, the day SB 1 took effect, DOJ sued Maryland, Prince George's County, and Montgomery County to block laws barring officers, including federal agents, from wearing masks on duty. Maryland's own Attorney General had warned applying the ban to federal officers carried significant Supremacy Clause risk.

## Key Points

SB 1, sponsored by Sen. Malcolm Augustine, bars local, state, and federal law enforcement from wearing face coverings on duty, with exceptions for undercover work and specific medical or safety gear. Officers must display their department, last name, and badge number. Violations carry a civil citation of up to $1,500. The Maryland Police Training and Standards Commission is charged with a uniform statewide face-covering policy.

DOJ filed in U.S. District Court in Maryland the same day the law took effect. The suit also names Prince George's County, which passed its own mask ban in April, and Montgomery County, whose Unmask ICE Act passed unanimously the same month, plus the Training and Standards Commission. DOJ's core argument is intergovernmental immunity: a state cannot directly regulate how federal officers perform federal duties.

DOJ leans heavily on Maryland's own lawyers. Attorney General Anthony Brown's pre-signing letter called the law legally sufficient overall but said applying it to federal officers posed significant Supremacy Clause risk, with a high likelihood a court would strike that application. County legal officers raised similar concerns. DOJ also cites other courts that have enjoined similar state and local laws. Sponsors and Senate President Ferguson say they anticipated the challenge and expect the law to hold.

## Relevance

This is the second DOJ preemption suit against Maryland's immigration-response laws, after the July challenge to the Community Trust Act. The pattern matters for Frederick: any local measure that tries to regulate federal agents directly is likely to draw the same suit, and the intergovernmental immunity argument is strongest exactly where a law reaches federal officers.

The useful distinction for Frederick is between regulating federal agents, which is legally exposed, and governing the City's own officers and resources, which is well within local authority and is how the City Values Act is built. If calls for a Frederick unmask ordinance come up after the Tasker's Chance confrontation, the defensible version applies to Frederick Police and City personnel, while the federal piece is left to the state law and its litigation.

Worth watching: whether a court enjoins only the federal application and leaves the requirements for state and local police intact. That outcome would still bind Frederick Police.
