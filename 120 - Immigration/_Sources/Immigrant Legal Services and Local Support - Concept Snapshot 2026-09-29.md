# Immigrant Legal Services and Local Support

Enforcement rules (see [[City Values Act]] and [[Maryland Community Trust Act]]) govern what government may do. This page covers what local government and its partners can build: legal help, advisory channels, trusted information, and economic footholds.

## Frederick's existing infrastructure

- **Local legal providers.**
  - The Spanish Speaking Community of Maryland (SSCM), founded 1971, is accredited by the U.S. Department of Justice to provide immigration legal services and has a Frederick office at 329 S. Jefferson Street ([[Spanish Speaking Community of Maryland]]).
  - Centro Hispano de Frederick's board includes an immigration attorney ([[Centro Hispano de Frederick]]).
  - In March 2026, Centro Hispano reported sharply rising demand for its services, while the County Immigrant Affairs Commission's chair reported a drop of more than 50% in people seeking services starting June 2025 ([[Immigration Enforcement -- Frederick County WYPR]]).
- **County advisory body.** Frederick **County** has a standing Immigrant Affairs Commission, chaired by Claudia Hernandez, that meets at Winchester Hall. In 2026 its work included Immigrant Heritage Month storytelling, revising the County resource guide after public comment that it didn't serve undocumented residents, and advocating for a full-time County Immigrant Liaison position that County Executive Fitzwater proposed ([[IAC Brief 2026-05-27]]). The **City** has no equivalent standing body.
- **Rapid response.** The Frederick Rapid Response Network organized in 2025 with a leadership team, tiered notification protocols, and canvassing capacity, and was building toward accompaniment, mutual aid, and training. It treated its response at Katherine Drexel as a positive case study ([[Immigration Policy Brief -- 2026-03-22]]). The network's functions have since been absorbed into other organizations ([[Maryland ICE Warrantless Arrest Lawsuit]]). For its role during enforcement incidents, see [[ICE Enforcement in Maryland and Frederick]].
- **Municipal ID.** It's pre-legislative, with no draft, and is being reframed as a financial-inclusion and affordability tool rather than only an immigration protection ([[Legislative Roadmap FY2027]]).

## Funding immigrant legal representation

- **Why it matters.** Without a lawyer, fewer than 10% of immigrant children win the right to remain while their cases proceed ([[Federal Contract Collapse Leaves Unaccompanied Immigrant Children Without Legal Counsel]]).
- **The legal footing: Harris County, Texas.** Texas AG Ken Paxton sued in November 2025, calling Harris County's funding of immigrant legal-aid nonprofits an unconstitutional gift of public funds. He brought a parallel suit against Bexar County. Both trial courts rejected the suits. On appeal, Texas's 15th Court of Appeals, an all-Republican court created in 2023 for state-government cases, ruled against Paxton for lack of any proof of harm. The same court had sided with Paxton against San Antonio's abortion-travel fund, so it found a public purpose in immigrant legal aid that it didn't find there. The program ran throughout the litigation ([[Local Government Authority to Fund Nonprofit Immigrant Legal Services (Harris County)]]).
- **An operational model: Portland, Oregon.** On February 27, 2026, the council advanced **$150,000** from its roughly $850,000 Legal Priorities Reserve Fund to SOAR Immigration Legal Services, covering about 75 immigrants and refugees. The recipient was recommended by the city's standing New Portlanders Policy Commission, after Councilor Elana Pirtle-Guiney asked it for practical, low-cost steps. The vote exposed tensions:
  - Councilor Candace Avalos wanted broader consultation and resources for the whole rapid-response network, not one nonprofit.
  - Councilor Angelita Morillo, the only immigrant on the council, questioned favoring a single nonprofit.
  - An emergency-ordinance route failed for lack of nine votes.
  
  ([[Portland 150K Immigrant Legal Services Appropriation -- Model and Internal Tensions]]; [[Portland Advances $150K in City Funds for Immigrant Legal Services]]; *the two notes clip the same OPB article*)
- **The shared structure.** Government funds a nonprofit intermediary, the nonprofit delivers the representation, and the public benefit of legal access for people in proceedings is the legitimate public purpose ([[Immigrant Legal Services -- Peer City Funding Models and Frederick Options]]).
- **The 2026 federal collapse.**
  - **The lapse.** The federal legal-services contract with the Acacia Center for Justice lapsed July 31, 2026. Acacia coordinated about 100 providers, including Baltimore- and DC-based Amica. More than 20,000 unaccompanied children risk losing counsel, and children entering custody since then have largely gone without attorneys.
  - **The replacement.** HHS first chose a small Texas firm, which withdrew. It then awarded a $158M no-bid contract (August 7, 2026 to February 6, 2027) to Our Rescue, formerly Operation Underground Railroad, which has no apparent immigration-representation history.
  - **The withheld funding.** HHS had withheld $65M in appropriated legal-representation funds since December 2025, after Acacia's attorneys refused to hand over sensitive case data.
  
  ([[Federal Contract Collapse Leaves Unaccompanied Immigrant Children Without Legal Counsel]])
- **Maryland's response.** Amica's Children's Program is monitoring 700+ unaccompanied-child cases. The University of Maryland's Chacón Center is building a referral, training, and pro bono network, and plans to screen Maryland's roughly 3,800 children in foster care for unaddressed immigration status. Providers report immigration judges under pressure to expedite cases, pushing children with pending Special Immigrant Juvenile Status toward voluntary departure ([[Maryland Legal Aid Groups Build Referral Network to Fill Federal Immigration Defense Gap]]).

## Standing advisory bodies

Portland's New Portlanders Policy Commission is a standing volunteer body that advises the council on how city policy affects immigrant and refugee residents. It shows the governance model behind the Portland appropriation. It's cheap to create (a resolution, a mandate, an appointment process), and it gives the council a community-grounded recommendation to act on rather than individual judgment ([[Standing Immigrant Advisory Commission as Low-Cost Governance Infrastructure]]).

## Trusted information

Four nonprofit newsrooms formed the Immigrant News Coalition: Conecta Arizona, El Tímpano (Bay Area), Documented (New York), and Sahan Journal (Minneapolis). Their model has three parts: publishing in multiple languages across platforms, giving actionable information ("what does this mean for your life?") rather than explaining communities to outsiders, and running disinformation-defense workshops. El Tímpano's Madeleine Bair: "When people don't have a place to go for trusted information, that's when fear sets in" ([[Immigrant News Coalition and the Disinformation Defense Function]]; [[Immigrant News Coalition -- Multilingual Journalism as Community Infrastructure in ICE Era]]; *both notes cover the same Next City podcast*).

## Organizing through culture

During the 2026 World Cup, the Miami collective Artists for Artists: MIA joined the "No ICE in the Cup" campaign. Its posters, drawn in a vintage tourism-postcard style, carried know-your-rights information and hotlines to fans drawn by diaspora loyalty, not advocacy. This came after DHS said ICE would be "out there everyday" at World Cup events and more than 120 organizations issued a travel advisory ([[Art as Immigration Organizing Infrastructure During the 2026 World Cup]]).

## Economic inclusion

- **The "Great Ownership Transfer."** About 6 million small and mid-sized businesses are expected to change hands by 2035 as owners retire. More than 1 million of those are viable for sale, worth about $5 trillion. Immigrants are 21% of U.S. business owners and 27% of Main Street business owners, and many want to buy an existing business rather than start one ([[Immigrant Entrepreneurs as a Tool for Main Street Ownership Transitions]]).
- **Diaspora networks as infrastructure.** Schenectady deliberately recruited Guyanese-American families from New York City, and later research connected that migration to higher homeownership and neighborhood revival. Parma, Ohio formally recognizes its Ukrainian and Polish Villages. The author recommends that cities inventory diaspora networks and immigrant-serving organizations as seriously as land and utilities ([[Cities Recruit Immigrant Entrepreneurs by Treating Diaspora Networks as Economic Infrastructure]]; *same article as the preceding note*).
- **Frederick's Golden Mile.** The corridor hosts 555 businesses, about 50 restaurants and bakeries, and 17 international grocers spanning more than 20 cuisines ([[Golden Mile Small Area Profile]]).
- **Cities as practical integrators.** Former Bristol mayor Marvin Rees argues that national governments treat migration as a political threat while cities manage it as an operational reality. European examples:
  - Turin's labor-mobility pilot with Uganda
  - Barcelona's bundle of career guidance, housing, legal aid, and language classes
  - Andalusian partnerships channeling Moroccan workers into renewable-energy jobs
  - Milan's green-jobs upskilling

  Rees's Bristol maxim: "If people are going to live in your city, the smartest thing you can do is help them succeed" ([[Cities as the Pragmatic Immigration Policy Actors When National Governments Fail]]).
