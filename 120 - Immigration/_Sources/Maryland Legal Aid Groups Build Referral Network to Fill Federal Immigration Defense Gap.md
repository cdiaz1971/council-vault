# Maryland Legal Aid Groups Build Referral Network to Fill Federal Immigration Defense Gap

## Summary

With federal funding for unaccompanied-minor legal representation gone, Maryland groups including the Amica Center and the University of Maryland's Chacón Center for Immigration Justice are building their own referral and pro bono networks rather than waiting for a federal fix, while due-process protections erode inside immigration courts.

## Key Points

- An August 2026 federal court filing (N.D. Cal.) describes an unaccompanied child appearing alone before an immigration judge in Los Angeles and requesting voluntary departure without understanding their rights. HHS's designated temporary provider, the U.S. Committee for Refugees and Immigrants (USCRI), had an attorney in the room who said nothing; a different, unaffiliated attorney intervened as friend-of-the-court and the judge granted a continuance. Amica and three other providers allege in the filing that USCRI attorneys frequently fail to show up, arrive late, or stay silent at critical moments.
- Amica's Children's Program (managing attorney Scott Bassett) is currently monitoring more than 700 unaccompanied-child cases. Smaller nonprofits nationally have had to cut back assistance or close.
- Chacón Center Program Director Francisco Flores says the durable fix needs to come from Congress, but in the meantime the center is building a referral and training network and recruiting pro bono attorneys. A new initiative aims to screen Maryland's roughly 3,800 abused, neglected, or abandoned children in foster care to identify unaccompanied immigrants whose immigration status might otherwise go unaddressed.
- Immigration judges are under pressure to expedite cases and limit extensions, which Bassett says compromises due process -- particularly for children with pending or approved Special Immigrant Juvenile Status who are being pushed toward voluntary departure before their cases can be fully heard.
- Flores frames the work personally: born in the US to Salvadoran immigrant parents, he was drawn to immigration law after watching his family and community struggle for legal status.

## Relevance

A concrete Maryland-based model -- referral network plus foster-care screening -- worth watching alongside Frederick's own immigrant-support infrastructure and RISE Coalition ties. Also relevant precedent if Frederick ever considers funding or partnering on local immigrant legal aid; see [[Immigrant Legal Services -- Peer City Funding Models and Frederick Options]].
