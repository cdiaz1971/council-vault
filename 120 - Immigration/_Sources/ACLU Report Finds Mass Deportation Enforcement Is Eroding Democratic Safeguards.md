# ACLU Report Finds Mass Deportation Enforcement Is Eroding Democratic Safeguards

## Summary
An October 2026 ACLU report, the first comprehensive civil rights review of mass deportations, finds a 2,000-agency, 25,000-agent enforcement apparatus is being used as a mechanism for broader attacks on citizenship, legal status, and democratic accountability.

## Key Points
- The report, "76 Ways Mass Deportation Is Breaking Our Democracy," argues mass deportation functions as pretext, mechanism, and distraction for broader attacks on rights and democratic safeguards.
- More than 2,000 federal, state, and local agencies now participate in a national deportation policing force, using over 25,000 federal law enforcement agents plus state/local police deputized through the 287(g) program.
- Enforcement tactics cited: checkpoints in national forests, patrols at school bus stops, and pretextual traffic stops, with many of those stopped being citizens or legal residents.
- Alleged abuses: agents shooting, beating, and dragging people including protesters and observers; government withholding information about agent conduct; undercover surveillance of anti-ICE activists.
- Scale of resources: government access to location, travel, and biometric data, plus $240 billion in congressional funding for immigration enforcement.
- Citizenship and legal-status effects: an attempt to end birthright citizenship; roughly 6.1 million people losing legal status; a reported 75% drop in the naturalization rate compared to two years earlier; halted naturalization interviews and oath ceremonies; refugee resettlement nearly shut down except for white Afrikaners from South Africa.
- Cites Congressional Democrats' "shadow" hearings and testimony from the mother of Renee Good, who was killed by a federal agent.
- Policy recommendations: a broad path to citizenship for longtime residents and noncitizens; replacing ICE with a new Immigration Management Agency and ending mass immigrant detention; modernizing asylum and border management; "fail-safe" accountability through judicial review, a private right to sue, and ending functional immunity for agents; and congressional oversight starting with the next Congress in January.

## Relevance
National-level evidence base for the [[Effects of Immigration Enforcement]] page and useful context for César's City Values Act advocacy (UMD guest lecture, advisory panels) -- the naturalization-rate drop and 287(g) program scale are directly relevant to Maryland's own 287(g) ban (SB 245/HB 444, signed Feb. 17) and to framing local non-cooperation policy as a response to a documented national pattern rather than a one-off local concern.
