# Zoning as Resistance -- Cities Using Land Use Law to Block ICE Detention Centers

## Summary

Cities are experimenting with land use tools to resist ICE detention facility siting, but the tools are limited against federally owned properties. San Antonio voted to restrict where private detention centers can be built after concluding it lacked authority to block a federally owned facility. Howard County, Maryland revoked a building permit for a planned detention facility. The federal government purchased a Maryland warehouse for $102M and holds near-absolute authority over that property regardless of local opposition.

## Key Points

The federal government has purchased warehouses and industrial properties in at least eight states for conversion to immigration detention centers, including a $102M acquisition in Maryland, an $84M purchase in Pennsylvania, and a $70M purchase in Arizona. DHS initially provided no notification to local governments in most cases -- cities and counties learned about planned detention centers through news reports or community organizers, not federal communication.

The key legal distinction: local governments have almost no tools to block facilities on federally owned property. The federal government's sovereign authority over its own land effectively overrides local zoning. However, local tools do exist for privately operated facilities under contract to the federal government, because those operators typically need local permits, utility connections, and zoning approvals.

San Antonio's approach after accepting the limits of federal authority: amend the zoning code to severely restrict where private detention centers can be built within city limits. This forecloses future private-operator expansion even if it cannot undo the federally owned facility already planned.

Howard County, Maryland took a more direct approach for a facility that had not yet been permitted: revoke the building permit before construction began. This works only in the window before federal acquisition and construction are complete.

Kansas City residents used sustained public pressure to convince a private property owner to decline to sell to the federal government -- a community organizing strategy rather than a legal one, but effective in that case.

As of June 22, 2026, DHS is reportedly planning to sell or give away seven of eleven warehouses recently acquired for detention purposes. The Maryland property is not among those planned for disposition.

The broader lesson from the Planetizen analysis: there is only so much local planners and officials can do. The tools that exist are (1) zoning restrictions on private operators, (2) permit revocation before construction, (3) utility service denial as a legal tool (used in Social Circle, GA), and (4) community organizing pressure on private sellers.

## Relevance

Maryland has a detention facility in play. Howard County's permit revocation is the closest peer-jurisdiction action. Frederick does not currently face an ICE facility siting threat, but this analysis is useful background for understanding the scope of local authority -- and its limits -- in the event one is proposed.

More broadly, this is a Layer 3 democratic survival question: local governments as physical infrastructure for either federal enforcement or community protection. The City Values Act establishes Frederick's non-cooperation posture for city staff and city property. This research establishes that zoning is the next layer of that posture for any private-operator enforcement infrastructure.

The [[Immigration - In-Progress Plans#Institutional Solidarity Pact|Institutional Solidarity Pact planning]] thread is relevant here too: a regional civic coalition coordinating responses to federal enforcement infrastructure is a more durable posture than any single city's zoning amendment.
