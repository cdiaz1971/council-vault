# Private Prison Corporations and the Deportation Industrial Complex

## Summary

Demos analysis of how private prison corporations (CoreCivic, GEO Group) profit from ICE detention expansion, use those profits to shape immigration policy through campaign finance loopholes, and thereby perpetuate a self-reinforcing "deportation-industrial complex."

## Key Points

The Demos analysis "Profit Over People" documents the structural feedback loop at the heart of immigration enforcement expansion:

1. Private prison companies (CoreCivic, GEO Group) hold federal contracts to operate ICE detention facilities.
2. As federal immigration enforcement spending surges, these contracts grow and corporate profits increase.
3. Loopholes in campaign finance law allow government contractors to channel those profits into political influence -- shaping the policies that determine enforcement levels.
4. More enforcement means more detention means more contracts means more profit means more political influence.

Demos calls this the "deportation-industrial complex" -- a term that names the structural relationship rather than treating immigration enforcement as a neutral policy choice. The analysis frames this as a democratic problem, not merely a humanitarian one: putting corporate profits ahead of due process and the public interest undermines democratic governance.

The policy ask is closing campaign finance loopholes for government contractors -- ensuring that companies profiting from federal enforcement cannot use those profits to lobby for more of the same.

## Relevance

The "deportation-industrial complex" framing is analytically useful and rhetorically powerful for council communications. It explains why enforcement expansion is self-perpetuating: it is not driven solely by ideology but by structured financial incentives that survive changes in public opinion.

For Frederick specifically: any decision to extend or expand cooperation with federal immigration enforcement -- whether through data-sharing, technology contracts, or 287(g) agreements -- is a decision to participate in a system with these profit incentives embedded in it. That's a materially different framing than "public safety partnership."

This connects to the broader Infrastructure of Democratic Survival synthesis. The private prison financing loop is one of the structural mechanisms that makes authoritarian enforcement durable even when it lacks majority support.
