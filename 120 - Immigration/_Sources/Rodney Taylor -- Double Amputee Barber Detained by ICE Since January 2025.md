# Rodney Taylor -- Double Amputee Barber Detained by ICE Since January 2025

## Summary

Rodney Taylor, a Liberian-born barber and double amputee who has lived in the US since age 2, has been held in ICE detention since January 15, 2025. He had a valid work permit and a green card in process at the time of his detention. The Georgia Parole Board pardoned his only criminal charge in 2010, but ICE used it as the basis for detention anyway. His case was the subject of a February 2026 letter from 21 members of Congress documenting denial of medical care and inadequate facilities.

## Key Points

Taylor's situation is a compound violation: a man who came to the US as a toddler, was pardoned for a teenage offense sixteen years before his detention, had active immigration documents in process, and still got picked up. ICE detained him on January 15, 2025, the day before the presidential inauguration, suggesting coordinated early enforcement targeting.

The congressional letter to ICE Acting Director Todd Lyons and former DHS Secretary Kristi Noem documented that Taylor was denied medical care multiple times and went six days without a shower because the facility lacked appropriate equipment for a double amputee. His wife Mildred Danis-Taylor spoke at the May Day 2026 rally in DC after a 12-hour bus ride from Georgia, calling his treatment "not just neglect, this is abuse."

The case also highlights the 287(g) expansion in Georgia: five jurisdictions had agreements in 2024; today there are more than 40. Danis-Taylor contrasted this directly with Maryland's new ban, calling for nationwide elimination of 287(g) agreements.

## Relevance

Taylor's case is one of the clearest examples of ICE using pardoned criminal records as detention pretexts, a pattern worth tracking as Frederick continues to work on immigrant protection policy. The medical neglect angle and the 287(g) expansion in Georgia provide concrete contrast cases for why Maryland's legislative approach matters. If César is speaking publicly about immigration enforcement, Taylor's story alongside Abrego Garcia's is a powerful two-case illustration of the human cost.
