# Dominican Voters in NY-13 Prioritize Neighborhood Conditions Over Identity Fights

## Summary
After a primary in Upper Manhattan and the Bronx dominated by fights over Dominican identity, Dominican voters interviewed ahead of the November 3 general say they care most about public safety, clean streets, housing costs, and jobs, and several want public money spent on neighborhoods rather than detention.

## Key Points
- **The district.** New York's 13th covers parts of Upper Manhattan and the Bronx and is about 28 percent Dominican. The general election is November 3, 2026, with early voting from October 24. Republican nominee is Manual Williams.
- **The primary.** In June, progressive Darializa Avila Chevalier beat incumbent Rep. Adriano Espaillat, the first Dominican-American and first formerly undocumented member of Congress, by about four points. The race turned on her past comments: a 2022 post condemning Dominican nationalism as violent and a 2018 tweet about a unified Hispaniola. Spanish-language radio amplified the controversy, and she called the accusations misinformation. Some older voters saw the comments as an attack on the flag and their faith.
- **What voters say now.** A mother in Washington Heights puts public safety first because of open drug use her daughters see and package thefts in her building, and she wants money spent on safer streets and housing rather than facilities like Alligator Alcatraz. A Marble Hill resident who voted Trump in 2024 on family and religion wants public safety and mental health services for people facing housing insecurity. A longtime Democrat wants more municipal and federal jobs. A former Espaillat supporter switched to Avila Chevalier because he felt Espaillat had drifted to foreign policy and away from local issues.
- **Cost of living crosses party lines.** One voter expects some Hispanic Trump voters to sit out rather than vote Republican again, driven by cost of living.
- **City response.** On October 20, Mayor Mamdani expanded NYC's Crisis Management System, community-based violence interrupters who also connect people to jobs and mental health help, to Washington Heights, Inwood, Harlem, and Bushwick, framed as prevention-first public safety.
- Neither candidate answered questions about local public safety funding.

## Relevance
A useful corrective for campaign and communications work with Latino immigrant voters. Identity matters to them, but what they ask of representatives is concrete: safety, cleanliness, housing, jobs. Several framed detention spending as money taken from their neighborhoods, which is a bridge between immigration and quality-of-life messaging that works even with Trump-voting Latinos. Relevant to how César talks about the CVA and affordability agenda in District 2, and to the Maryland Latino candidate wave in [[Latino Candidates Now Run Up and Down Maryland Ballots in 2026]]. Mamdani's crisis management expansion is a prevention-first public safety model worth watching.
