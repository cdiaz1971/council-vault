# 2030 Census Proposal to Exclude Noncitizens and Drop Race Data

## Summary

The Census Bureau's September 2026 proposal for the 2030 Census would drop millions of lawfully present noncitizens from apportionment counts, eliminate the race and ethnicity question, and tie usual residence to tax records. Demos argues the package would shift House seats away from immigrant-heavy areas, gut civil rights enforcement data, and depress participation in exactly the communities Frederick serves.

## Key Points

The proposal was published in the Federal Register on September 10, 2026 (document 2026-18481, "Decennial Census of the Population of Americans: Proposed Residence Criteria and Proposed Regulations"). Demos filed a public comment opposing it on October 28, 2026. Demos groups the changes into three parts, and notes these are only three of many in a sweeping package.

**Who gets counted.** The Bureau would exclude a wide set of noncitizens from the population used to apportion House seats, including work-permit holders, international students, H-1B holders, refugees, asylees, DACA recipients, and TPS holders. Because apportionment is based on total population rather than eligible voters, states and regions with larger immigrant populations would lose seats and power would be redistributed. Demos cites research on citizen-based redistricting showing these shifts fall hardest on Black, Latino, and Asian American communities and move representation toward whiter areas. It also notes the Black immigrant population has more than doubled in recent years.

**How people are counted.** The Bureau would stop asking about race and ethnicity on the decennial census and related enumeration forms, a question asked on every census since 1790. That data is the baseline for enforcing the Fair Housing Act, the Voting Rights Act, and federal employment discrimination protections, which statutes require. Without it, systemic discrimination becomes very hard to identify or prove.

**Where people are counted.** "Usual residence" would be redefined to match tax records such as returns and W-2s, rather than where a person actually lives and sleeps most of the time. This would undercount low-income residents, young children, people who move often, college students, and military personnel.

**Downstream effects.** The proposal does not directly change the counts used to distribute federal funds, but Demos argues the fear it creates will suppress immigrant participation and degrade census accuracy for everyone. Census data steers trillions in federal spending through programs like Medicaid, SNAP, highway funds, and school lunches, so gaps in the count become funding gaps for whole communities.

**Remedy.** Congress has broad constitutional authority over the census. Demos calls on Congress to legislate who must be counted and where, preserve demographic data for apportionment and redistricting, and use appropriations and oversight to fund outreach to historically undercounted communities.

## Relevance

This lands squarely on Frederick's immigrant community and on the logic behind the [[City Values Act]]. Frederick's Latino and immigrant residents are exactly the population the apportionment exclusion targets, and the participation chill compounds the effects already documented in [[Effects of Immigration Enforcement]]. An undercount in Frederick hits the City twice, through lost federal formula dollars and through weaker data for planning schools, housing, and transit.

The race and ethnicity question matters for housing work too. Fair Housing Act enforcement and any disparate-impact analysis of Frederick's housing and tenant-protection legislation depend on that baseline data.

The tax-record residence rule would also distort counts of renters, doubled-up households, and young children, the residents César's housing agenda is built around.

It connects to the congressional map fight in [[Maryland Special Session on Congressional Redistricting August 2026]], since apportionment changes would reshape how many seats Maryland has to draw in the first place. Natural partners for a local response are RISE Coalition of Western Maryland, Centro Hispano, and SSCM, each of which does census outreach in a typical cycle.
