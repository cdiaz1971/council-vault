# Federal Immigration Policy

These policies sit outside municipal authority. They set the legal status, and the precarity, of many of the residents Frederick's local protections are meant to serve. For the public-charge rule, see [[Effects of Immigration Enforcement]].

## Who is here: the unauthorized population

- **Size and makeup.** The Migration Policy Institute (MPI), using 2024 Census ACS data, estimates the unauthorized population peaked at **15.8 million by mid-2024**, the highest on record. It had held near 11 million from 2010 to 2019, and the net rise of about 5 million since 2019 works out to roughly 8% a year. Mexico's share fell from 62% in 2010 to 35%, with growth driven by Ecuadorians, Colombians, Venezuelans, Guatemalans, and Hondurans ([[Unauthorized Immigrant Population Hit 15.8 Million by Mid-2024]]).
- **Parole programs behind the rise.** Biden-era humanitarian parole for Afghans, Cubans, Haitians, Nicaraguans, Ukrainians, and Venezuelans, plus appointment-based entry through the CBP One app, covered about 1.4 million people ([[Unauthorized Immigrant Population Hit 15.8 Million by Mid-2024]]).
- **Reversal.** The administration has revoked those parole grants or is in the process of revoking them, turned CBP One into a self-deportation tool, and ended TPS for multiple nationalities, while border entries are at historic lows. MPI projects a decline from the 2024 peak "should the administration succeed in its efforts to sizably ramp up deportations and if border entries remain at their remarkably low levels." Both conditions are uncertain, and legal challenges are ongoing. People who arrived with parole or TPS, obtained work permits, and put down roots now face retroactive loss of status, which is a different situation from long-term undocumented residents ([[Trump Administration Policies Likely to Reduce Unauthorized Population From 2026 Peak]]; *the two MPI notes clip the same article*).

## Temporary Protected Status

- **The pattern.** Since January 2025 the administration has ended TPS for more than a dozen countries, most recently Haiti, Ethiopia, and Syria. The family of a 20-year-old Haitian immigrant in Ohio, an honors graduate planning to study medicine, said he died after losing TPS protection ([[Trump Administration's TPS Termination Pattern Spans More Than a Dozen Countries Since 2025]]).
- **El Salvador.** TPS for roughly 200,000 Salvadorans, in place for 25 years, was set to expire **September 9, 2026**. Democratic legislation led by Rep. Suhas Subramanyam would have extended it 18 months for more than 170,000 people, but it hadn't passed by the deadline. The administration's position is that TPS has functioned as an unintended amnesty. The DC region has the country's second-largest Salvadoran population, after Los Angeles ([[Salvadoran TPS Termination Threatens Deportation for Nation's Second-Largest Population Center]]; *same article as the preceding note*).

## Refugee admissions

The FY2026 refugee cap is **17,500**, the lowest in 25 years and well below the roughly 50,000 annual average from 2001 on. Admissions in the first ten months of FY2026 were **10,258**, versus 38,102 in FY2025 and 100,034 in FY2024. Nearly all were white South Africans, under a February 2025 executive order prioritizing Afrikaners. South African leaders dispute that Afrikaners face systemic persecution. Refugees have historically come largely from the DRC, Syria, Ukraine, Bhutan, Iraq, Afghanistan, Sudan, Venezuela, Eritrea, Myanmar, and Guatemala ([[Refugee Admissions on Track for Record Low Under Trump's Afrikaner-Prioritized Cap]]).

## Birthright citizenship

An executive order would end birthright citizenship for children of undocumented immigrants and temporary-visa holders. It is before the Supreme Court in *Trump v. Barbara*. Scot Nakagawa's essay examines what losing the 14th Amendment's birthright guarantee would mean in practice:
- **Proof of citizenship would change.** A birth certificate would prove only birth. Citizenship would require an unbroken documentary chain through one's ancestors.
- **Who would struggle to prove it:** descendants of enslaved people (slavery produced property records, not citizenship records), Native Americans (whose citizenship rests on a 1924 statute), people born at home, anyone whose name changed, adoptees, and people from the territories (whose citizenship is statutory).
- **Everyday life would become a checkpoint.** Every administrative interface, from school and hospital to licensing and employment, would become a citizenship check.

The amendment was written to take membership out of the hands of political majorities ([[If Being Born Here Stopped Being Enough -- Birthright Citizenship and the 14th Amendment]]).

## Economic eligibility: SBA lending

From **March 1, 2026**, the SBA's 7(a) and 504 guaranteed-loan programs require 100% of a business's owners to be U.S. citizens or nationals residing in the U.S. The prior rule allowed as little as 51% ownership by citizens or lawful permanent residents. A March 9 notice extended the rule to microloans and surety bonds from April 1. Any green-card-holder stake disqualifies the business, and later ownership changes can jeopardize compliance on existing loans. SBA framed the change as prioritizing "American job creators." Immigrant-business networks note that immigrants start businesses at about twice the native-born rate, and that corridors dependent on immigrant-owned storefronts are already seeing effects ([[SBA Bars Green Card Holders From Guaranteed Small Business Loans]]). See also the immigrant-entrepreneur material on [[Immigrant Legal Services and Local Support]].
