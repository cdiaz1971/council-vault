# Cities as the Pragmatic Immigration Policy Actors When National Governments Fail

## Summary

Former Bristol Mayor Marvin Rees, writing for Next City, argues that national governments treat migration as a political threat to be managed while cities treat it as a demographic and economic reality to be addressed. European examples show mayors developing labor mobility programs, integrated services, and green economy pathways that outperform national restriction-based approaches.

## Key Points

The core argument: irregular border crossings into Europe are at their lowest levels in years despite increasingly punitive national policies. The restrictionist posture is a response to a political crisis, not a migration crisis. Cities receive about 70% of the world's displaced people and generate more than 80% of global GDP. By 2050, close to a third of Europe's population will be over 65 while the global South has vast young cohorts without work. Managed migration is the connective tissue.

Examples from European cities: Turin is piloting a labor-mobility program with Uganda through pre-departure training, formal contracts, and post-arrival support. Barcelona bundles career guidance with temporary housing, legal aid, psychological support, and language classes. Andalusia municipalities are partnering with Moroccan counterparts to channel workers into renewable energy jobs. Milan is creating tens of thousands of green jobs with upskilling programs for migrants.

Rees's frame from Bristol: "If people are going to live in your city, the smartest thing you can do is help them succeed." The One City Task Force operated on that premise and treated newcomers as assets rather than problems.

Green economy jobs are the largest opportunity: a recent study identified green job shortages in construction, transport, and waste across 25 cities, with $300 billion in growth potential by 2040 if filled through training and sensible immigration policy.

## Relevance

This is the theoretical underpinning of what Frederick is doing through the City Values Act, the Community Trust Act support, and the immigrant community organization partnerships. The argument is not just moral but structural: cities that treat migration as an asset will outperform cities that treat it as a liability, and local governments are better positioned than national governments to operationalize that.

The labor-mobility and green economy framing is worth developing for Frederick's economic development context. Frederick is not Turin or Barcelona, but the logic of pairing immigration welcome with workforce development and housing stability is exportable to a mid-sized mid-Atlantic city with a significant immigrant population and a housing production challenge.

The article also provides rhetorical grounding for pushing back on Frederick County officials who have joined the anti-CTA lawsuit: the county's own economic interests are better served by treating immigrant residents as contributors than as enforcement targets.
