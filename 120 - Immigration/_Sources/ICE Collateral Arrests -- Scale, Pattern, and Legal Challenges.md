# ICE Collateral Arrests -- Scale, Pattern, and Legal Challenges

## Summary

New data from the Deportation Data Project, analyzed by Stateline, reveals that roughly one in four ICE arrests since August 2025 were classified as "collateral" -- meaning they arose not from preexisting warrants but from street sweeps where agents detained people based on appearance or proximity to a target. Maryland is among eight states where collateral arrests exceeded 30% of total enforcement actions. Courts have repeatedly found these arrests constitutionally suspect.

## Key Points

The numbers are significant: approximately 64,000 collateral arrests out of 253,000 total ICE arrests in the August 2025 to early March 2026 window. The criminal profile of collaterally arrested individuals is notably lower than warrant-based arrests: less than 2% had violent crime convictions (one-third the rate of warrant-based arrests), and only 18% had any criminal conviction at all versus 33% for warrant-based arrests.

The mechanism: street sweeps and large operations allow agents to detain people based on appearance or location near a wanted person, and then issue a warrant retroactively if they believe the person is deportable and likely to flee. ICE formalized this as policy in January 2026, though courts have challenged it. A consent decree from an Illinois case requires "reasonable suspicion" before arrest; a federal judge found in February that ICE's new real-time warrant policy violates that decree.

Public backlash and court injunctions appear to have reduced large-scale sweeps. Minneapolis and Chicago operations drew particular outrage. Arrests peaked at 40,000 in December and fell to 30,000 in February.

The Maryland number matters: over 30% of Maryland's ICE arrests were collateral, one of the highest rates in the country. This is the enforcement context in which the City Values Act and any municipal non-cooperation policy operates.

Legal landscape: ACLU DC won a preliminary injunction halting warrantless arrests without probable cause in DC. North Carolina citizens sued after being arrested while landscaping. An Idaho case involves 105 arrests at a Latino community event when agents had warrants for five people.

## Relevance

This is directly actionable for Frederick's immigration policy work. The collateral arrest pattern -- targeting people based on appearance while near someone with a warrant -- is the mechanism that has driven fear in immigrant communities even among people with legal status or citizenship. The fact that less than 2% of collaterally arrested individuals had violent crime convictions undercuts the public safety framing the administration uses. This data is useful for City Values Act arguments, for public communication, and for briefing community organizations including Centro Hispano and SSCM.
