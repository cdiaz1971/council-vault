# Utah Uproar Sues to Block ICE Detention Center Using Maryland Hagerstown as Model

## Summary

Uproar Utah, an arm of the Utah Refugee Justice League, announced plans to sue DHS and ICE to stop a proposed 10,000-person detention mega-center in Salt Lake City -- explicitly drawing on the legal strategy and organizing model used to block the Hagerstown, Maryland detention center. Salt Lake City and Salt Lake County have already filed separate litigation. The Maryland pattern is now national template.

## Key Points

The proposed Salt Lake City facility would be housed in an 830,000+ square foot warehouse purchased by ICE for $145.4 million in March 2026. The facility could hold up to 10,000 people -- described by former U.S. attorney Brent Ward as "insane" and "contrary to Utah's values."

The legal theory being used in both Utah cases and the Maryland case centers on the National Environmental Policy Act (NEPA): DHS and ICE bypassed required planning and environmental review processes. In Maryland, U.S. District Judge Brendan Hurson granted a partial block in April, allowing only limited interior work to proceed. Maryland AG Anthony Brown filed that suit.

Uproar Utah's additional claims go beyond NEPA to fairness and due process for detainees -- conditions, spread of disease, quality of medical care. They are seeking additional plaintiffs among Utahns already detained by ICE.

The Maryland Hagerstown case is specifically named as the model Uproar Utah is following. Executive Director Marie Cornwall said opponents of the Maryland center have been "very helpful" in guiding the Utah response.

The DHS transition context: ICE bought the Salt Lake City property days after Kristi Noem was fired. New Secretary Markwayne Mullin paused new warehouse deals while reviewing Noem-era contracts -- creating uncertainty about whether this facility will proceed regardless of litigation.

A Mormon Women for Ethical Government voice at the press conference named the broken immigration system, not the people inside it, as the problem -- a framing useful for broad coalition building.

## Relevance

This story matters for Frederick in two ways. First, it confirms the Hagerstown opposition model is working well enough to be replicated nationally -- which strengthens the argument for Frederick's continued investment in immigration protection infrastructure and coordination with statewide networks. Second, it documents that NEPA-based litigation is the primary legal vehicle being used to slow or stop these facilities, which is worth tracking as federal facility proposals evolve.

The Frederick Rapid Response Network should be aware that the Maryland case is now cited nationally as a model. That creates both an obligation to continue supporting that fight and an opportunity to draw on the national network of groups doing similar work (Uproar Utah, NAACP, etc.) for resources and coordination.
