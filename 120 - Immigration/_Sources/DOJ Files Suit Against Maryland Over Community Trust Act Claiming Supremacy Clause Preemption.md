# DOJ Files Suit Against Maryland Over Community Trust Act Claiming Supremacy Clause Preemption

## Summary

The US Department of Justice filed suit in US District Court in Baltimore on July 10 seeking to invalidate the Community Trust Act. The federal complaint argues the CTA obstructs federal immigration enforcement and violates the Constitution's Supremacy Clause by preventing local law enforcement from cooperating with ICE on administrative detainers.

## Key Points

The 26-page complaint names the state and AG Anthony Brown as defendants. Gov. Moore is not a defendant. The DOJ repeatedly compares the CTA to "sanctuary city legislation" and invokes the Supremacy Clause, which preempts state and local laws that directly conflict with federal law.

The CTA requires a judicial warrant before local law enforcement is obligated to honor an ICE detainer, replacing the prior system that allowed administrative warrants. The law was passed as a complement to the February 2026 bill banning 287(g) agreements, after advocates worried informal cooperation would continue.

Moore's office responded that Maryland cooperates with federal law enforcement where that "makes our people safe" and contrasted targeted criminal enforcement with enforcement that "ripped mothers out of cars and detained five-year-olds."

The federal lawsuit comes on top of the May 2026 sheriffs' lawsuit filed by FAIR on behalf of 17 Maryland counties including Frederick. The DOJ suit and the sheriffs' suit are now parallel tracks challenging the same law, which could be consolidated or proceed separately.

AG Brown's office declined to comment.

## Relevance

This is the most significant direct legal threat to the CTA since its passage. The DOJ suit elevates the challenge from a coalition of sheriffs to the full weight of federal prosecution. The Supremacy Clause argument is the same one used against state marijuana laws, sanctuary policies, and other state assertions against federal authority. The courts have been mixed on these cases.

For Frederick, the practical stakes are immediate. The Frederick County Sheriff already joined the sheriffs' lawsuit. If the CTA is enjoined while the federal suit proceeds, Frederick County law enforcement would likely resume informal ICE cooperation. The Frederick Rapid Response Network and the community organizations need to treat injunction risk as a live operational scenario, not a hypothetical.

The City Values Act passed under local authority, not state law. That distinction matters: even if the CTA is enjoined, Frederick's own policy framework would remain in place. The question is whether local police, who fall under the city rather than the sheriff, would be affected.

The DOJ complaint's reference to Moore's own caveat that the law "presents real implementation challenges" is notable. The federal lawyers are using Moore's own words as evidence of the law's conflict with federal enforcement needs.
