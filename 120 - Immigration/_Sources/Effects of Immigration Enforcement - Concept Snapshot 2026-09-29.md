# Effects of Immigration Enforcement

The documented harm of enforcement isn't limited to the people arrested. Fear alone moves families away from work, school, childcare, doctors, and benefits they're entitled to. Many of those affected are U.S. citizens, especially children in mixed-status households ([[Immigration Enforcement Chilling Effect Compounds Across Schools Childcare and Legal Protections]]).

## In Frederick

- **Service use and demand.** At the March 19, 2026 joint City-County council meeting, Immigrant Affairs Commission chair Claudia Hernandez reported a drop of more than 50% in people accessing services beginning June 2025. She described a hypervigilant community in which many were choosing to self-isolate, with numbers only slowly beginning to recover. Centro Hispano reported the opposite for its own services: demand rising sharply over the prior six to eight months ([[Immigration Enforcement -- Frederick County WYPR]]).
- **Households after a deportation.** Bolts' September 2026 reporting follows a Salvadoran family after a stepfather's deportation: lost income, disrupted schooling, family separation, and curtailed movement. Other residents described reluctance to call police and growing reliance on mutual aid ([[Frederick County Immigration Enforcement -- Jenkins, Community Harm, and the Limits of Reform]]).
- **During Taskers Chance (September 2026).** Council Vice President Katie Nash: "Now people are just not leaving their houses." Rep. April McClain Delaney said families, "including many with lawful immigration status," were staying inside and afraid to come out ([[ICE Raid Press Coverage Brief -- 2026-09-09]]).

## Schools and childcare (Maryland)

- **Arrests.** Immigration arrests in Maryland nearly tripled, from about 1,300 in the last Biden year to almost 4,700 in the first year of Trump's second term, after the policy steering agents away from "sensitive locations" like schools was rescinded ([[Immigration Enforcement Fear Driving Hispanic School Enrollment Decline in Maryland]]).
- **School enrollment.** Hispanic K-12 enrollment fell for the first time in years, after growing about 4.7% a year since pandemic lows. Among Maryland schools with at least 500 Hispanic students, 12 of the 20 sharpest declines were in Montgomery County (6–11% at individual schools). Montgomery and Prince George's together enroll more than half of the state's roughly 210,000 Hispanic students. The Hispanic graduation rate fell 4.4 percent between 2023-24 and 2024-25 (5.5% for English learners, versus 1.2% overall), and international-student intake offices reported unprecedented declines ([[Immigration Enforcement Fear Driving Hispanic School Enrollment Decline in Maryland]]).
- **Childcare.** Arco Iris Bilingual Children's Center in Laurel went from full with a waitlist for thirteen years to about half capacity. Its director traces part of the drop to a December incident when ICE parked eleven cars outside the center. One parent wondered whether the center's Spanish name draws attention. This came on top of Maryland's May 2025 childcare-scholarship freeze. Scholarships resumed July 1, 2026 with $20 million restored, but a waitlist remains ([[Immigration Enforcement Fear Compounding Maryland Childcare Access Crisis]]).
- **Why school-protection laws don't prevent this.** They restrict what school and local officials may do to assist ICE, not what federal agents do in a parking lot or on an adjacent street ([[ICE Enforcement at Schools Shows Limits of State and Local Sensitive-Location Restrictions]]).

## Health care

The American Academy of Pediatrics surveyed more than 1,600 pediatricians (April–June 2026), whose patients are on average about a quarter from immigrant families. Over the past year:
- **40%** saw families avoid medical care for a child because of a household member's status
- **31%** saw families avoid applying for Medicaid, CHIP, or SNAP
- just over **a fifth** saw families keep children home from school

Avoidance reached **48%** among urban and inner-city pediatricians, versus **32%** among rural ones. Clinicians describe children arriving sicker and missing preventive care and surgeries. The AAP considers these figures an undercount. Nationally, a quarter of children have at least one foreign-born parent ([[Immigration Enforcement Drives Immigrant Families Away From Pediatric Care]]).

## Public benefits and the public-charge rule

- **The rule.** New USCIS guidance, effective **September 18, 2026**, lets officers weigh enrollment in non-cash benefits, including Medicaid, CHIP, and SNAP, against people seeking green cards, certain visas, or admission. It reverses the 2022 rule that excluded those programs. Asylees, refugees, trafficking victims, VAWA self-petitioners, and other protected classes are exempt. DHS itself projected nearly $9 billion a year in reduced federal and state benefit spending from disenrollment, with downstream revenue effects on hospitals, pharmacies, SNAP grocers, and landlords in federally funded housing ([[New Public Charge Rule Expands to Non-Cash Benefits]]).
- **The chilling effect beyond eligibility.** KFF estimates **1.4 to 4.1 million** Medicaid and CHIP enrollees in mixed-status households could disenroll out of fear, including U.S.-citizen children whose own eligibility is unaffected. Research on the first Trump-era rule found families avoiding food assistance and health care even when eligible, and disability advocates flag immigrants with disabilities as disproportionately affected ([[Public Charge Fear Could Push Millions Off Benefits Regardless of Eligibility]]; *the two notes clip the same Stateline article*).

## Work, housing, and local economies

- **Twin Cities, Minnesota.** During Operation Metro Surge, tens of thousands of low-income immigrant households stopped going to work by January 2026. HOME Line tracked more than 2,400 eviction filings in Minneapolis and St. Paul in the first weeks of 2026, with estimates of $47 million or more in lost wages and $15.7 million in added rental-assistance need. Five unions and a new tenants' union threatened a March 1 rent strike, demanding an eviction moratorium and $50 million in relief ([[ICE Enforcement Chilling Effect -- Economic and Housing Disruption]]).
- **Memphis, Tennessee.** The state-requested Memphis Safe Task Force brought the National Guard and more than 30 federal agencies under 287(g) agreements. The 287(g) program was reported to have seen a 916% increase in county participation since January 2025. Critics documented pretextual stops of Black and Latino residents, and the volunteer network Vecindarios 901 built an observer network ([[ICE Enforcement Chilling Effect -- Economic and Housing Disruption]]).
- **The pattern.** Fear suppresses work and spending, raises eviction pressure, and strains local housing systems, with effects falling on low-income renters regardless of status ([[ICE Enforcement Chilling Effect -- Economic and Housing Disruption]]).

## Civic life

Enforcement posture alone can keep families from public milestones. When federal agents announced status checks at base gates for a Marine Corps graduation at Parris Island, some parents missed their children's graduation even though DHS said no arrests would be made ([[ICE at Military Graduations -- Chilling Effect on Immigrant Families]]).
