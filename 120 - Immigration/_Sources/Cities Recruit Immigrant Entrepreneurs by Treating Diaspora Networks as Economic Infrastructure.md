# Cities Recruit Immigrant Entrepreneurs by Treating Diaspora Networks as Economic Infrastructure

## Summary
Schenectady's deliberate recruitment of Guyanese-American families and Parma's recognition of its Ukrainian and Polish Villages show cities treating immigrant community networks as economic infrastructure rather than just a cultural feature.

## Key Points
Schenectady, New York, facing a dwindling population and vacant storefronts, deliberately recruited Guyanese-American families from New York City rather than waiting for organic growth. Later research connected that migration to increased homeownership and neighborhood revival in the city. Parma, Ohio formally recognizes its Ukrainian Village and Polish Village, treating those as long standing settlements rather than incidental demographics.

The article argues these community networks function as real economic infrastructure: language support, trusted introductions, informal financing channels, customer bases, and church or civic ties that let a newcomer avoid starting from zero. Its recommendation is that cities should inventory diaspora networks, cultural institutions, and immigrant serving organizations with the same seriousness they inventory land, zoning, and utilities, because those relationships may matter as much to where a family settles as the space itself.

## Relevance
Frederick already has this kind of infrastructure through Centro Hispano, SSCM, and AACF. This article's framing suggests those relationships could be treated more explicitly as an economic development asset, not just a service and advocacy network, particularly as Golden Mile's engagement phase and eventual plan take shape.
