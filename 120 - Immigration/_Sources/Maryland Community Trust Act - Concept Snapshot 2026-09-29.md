# Maryland Community Trust Act

## What the law does

The Community Trust Act (SB 791), enacted as **Chapter 872** of 2026, limits how Maryland state and local law enforcement and correctional facilities cooperate with federal immigration enforcement. According to the enacted text:

- It distinguishes **judicial warrants** from documents issued by federal executive agencies. Local agencies are obligated only by a judicial warrant, not an ICE administrative detainer.
- It addresses immigration-status inquiries, detention, transfers, and information sharing.
- It contains exceptions, including for certain convicted individuals and routine booking.
- It requires agencies to adopt policies with penalties for violations, and it permits civil enforcement and damages in specified circumstances.

([[Frederick County Immigration Enforcement -- Jenkins, Community Harm, and the Limits of Reform]], citing the official bill record and enacted chapter)

News coverage described the exceptions as people convicted of felonies in the U.S., registered sex offenders, people who served 12–18 months in state prison, and people who committed offenses in another state and served at least five years ([[Maryland Community Trust Act -- Closing the ICE-Hold Loophole]]; [[Community Trust Act -- Judicial Warrant Requirement and Sheriff Resistance]]). *That list is reporters' paraphrase; the enacted text controls.*

The law also restricts police and correctional officers from proactively sharing information about individuals with ICE ([[Maryland Community Trust Act -- SB791 Passage and ICE Detainer Limits]]).

Its practical limit: the CTA governs what state and local agencies may do. It doesn't govern what federal agents do once they encounter someone. Warrant-based rules like it create legal exposure and a remedy after the fact rather than preventing an encounter ([[Warrant-Based ICE Restrictions Create Legal Exposure Not Prevention]]).

## How it became law

- **The 287(g) ban came first.** SB 245/HB 444 banned 287(g) agreements between local law enforcement and ICE and took effect on Governor Moore's signature, **February 17, 2026**. With the ban in force statewide, all nine Maryland counties that had 287(g) agreements, **Frederick** among them, dropped them. Most formally notified ICE the same day or the next ([[Maryland 287(g) Ban -- Implementation and Compliance After Governor Signature]]; [[Maryland Community Trust Act -- Closing the ICE-Hold Loophole]]).
- **Why the CTA followed.** After the ban, some sheriffs signaled they would keep cooperating with ICE informally. Sponsor Sen. Clarence Lam called the CTA the "critical bookend" that closes that gap ([[Maryland Community Trust Act -- Closing the ICE-Hold Loophole]]).
- **Passage.** The General Assembly passed the CTA and the companion Data Privacy Act (HB 711) on the final day of the 2026 session. The CTA carried an emergency designation, and We Are CASA led the advocacy coalition ([[Maryland Community Trust Act -- SB791 Passage and ICE Detainer Limits]]). Del. Crystal Jackson Parker was the only Democrat on the Judiciary Committee to vote against it ([[Community Trust Act and Data Privacy Act -- Maryland Enacted Without Governor Signature]]).
- **No signature.** On May 22, Moore announced he would let both bills become law without his signature. He said he supported their goals but cited "real implementation challenges" around joint federal-state investigations, which his office said it would address with the Attorney General through executive action and possibly legislation ([[Maryland Community Trust Act -- Governor Allows Law Without Signature]]).
- **Enactment.** The CTA became law on **May 31, 2026** ([[Frederick County Immigration Enforcement -- Jenkins, Community Harm, and the Limits of Reform]], citing the enacted chapter).
- **Other bills in the same push.** We Are CASA listed HB 1017 (private immigration detention), SB 810 (the Maryland Values Act, sensitive locations), SB 792 (hospital access), HB 1350 (in-state tuition), HB 963 (noncitizen estate representatives), and SB 1 (officer face coverings) as advanced in the same session ([[Maryland Community Trust Act -- SB791 Passage and ICE Detainer Limits]]).

## Companion laws

- **Data Privacy Act (HB 711).** It amends the Public Information Act to prohibit businesses from selling personal data for immigration enforcement. It was sponsored by Del. Lorig Charkoudian; the House version became law, while the Senate version (Lam) didn't advance in the House. Opponents raised concerns about weakening the Public Information Act and about making officials judge a requester's purpose. We Are CASA called the pair a "one-two punch": enforcement cooperation on one side, the data pipeline on the other ([[Data Privacy Act -- Blocking Personal Data for Immigration Enforcement]]; [[Maryland Community Trust Act -- Governor Allows Law Without Signature]]).
- **No Kings Act (SB 346/HB 351).** Final passage came April 11, and it was signed May 13, 2026, **effective October 1, 2026**. It gives Marylanders and the Attorney General a state civil cause of action against federal officials who violate constitutional rights under color of law. It excludes people acting under a joint task force that primarily enforces federal law, which limits its reach against task-force immigration operations. Sponsors were Sen. Jeff Waldstreicher and Majority Leader David Moon ([[Maryland No Kings Act -- Final Passage and Local Implications]]; [[Maryland No Kings Act -- Civil Liability for Federal Constitutional Violations]]).

## Frederick County

- **287(g) history.** Frederick County's 287(g) agreement dated to 2008 under Sheriff Chuck Jenkins and used the Jail Enforcement Model, asking about immigration status at booking ([[Immigration Enforcement -- Frederick County WYPR]]). Jenkins claims roughly 2,000 referrals to ICE ([[Frederick County Immigration Enforcement -- Jenkins, Community Harm, and the Limits of Reform]]). The agreement ended with the February 2026 ban, and Jenkins warned the change could increase direct ICE activity in the county ([[Immigration Enforcement -- Frederick County WYPR]]). César urged a statewide 287(g) ban in remarks as Council Member-elect ([[287 speech]]).
- **The Sheriff is a plaintiff against the CTA** (see Litigation).
- **City and county jurisdiction are separate.** The Sheriff is an independently elected county officer. The City's [[City Values Act]] governs City agencies, including the Frederick Police Department, and the City can't direct the Sheriff ([[Community Trust Act -- CTA Sheriffs Lawsuit Synthesis]]). Where the CTA applies statewide, the CVA adds a City-level judicial-warrant standard for non-public City facilities ([[Maryland 287(g) Ban -- Implementation and Compliance After Governor Signature]]).
- **After the formal partnership ended.** Bolts reporting describes residents' continuing fear and intensified direct federal enforcement. It frames the November 2026 sheriff election as a choice between Jenkins, who defends cooperation and won in 2022 by 2.8 points, and challenger Karl Bickel, who proposes monitoring ICE operations and publishing reports ([[Frederick County Immigration Enforcement -- Jenkins, Community Harm, and the Limits of Reform]]; [[Karl Bickel]]).

## Litigation

- **Gahler v. Moore (No. 8:26-cv-02057, D. Md., Greenbelt).** Filed May 26, 2026 by the Federation for American Immigration Reform (FAIR) on behalf of 17 sheriffs, led by Harford County Sheriff Jeffrey Gahler. The plaintiff counties are Allegany, Calvert, Caroline, Carroll, Cecil, Dorchester, **Frederick**, Garrett, Harford, Kent, Queen Anne's, St. Mary's, Somerset, Talbot, Washington, Wicomico, and Worcester. It names the State, Governor Moore, and AG Anthony Brown, and claims the CTA "intentionally obstructs federal law enforcement" ([[Frederick County Sheriff Joins Lawsuit Challenging Community Trust Act]]).
- **United States v. Maryland (D. Md., Baltimore).** DOJ filed a 26-page complaint on **July 10, 2026** against the State and AG Brown, but not Moore. It argues the CTA violates the Supremacy Clause, repeatedly likens it to "sanctuary city legislation," and quotes Moore's own "implementation challenges" statement ([[DOJ Files Suit Against Maryland Over Community Trust Act Claiming Supremacy Clause Preemption]]). The DOJ and sheriffs' complaints share nearly identical language in places ([[DOJ Sanctuary Lawsuit Campaign National Pattern and Maryland]]).
- **The legal theories.**
  - DOJ argues that sanctuary laws obstruct lawful federal enforcement.
  - Defenders argue that the Supremacy Clause bars conflicting state laws but creates no duty to assist, and that under the Tenth Amendment anti-commandeering doctrine (*Printz v. United States*) the federal government can't compel state officers to run federal programs ([[DOJ Sanctuary Lawsuit Campaign National Pattern and Maryland]]).
  - Courts have treated detainer compliance as voluntary. Honoring detainers without a judicial warrant can also create Fourth Amendment liability ([[End Sanctuary Cities Act and the Anti-Commandeering Defense]]).
- **What a ruling against the CTA would mean for the City Values Act.** *confidence: low. Sources disagree.* One view is that the CVA passed under local authority and would remain in place ([[DOJ Files Suit Against Maryland Over Community Trust Act Claiming Supremacy Clause Preemption]]). Another is that the same preemption reasoning would likely reach it, because it uses the same warrant-based design ([[County-Level Trust Acts Proliferate as Community Trust Act Faces DOJ Preemption Challenge]]; [[Immigration Enforcement Chilling Effect Compounds Across Schools Childcare and Legal Protections]]). See also [[City Values Act]].

## National context

- **The DOJ campaign.** The Justice Department filed 27 lawsuits against more than 20 sanctuary jurisdictions after January 2025, including Connecticut, New Jersey, New York, a Michigan county, and Philadelphia, and the pace has accelerated. The results are mixed. A Trump-appointed judge enjoined Philadelphia's agent mask and unmarked-vehicle policy, while six of its seven related ordinances took effect. A Biden-appointed judge dismissed DOJ's suits against four New Jersey cities ([[DOJ Sanctuary Lawsuit Campaign National Pattern and Maryland]]).
- **Federal legislation.** Sen. Lindsey Graham's End Sanctuary Cities Act (S. 3805) would criminalize local officials who decline ICE detainers. Legal commentators argue it's unconstitutional under anti-commandeering precedent ([[End Sanctuary Cities Act and the Anti-Commandeering Defense]]).
- **State pushback.** Democratic lawmakers introduced about 242 immigration-enforcement pushback bills in 32 states in 2026, and 53 passed in 17 states. They cover officer masking, sensitive locations, detention-facility zoning, and, in Rhode Island, a right to sue over arrests without a judicial warrant. Courts are testing their limits: California's mask ban was blocked for exempting state officers, and a private prison operator is challenging Colorado's detention health-inspection law ([[State Legislatures Push Back on Federal Immigration Enforcement Through Local Law]]; [[Courts Are Testing the Limits of State Authority Over Federal Immigration Enforcement]]; *the two notes clip the same Stateline article*).
- **County layers.** Montgomery County has a County Values Act layered on the CTA. Advocates call the overall effectiveness of the state and county layers "a mixed bag" while the DOJ suit is pending, and note that before the CTA virtually every Maryland county collaborated informally with ICE to some degree ([[County-Level Trust Acts Proliferate as Community Trust Act Faces DOJ Preemption Challenge]]).
