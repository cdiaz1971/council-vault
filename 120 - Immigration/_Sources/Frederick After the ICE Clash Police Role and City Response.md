# Frederick After the ICE Clash Police Role and City Response

## Summary
A Banner follow-up on the Taskers Chance ICE clash examines how Frederick is absorbing the episode: clergy observer networks, a full two-day timeline from Díaz and Nash, the police chief's limits on intervening, the mayor's view that officers must act when rights are violated, and the sheriff's claim that state non-cooperation laws invited the surge.

## Key Points
- **Community infrastructure was already forming.** Rev. Chance Martinez-Colon of Grace United Church of Christ took a clergy team to Minneapolis to watch its enforcement surge, then trained Frederick clergy as ICE observers. Rev. Beth Hutton of Calvary United Methodist frames her objection as a lawfulness question, not opposition to ICE as such. Before the clash, volunteers were already walking children home from an elementary school, delivering groceries to families with a detained relative, and posting neighborhood ICE alerts.
- **Timeline.** Díaz and Nash got the first call just after 6 a.m. on Thursday, September 3. Agents held an older woman and a teenager for hours, accusing them of interfering. ICE told FPD that someone had escaped custody as a result. The pair were released with a warning that charges could follow. Council members rotated through the scene that day. About five or six ICE vehicles were present at any given time. Around 8 p.m. Friday, September 4, vehicles lined up at one Stoneybrook Court house. Around 9 p.m. they drove onto sidewalks, one nearly striking Nash, and she called the police chief. About a dozen FPD officers arrived as the crowd grew to roughly 50. ERO agents used batons and dragged observers off the porch. A resident opened the door, agents searched, and left empty-handed.
- **Police role.** Chief Kevin Meyer (on WFMD) called it an impossible situation: officers cannot interfere with federal operations, are present to deter excessive force, and keep body cameras rolling. ICE would not tell FPD whether it had a warrant. Retired Howard County colonel Gary McLhinney noted federal agencies normally notify local police before operating in their jurisdiction.
- **Mayor's position.** O'Connor said ICE appears to lack the organization and training for these operations and will review bodycam footage with Meyer. He went further than the chief: officers who observe a rights violation have an obligation to decide what level of intervention is appropriate to stop it.
- **Sheriff's counter-narrative.** Sheriff Chuck Jenkins, a plaintiff in the sheriffs' suit against the state non-cooperation laws, argued the 287(g) ban caused the federal surge and that the confrontation was avoidable.
- **Political context.** The article credits the City Council under Díaz with passing the non-cooperation measure this year, while calling it perhaps more symbolic. Latino residents are about 20% of the city as of the 2024 Census. Rep. April McClain Delaney called for a full, transparent investigation.

## Relevance
Two threads here matter more than the incident recap already in [[ICE Confrontation in Frederick's Tasker's Chance Neighborhood]]. First, the gap between Meyer and O'Connor on duty to intervene is a live policy question the Council could settle, through a written FPD directive on officer conduct during federal operations (observe, document, intervene when a rights violation is witnessed) rather than leaving it to the chief's discretion in the moment. Second, Jenkins has a ready narrative that the state laws caused this. That framing will surface in any Council discussion and in the UMD URSP708 advisory panel appearances, so it is worth having a prepared answer. The "more symbolic" characterization of the City Values Act is also worth answering on the record with what the CVA actually changed in city practice.
