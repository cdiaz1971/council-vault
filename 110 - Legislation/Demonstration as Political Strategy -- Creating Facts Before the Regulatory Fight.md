# Demonstration as Political Strategy -- Creating Facts Before the Regulatory Fight

## The Pattern

A structural pattern runs through several threads in the council work that hasn't been named as a unified strategy: before formal regulatory change becomes possible, someone has to create a fact on the ground that changes what decision-makers believe is true about risk, feasibility, and public appetite.

This is not unique to any one policy domain. It shows up in housing reform, in surveillance technology governance, and in the approach to democratic resilience organizing. The mechanism is the same across all three: demonstration precedes legislation because legislation requires a political condition that only demonstration can create.

Three instantiations from the vault this year:

**Housing reform (Better Block / Imagination Problem thread).** [[The Imagination Problem in Housing Reform -- Why Code Change Is Step 3 Not Step 1]] names this most explicitly. You cannot argue people past the imagination barrier -- they have to experience the alternative before they can stop opposing it. Better Block's methodology demonstrates this empirically: a $5K-$50K temporary activation routinely unlocks millions in follow-on public investment because it converts institutional skeptics, not just residents. The pop-up comes before the rezoning because the rezoning requires a belief -- in officials, in residents, in developers -- that the alternative works. The demonstration creates that belief faster than any argument could.

**Data center moratorium (May 2026 brief).** The data center moratorium brief, even though the moratorium itself was ultimately not pursued, operated on the same logic: establish a political posture -- publicly, visibly -- before the formal legislative process begins, in order to change the terms on which the Planning Commission and Council consider the text amendment. The public signal that Frederick is paying attention to data center impacts changes what developers and the administration believe they can quietly advance. The moratorium threat was a demonstration of political will, not a legislative end in itself.

**Surveillance technology procurement.** The Flock Safety research cluster and the [[Surveillance Vendor Capture -- How Technology Gets Into Local Government Against Community Will]] synthesis document how surveillance technology enters local government: quietly, through procurement channels, before any public deliberation occurs. The counter-move -- establishing a public position on procurement governance before any specific contract arrives -- is the same pattern in reverse. You create the political fact (Frederick has a policy expectation) before the vendor arrives with a contract, so the vendor faces an existing norm rather than a vacuum.

## Why This Pattern Matters

The default political timeline runs: problem identified, policy proposed, public deliberation, vote. The demonstration-first pattern runs: demonstration or position established, political condition created, policy proposed from a position of already-existing belief rather than contested assertion.

The difference is not cosmetic. In the default timeline, the reformer bears the entire burden of proof at the moment of maximum opposition -- when the developer is at the table, when the vendor has already built relationships with staff, when residents are being asked to imagine something they've never seen. In the demonstration-first timeline, a portion of that proof burden has already been discharged before the formal fight begins.

This is also why visible public advocacy -- Better Block activations, published position statements, public research clusters like the surveillance vault -- is not supplementary to governance. It is the precondition for the governance to work.

## The Lubeck Sequencing Problem

Aaron Lubeck's hierarchy of needs for great urban development names a related constraint: cultural discontent must precede political will, which must precede legal reform, which must precede design capacity. You cannot skip steps. If you start with the code, you get a law that no one enforces and no one builds to because the upstream conditions don't exist yet.

The demonstration strategy is how you accelerate the upstream conditions without waiting for them to develop organically. A single well-executed Better Block activation can compress years of cultural discontent into a single afternoon. A moratorium threat can compress years of political credibility-building into a single public statement. A published procurement standard can compress years of norm-setting into a single policy document.

## Frederick Applications

The pattern suggests a consistent pre-legislative move for any major reform thread:

1. **Identify the imagination gap.** What do residents, officials, or staff currently believe is not possible, not wanted, or not risky enough to regulate? That gap is the target for the demonstration.

2. **Design the smallest demonstration that closes the gap.** For housing: a temporary street activation or popup market on a currently car-dominated corridor. For surveillance: a published position statement and policy template before any specific contract surfaces. For affordability: an ALICE-framing public event that makes the constituency visible before the legislative ask.

3. **Run the demonstration before the formal proposal.** The timing matters. A demonstration after opposition has consolidated is much harder to execute and less likely to convert. The demonstration works best when it creates the political condition, not when it responds to its absence.

4. **Track the belief shift, not just the demonstration.** Better Block's methodology works because it measures what changes: permit applications, investment commitments, council votes. The demonstration is an instrument for shifting belief; belief shift is the actual output.

## What This Synthesis Adds

None of the individual threads -- the housing imagination work, the moratorium brief, the surveillance procurement cluster -- names this as a general strategy. Together they document that it is one: a recurring approach to creating the political conditions for regulatory change before the formal fight begins. Naming it makes it portable. The next time a new policy domain opens up, the question becomes: what is the demonstration that creates the political fact we need before we file the legislation?
