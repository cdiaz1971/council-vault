# Municipal ID -- Legal Risks and Federal Preemption

Municipal ID programs operate in a legal gray zone shaped by home-rule authority, state preemption doctrine, and federal immigration supremacy. Understanding the failure modes matters as much as the program design.

## The Core Legal Framework

Local governments in home-rule states have broad authority to issue municipal IDs because no federal law prohibits it and most states have not preempted the practice. Maryland's home-rule framework gives Frederick City sufficient authority to enact an ID ordinance without state legislative approval, provided the program does not conflict with state law or attempt to confer benefits that state law reserves for specific populations.

Municipal IDs are explicitly not REAL ID-compliant and cannot be used for federal purposes: boarding planes, entering federal buildings, or accessing federally controlled benefits. Communicating this clearly is not just a legal necessity -- it protects cardholders from misunderstanding the card's scope in ways that could expose them to harm.

## Federal Preemption Risk

The most significant legal exposure is field preemption. Federal courts have held that immigration regulation is exclusively a federal domain. A municipal ID ordinance that appears to regulate immigration status -- even indirectly -- invites a preemption challenge. The safe path is to frame the program entirely around local service access, public safety, and community inclusion, with no reference to immigration status as a criterion for eligibility or benefit.

The program must be immigration-status neutral: available to all residents regardless of status, with eligibility based solely on proof of identity and residency. This is both legally protective and politically defensible.

## Data Retention as Legal Exposure

The decision to prohibit retention of application documents is not just a privacy best practice -- it is a legal shield. If Frederick retains copies of identity documents, a federal subpoena or court order could compel disclosure. Prohibiting retention from the start eliminates the data that could be demanded. The ordinance should explicitly state that neither application forms nor the fact of participation will be disclosed to any federal, state, or local law enforcement entity, and that Frederick will defend against disclosure to the maximum extent permitted by law.

## Risk of Program Dismantlement

Programs established only by executive policy or administrative rule are vulnerable to reversal by a future administration or council majority. Establishing the program through ordinance embeds it in local law, requires council action to undo, and creates enforceable accountability mechanisms. This is the right path for Frederick.

## Why This Matters for César

The memo you've already drafted directs a feasibility study before legislation. The legal exposure around data retention and federal preemption should be front-loaded into that study's scope -- not discovered after the program is designed. Framing the ordinance around local service access (not immigration status) insulates Frederick from the most likely legal challenge vector.
