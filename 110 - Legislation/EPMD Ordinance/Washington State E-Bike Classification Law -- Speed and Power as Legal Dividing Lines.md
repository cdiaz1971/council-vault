# Washington State E-Bike Classification Law -- Speed and Power as Legal Dividing Lines

## Summary

Washington state's new e-bike classification law uses 20 mph unpowered speed and 750W motor power as the statutory dividing line between e-bikes and electric motorcycles, with Class 3 restricted to riders 16+. A peer-state model for Frederick's EPMD ordinance classification framework.

## Key Points

**The statutory thresholds:** Under the new Washington law:
- E-bikes cannot exceed 20 mph without the rider pedaling
- E-bike motors cannot produce more than 750 watts
- Vehicles exceeding either threshold are classified as electric motorcycles, requiring a license and minimum age of 16
- Class 3 e-bikes (motor assistance up to 28 mph with pedaling) are also restricted to riders 16 and older

**The policy driver:** Unsafe operation -- particularly by minors without licenses, registration, or safety equipment -- drove the legislation. The Pierce County Sheriff's Office explicitly named juvenile operators on public spaces as the proximate cause. The law responds to a real enforcement gap that has emerged as higher-powered electric vehicles spread into the consumer market.

**What it does not do:** The law does not resolve access questions for Class 1 and 2 e-bikes on trails or paths. That remains the more contested policy territory, particularly around pedestrian-speed facilities.

## Relevance

Frederick's EPMD ordinance (G-26-___) is built on a similar classification framework, distinguishing Class 1, 2, and 3 devices by speed and power. The WA law confirms that the 20 mph / 750W threshold is becoming a durable national standard for the e-bike / electric motorcycle divide, which strengthens the ordinance's definitional foundation.

The 16+ age restriction for Class 3 also tracks with the equity argument in [[EPMD Ordinance -- Equity Argument, Class 1 and 2 Path Access]] -- Class 1 and 2 (lower speed, no throttle) are the access-enabling devices, and they are treated differently under both WA law and the Frederick draft. This peer-state evidence is useful if AMAC or council members push back on the classification logic.

[[Massachusetts Speed-Based Tiered E-Bike Regulation -- Peer State Model]] is the other key peer reference. Together WA and MA establish that tiered classification by speed and power is the emerging standard, not a novel Frederick approach.
