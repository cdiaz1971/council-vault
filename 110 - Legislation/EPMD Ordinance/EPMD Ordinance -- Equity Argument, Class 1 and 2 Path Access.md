# EPMD Ordinance -- Equity Argument, Class 1 and 2 Path Access

## Summary

Sourced argument that the draft EPMD ordinance's opt-in default for shared-use paths creates a distributional harm that falls hardest on lower-income and transportation-dependent residents. Three pillars: federal research showing low-income workers are the heaviest e-bike users; documented cost savings that make e-bikes a genuine car replacement for ALICE households; and regional precedent showing every comparable mid-Atlantic jurisdiction permits Class 1 and 2 access by default. The strongest regional comparator is Montgomery County Parks -- a Maryland agency directly bordering Frederick County -- which in 2021 permanently authorized e-bikes on all hard-surface park trails, with prohibition only where expressly posted. Built for committee use and direct response to opposition.

---

## The Core Claim: E-Bikes Are Primary Transportation for Lower-Income Residents

The strongest sourced evidence on this point comes from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory. NREL's analysis of nearly 50,000 trips from its Can Do Colorado program -- which distributed e-bikes to low-income essential workers -- showed that several locations in downtown Denver provided time-, cost-, and energy-efficient access to opportunities using e-bikes compared to driving, and that low-income essential workers used their e-bikes most frequently to commute to work (NREL, "Mobility Energy Productivity and Equity: E-Bike Impacts for Low-Income Essential Workers in Denver," Transportation Research Record, 2023).

Low-income participants in the Can Do Colorado program rode their e-bikes nearly 50% more than non-low-income participants -- an average of 32 miles per week versus 22 miles per week (ClimateAction Center, citing Denver/RMI program data, 2022). When given the tool, lower-income workers used it harder and more purposefully than higher-income participants. That is a commuting pattern, not a recreational one. An ordinance that defaults to banning those bikes from shared paths creates a transportation burden that falls on the residents most dependent on the infrastructure.

The Can Do Colorado program put more than 4,700 e-bikes on the streets in 2022 alone, with over 65% of the funding going toward low-income residents (RMI, "Helping Cities Accelerate E-Bike Adoption," 2023). The federal government invested in getting e-bikes to lower-income workers specifically because the research showed they needed and used them most. A local opt-in default that restricts shared-path access undercuts that investment and the transportation need it addresses.

---

## The Cost Argument: E-Bikes Eliminate a Major Expense Burden

Studies have found that riding an e-bike costs approximately $200 per year in total operating costs, compared to over $3,000 per year for operating a car -- a savings of roughly $2,800 annually for a commuter who switches from driving to e-biking (MacFox Bike, "How Much Are Electric Bike Commuters Saving," 2025, citing multiple cost analyses). For a household at or below the ALICE threshold -- 43% of Frederick city households -- that figure is not abstract. It is rent, groceries, or childcare.

Research in Germany found that one-fifth of e-bike users either sold their car or decided to abandon previous plans to purchase a car (RMI, citing German transport research). E-bikes are not supplementing cars for these users; they are replacing them. That substitution only holds if the full infrastructure -- roads, lanes, and shared paths -- is accessible to the e-bike rider. Remove path access, and the transportation substitution breaks down for the trips where paths are the practical route.

---

## Why Path Access Specifically Matters

The counterargument will be that e-bike riders can use the road. That misses two things.

First, for the segment of District 2 residents who chose an e-bike partly because of anxiety about riding in vehicle traffic -- older riders, newer riders, immigrant residents unfamiliar with local street conditions -- road access is not a functional substitute for path access. Removing path access does not redirect those riders to roads; it removes them from the transportation network.

Second, shared-use paths in Frederick, including Carroll Creek Linear Park, function as direct routes to downtown employment and commercial areas. They are shorter and safer than parallel roadways for the trips they serve. Requiring an e-bike commuter to take a longer, more dangerous road route while a recreational conventional cyclist uses the path is a distributional outcome that advantages the more privileged user.

---

## Regional Precedent: What Neighbors Are Doing

The resistance to Class 1 and 2 path access is not the regional norm. Every comparable jurisdiction around Frederick treats Class 1 and 2 e-bikes as functionally equivalent to conventional bicycles on shared paths, with restriction as the opt-out -- not the default.

**Montgomery County Parks (strongest comparator).** Montgomery County Department of Parks -- M-NCPPC, the agency directly bordering Frederick County to the south -- ran a formal e-bike pilot program beginning June 2019. After six months of pilot data, the Montgomery County Planning Board voted unanimously in November 2019 to permanently allow e-bikes rather than extend the pilot. Park Directive MC-21-001, effective March 25, 2021, codified that decision: e-bikes are authorized on all hard-surface trails on M-NCPPC Montgomery County parkland except where expressly prohibited and posted accordingly (M-NCPPC, Park Directive MC-21-001, March 18, 2021, signed by Director Michael F. Riley). The default is permissive. Prohibition requires an affirmative posting decision. That is the exact inverse of what the Frederick draft creates.

Montgomery County's process also provides a procedural model: they ran a pilot, collected data, brought it to the Planning Board, and made a permanent decision based on evidence. The Board voted unanimously to make access permanent. That is the kind of deliberate, evidence-based process that produces durable policy -- and the outcome was full access as the default.

**C&O Canal National Historical Park.** The C&O Canal NHP -- a shared-use path running directly through Maryland -- has implemented the rule that e-bikes are allowed where traditional bicycles are allowed and prohibited where traditional bicycles are prohibited (C&O Canal Trust, "E-Bikes on the Towpath," citing NPS Policy Memorandum 19-01, 2019). That is the federal default for one of the most heavily used shared-path networks in the mid-Atlantic, running through our neighboring counties.

**Capital Crescent Trail.** On the NPS section of the Capital Crescent Trail -- running from Georgetown, DC, into Montgomery County, Maryland -- Class 1, 2, and 3 e-bikes are all permitted, subject to a 15 mph speed limit and a pedaling requirement (Coalition for the Capital Crescent Trail, Safety page, current). The Maryland section of the CCT runs through Montgomery County parkland governed by the same MC-21-001 directive above.

**Virginia.** Virginia state law provides that e-bikes may generally be used on bicycle or shared-use paths where bicycles are allowed, unless a locality, agency, or trail manager restricts them. Virginia State Parks specifically allow Class 1 and Class 2 e-bikes on bicycle paths or trails designated for bicycle use (Virginia Code, cited in Mole Hill Bikes Virginia E-Bike Laws Guide, 2024). Virginia's default is permissive; restriction is the opt-out. That is exactly what Maryland state law establishes and the opposite of what the Frederick draft creates.

The argument for Frederick's opt-in default requires explaining why Frederick's shared paths require stricter rules than Montgomery County Parks, the C&O Canal Towpath, the Capital Crescent Trail, or any Virginia state park trail. No such argument exists in the administrative record.

---

## The Two-Tier Access Problem

The most direct framing for committee: the ordinance as drafted allows a recreational cyclist on a high-end conventional road bike to use Carroll Creek Linear Park with no restriction, while banning a District 2 resident on a used Class 1 commuter e-bike from using the same path to get to work. The first user has more resources and is using the path for leisure. The second user has fewer resources and is using the path for transportation. The ordinance advantages the wealthier recreational user and burdens the lower-income transportation-dependent user. That is not a defensible equity position, and it is not required by Maryland state law.

Montgomery County -- Frederick's next-door neighbor -- looked at this same question through a formal pilot process and reached the opposite conclusion by unanimous vote.

## Speed Parity With Conventional Bicycles

Class 1 and 2 e-bikes are capped at 20 mph under Maryland law and under the federal consumer product safety framework. In practice, real-world average speeds are substantially lower. A Swiss study cited by the Rails-to-Trails Conservancy found that average e-bike speeds on shared paths were 14 mph, versus 8.7 mph for conventional cyclists. The top speeds of most e-bike users were well below the 20 mph cap. A 2018 Boulder County, Colorado pilot study found that e-bikes were only marginally faster than conventional bikes on uphill grades -- 13.8 mph versus 12.9 mph on average -- and slower on downhills. The speed differential between a Class 1 e-bike and a fast conventional cyclist is minimal. Treating them categorically differently is not supported by actual observed speed data.

Source: Rails-to-Trails Conservancy E-Bikes Toolbox (railstotrails.org); Boulder County E-Bike Pilot Study, 2018 (assets.bouldercounty.gov).

## Peer Jurisdiction Pilot Experience

Multiple cities and counties have piloted Class 1 and 2 e-bike access on shared paths and found no significant safety problems.

Boulder, Colorado conducted a formal pilot study allowing Class 1 and 2 e-bikes on paved city trails. The City found few e-cyclists among total trail users and no reports of bike collisions during the study period. Boulder then adopted a permanent rule allowing e-bikes on paved trails within city limits while retaining restrictions on designated open-space areas.

Park City, Utah ran a one-year pilot in 2015 limiting Class 1 and 2 e-bikes to paved trails wider than eight feet. The pilot included data collection: trail counts, field observations, intercept surveys, speed counts, and police report review. The city subsequently allowed electric bicycles on all paved multiuse trails and on soft-surface trails wider than five feet, with a 15 mph speed limit for all users.

Douglas County, Colorado aligned its open space rules with Colorado state law (which mirrors Maryland in allowing Class 1 and 2 on bicycle paths). A county spokesperson reported "zero real issues" following implementation.

Arizona adopted a statewide framework in 2019 allowing e-bikes with a top speed of 20 mph on trails statewide, with local authority to restrict. Scottsdale, Arizona specifically allows Class 1 and 2 bikes on shared use trails as of December 2018.

Source: Rails-to-Trails Conservancy E-Bikes Toolbox; GovTech, "A Look at E-Bike Policies in Cities Across Colorado" (October 2024); Boulder County E-Bike Pilot Study.

---

## Federal Agency Alignment

The National Park Service issued rules in 2020 formally allowing Class 1, 2, and 3 e-bikes on any park terrain open to other bicycles. The NPS Federal Register notice explicitly stated that categorically prohibiting e-bikes on trails "runs counter to evidence suggesting that impacts from e-bikes are more like impacts from traditional bicycles than motor vehicles." The Bureau of Land Management followed with equivalent rules. These represent the federal government's considered policy judgment that Class 1 and 2 e-bikes belong in the same regulatory category as conventional bicycles for path access purposes.

Source: Federal Register, General Provisions; Electric Bicycles (November 2, 2020). NPS e-bikes page (nps.gov).

---

## Enforcement Focus vs. Equipment Focus

Multiple jurisdictions that studied the issue concluded that behavior-based enforcement is more effective than equipment-based restrictions. Boulder County staff explicitly stated that in congested situations, "a bicycle travelling 5 mph may be unsafe" -- meaning speed and behavior, not vehicle type, is the relevant safety variable. This supports a regulatory approach that focuses on speed limits and yielding rules applicable to all trail users rather than categorical equipment bans. Frederick's ordinance already has section 33-6 operating regulations that could be applied to this end.

---

## Honest Caveat: What the Research Does and Does Not Show

There is no east coast study of the Boulder/Park City type -- a formal city-conducted pilot specifically measuring e-bike safety on shared paths before and after access was granted. The Colorado pilots remain the best-controlled evidence in the field.

What the east coast and Maryland regional record provides is extensive real-world practice: Montgomery County Parks, the C&O Canal, the Capital Crescent Trail, and Virginia state parks all permit Class 1 and 2 access without adverse incident reports in the public record. Montgomery County ran a formal pilot and made access permanent by unanimous board vote. The absence of documented problems in those systems is itself evidence.
