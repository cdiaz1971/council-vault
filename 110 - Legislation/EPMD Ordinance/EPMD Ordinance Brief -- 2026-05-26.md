# EPMD Ordinance Brief -- 2026-05-26

## Summary

Draft ordinance G-26-____ regulates Electrically Powered Micromobility Devices (EPMDs) citywide. The ordinance has legitimate public safety goals but contains three provisions that carry real equity and legal risk. The shared-use path ban is the most consequential problem: it defaults to prohibition on all City-owned paths and trails unless the City affirmatively posts permitting signs, which inverts Maryland state law and creates a transportation burden that lands hardest on lower-income and immigrant residents in District 2 who depend on e-bikes as daily transportation. The executive summary's claim of no discernible equity impact is wrong and should be challenged directly.

---

## What the Ordinance Does

The ordinance adds a new Chapter 33, Article II to the City Code and repeals Chapter 22, Article VI. It covers all EPMDs: e-bikes (Class 1, 2, and 3), e-scooters, e-cargo bikes, EPAMDs, and related devices. Key operative provisions:

**Downtown sidewalk ban (section 33-5(g)).** EPMDs prohibited on sidewalks in the downtown commercial and business districts. Targeted and defensible -- pedestrian conflicts in a concentrated commercial zone are a real problem.

**Commercial rental ban (sections 33-10 through 33-13).** All shareable, dockless e-bike and e-scooter operations (Bird-style) are prohibited citywide. Codifies existing practice. Not controversial.

**E-moto ban (section 33-5(f)).** High-powered electric motorcycles prohibited on all travel ways. Well-grounded and supported by AMAC. The e-moto definition is a significant improvement over earlier drafts.

**Shared-use path ban (section 33-5(j)).** A person may not use or operate an EPMD on City-owned paths, shared-use pathways, or trails unless the City has posted signs affirmatively permitting such use. The default is prohibition. Nothing in the ordinance requires those signs to be posted or sets a timeline for doing so. This is the critical provision.

**Carroll Creek Park (section 33-5(k)).** Same opt-in default as shared paths. EPMDs banned in Carroll Creek Park unless signs permit.

**Passenger prohibition (section 33-6(e)).** Unlawful to operate an EPMD with more passengers than the device was designed to accommodate by the manufacturer. As written, this bans passengers on devices not factory-configured for them -- capturing cargo bikes, bikes with child seats, and other modified-but-safe configurations.

---

## The State Law Problem

Maryland Transportation Article section 21-1205.2 (enacted 2019) establishes that electric bicycles may be operated wherever bicycles are allowed to travel, including bike lanes. A local authority or state agency with jurisdiction over a bicycle path may prohibit the operation of a Class 1 or Class 2 electric bicycle on the bicycle path -- but that is a permissive authority to restrict, not a mandate. A Class 3 electric bicycle may not be operated on a bicycle path unless the path is within or adjacent to a highway right-of-way or allowed by a local authority with jurisdiction.

The draft inverts the state law default. Maryland's baseline: e-bikes go where bicycles go, unless a local authority restricts it. The ordinance's baseline: e-bikes are prohibited on shared-use paths unless the City affirmatively permits it with signage. This is a more restrictive posture than state law requires, and for Class 1 and Class 2 e-bikes in particular -- which state law treats as functionally equivalent to conventional bicycles on most facilities -- it is a significant departure.

The ordinance also flattens the three-class distinction. State law restricts Class 3 e-bikes (pedal-assist up to 28 mph) more tightly than Class 1 and 2 (both capped at 20 mph). The draft treats all classes identically under the path ban. A low-speed Class 1 pedal-assist e-bike -- the kind most commonly owned by working-class commuters -- gets the same restriction as a faster Class 3 bike. That flattening is not required by state law and is a pure policy choice by the administration.

Constituent Raymond Brown (204 West College Terrace) correctly identified this in writing on May 26, 2026: a personally-owned Class 1 pedal-assist e-bike would be prohibited where a conventional bicycle is welcome. His framing is accurate and grounded in state law.

City Attorney Rachel Gray should be asked whether the opt-in default for Class 1 and 2 e-bikes is within the City's authority under section 21-1205.2, or whether it conflicts with the state's baseline permissive rule.

## District 2 Equity Implications

District 2 has a poverty rate 1.5 times the county rate, a foreign-born population 1.4 times the county rate, and a median household income at roughly 75% of the county median. City-wide, 43% of households fall below the ALICE threshold. For a significant share of District 2 residents, e-bikes are not a recreational amenity -- they are a primary commuting tool. A used Class 1 e-bike runs $400-$800 and eliminates car operating costs for short-distance urban trips.

Frederick's shared-use path network -- including Carroll Creek Linear Park and the paths connecting west Frederick neighborhoods to downtown employment -- functions as transportation infrastructure for carless and car-lite households. If those paths default to e-bike prohibited with no timeline or obligation for the City to post permitting signs, the ordinance creates a transportation burden that lands disproportionately on lower-income and immigrant residents.

The passenger prohibition compounds this. Cargo bikes and bikes with child seats are the household transportation solution for parents who cannot afford car-dependent childcare logistics. The current language would capture a parent using a properly-installed aftermarket child seat.

The executive summary states the ordinance "creates no discernible effect on individuals from an equity perspective." That assessment is wrong and should be challenged on the record.

## AMAC Letter -- Key Points

The Active Mobility Advisory Committee (May 5, 2026) identified five issues. Status in the current draft:

**E-moto definition gap** -- resolved. The draft defines e-motos and ties them to motorized minibike status under state law, exactly as AMAC recommended.

**Road speed limit language (section 33-6(h))** -- unresolved. As written, the language advising e-bikes to stay off roads where speed limits exceed the vehicle's top speed would ban EPMDs from 25 mph local streets. Needs to mirror state bicycle rules (limited-access highways only).

**Bell mandate (section 33-7(c))** -- needs confirmation. AMAC recommended striking the bell mandate in section 33-7(c) and relying on the "audible signal" requirement in section 33-6(f) only. The current draft as read does not appear to include a separate bell requirement in section 33-7(c) -- but this needs to be confirmed with staff, as the AMAC letter specifically flagged it.

**Passenger prohibition (section 33-6(e))** -- unresolved. AMAC's recommended fix is the right one: permit passengers when the device is specifically designed or properly modified to carry them.

**Shared-use path ban** -- AMAC's most significant unresolved concern. Letter notes it creates a more restrictive standard than state law and cuts against the City's stated transportation goals.

---

## Questions to Raise at Committee

Three questions are worth pressing:

First, on the path ban default: the current draft requires affirmative City action to permit e-bikes on shared paths. What is the timeline and process for the City to post permitting signs on paths where e-bike access is appropriate? Without a clear answer, the ordinance operates as a de facto permanent ban.

Second, on state law alignment: the ordinance treats Class 1, 2, and 3 e-bikes identically under the path ban. State law treats them differently. Has the City Attorney reviewed whether the opt-in default for Class 1 and 2 e-bikes is within the City's authority under Transportation Article section 21-1205.2, or whether it conflicts with the state's baseline permissive rule?

Third, on the equity impact statement: staff assessed no discernible equity impact. District 2 has a significant population of residents who depend on e-bikes for daily transportation, and Frederick's shared-use path network functions as commuting infrastructure for those residents. What data was used to support the no-impact assessment?

---

## Recommended Posture

Support the ordinance's legitimate goals: e-moto prohibition, downtown pedestrian safety, commercial rental ban. Push for three specific fixes:

1. Flip the path default from opt-in to opt-out for Class 1 and 2 e-bikes, consistent with Maryland state law. Class 3 may retain a more restrictive posture given the higher top speed.
2. Revise the passenger provision to permit passengers when the device is designed or properly modified for that purpose.
3. Correct the road speed language to mirror state bicycle rules rather than creating a de facto ban from 25 mph local streets.

Raymond Brown's constituent email and the AMAC letter together provide a strong public record for all three asks. Neither source is fringe -- both track state law and established best practice.

## Policy Arguments: Class 1 and 2 Path Access and Bell Requirement

*See separate policy arguments section below -- included here for integrated reference.*

### Argument 1: Speed Parity With Conventional Bicycles

Class 1 and 2 e-bikes are capped at 20 mph under Maryland law and under the federal consumer product safety framework. In practice, real-world average speeds are substantially lower. A Swiss study cited by the Rails-to-Trails Conservancy found that average e-bike speeds on shared paths were 14 mph, versus 8.7 mph for conventional cyclists. The top speeds of most e-bike users were well below the 20 mph cap. A 2018 Boulder County, Colorado pilot study found that e-bikes were only marginally faster than conventional bikes on uphill grades -- 13.8 mph versus 12.9 mph on average -- and slower on downhills. The speed differential between a Class 1 e-bike and a fast conventional cyclist is minimal. Treating them categorically differently is not supported by actual observed speed data.

Source: Rails-to-Trails Conservancy E-Bikes Toolbox (railstotrails.org); Boulder County E-Bike Pilot Study, 2018 (assets.bouldercounty.gov).

### Argument 2: Peer Jurisdiction Experience

Multiple cities and counties have piloted Class 1 and 2 e-bike access on shared paths and found no significant safety problems:

Boulder, Colorado conducted a formal pilot study allowing Class 1 and 2 e-bikes on paved city trails. The City found few e-cyclists among total trail users and no reports of bike collisions during the study period. Boulder then adopted a permanent rule allowing e-bikes on paved trails within city limits while retaining restrictions on designated open-space areas.

Park City, Utah ran a one-year pilot in 2015 limiting Class 1 and 2 e-bikes to paved trails wider than eight feet. The pilot included data collection: trail counts, field observations, intercept surveys, speed counts, and police report review. The city subsequently allowed electric bicycles on all paved multiuse trails and on soft-surface trails wider than five feet, with a 15 mph speed limit for all users.

Douglas County, Colorado aligned its open space rules with Colorado state law (which mirrors Maryland in allowing Class 1 and 2 on bicycle paths). A county spokesperson reported "zero real issues" following implementation. Officials noted that complaints about fast cyclists generally did not specify e-bikes, and that feared problems -- more crashes, fires, riders getting out of control -- did not materialize.

Arizona adopted a statewide framework in 2019 allowing e-bikes with a top speed of 20 mph on trails statewide, with local authority to restrict. Scottsdale, Arizona specifically allows Class 1 and 2 bikes on shared use trails as of December 2018.

Source: Rails-to-Trails Conservancy E-Bikes Toolbox; GovTech, "A Look at E-Bike Policies in Cities Across Colorado" (October 2024); Boulder County E-Bike Pilot Study.

### Argument 3: Federal Agency Alignment

The National Park Service issued rules in 2020 formally allowing Class 1, 2, and 3 e-bikes on any park terrain open to other bicycles. The NPS Federal Register notice explicitly stated that categorically prohibiting e-bikes on trails "runs counter to evidence suggesting that impacts from e-bikes are more like impacts from traditional bicycles than motor vehicles." The Bureau of Land Management followed with equivalent rules. These are not fringe positions -- they represent the federal government's considered policy judgment that Class 1 and 2 e-bikes belong in the same regulatory category as conventional bicycles for path access purposes.

Source: Federal Register, General Provisions; Electric Bicycles (November 2, 2020). NPS e-bikes page (nps.gov).

### Argument 4: Enforcement Focus vs. Equipment Focus

Multiple jurisdictions that studied the issue concluded that behavior-based enforcement is more effective than equipment-based restrictions. Boulder County staff explicitly stated that in congested situations, "a bicycle travelling 5 mph may be unsafe" -- meaning speed and behavior, not vehicle type, is the relevant safety variable. This supports a regulatory approach that focuses on speed limits and yielding rules applicable to all trail users rather than categorical equipment bans. Frederick's ordinance already has section 33-6 operating regulations that could be applied to this end.

Source: Boulder County E-Bike Pilot Study; BLM Federal Register notice.

### Argument 5: Equity and Displacement Effect

Banning e-bikes from shared paths while permitting conventional bicycles creates a two-tier system that favors wealthier riders. Recreational cyclists who ride for sport on high-end conventional bikes retain full access. Commuting residents who chose e-bikes specifically for transportation assistance -- the motor helps a lower-income worker with no car cover distances that would otherwise require transit or a car -- lose access. This is the inverse of an equitable transportation policy. For District 2 specifically, Carroll Creek Linear Park and connecting paths are commuting infrastructure, not recreation amenities.

### On the Bell Requirement

The AMAC letter recommends striking the specific bell mandate from section 33-7(c) and relying on the "audible signal" language in section 33-6(f). The arguments for this position are well-grounded:

**Handlebar geometry.** Modern e-bikes, cargo bikes, and bikes with integrated lighting or shifter systems often have crowded handlebar configurations that make bell installation impractical or impossible. A rigid bell requirement creates an equipment compliance problem that does not exist for conventional bikes.

**Unpredictable pedestrian reaction.** The AMAC letter explicitly flags that bells cause unpredictable pedestrian reactions. This is not a fringe concern -- it has been noted in cycling advocacy literature for decades. The D.C. Capital Bicycle Club documented in 1887 that the bell "is construed as an unauthorized order to clear the road" and has the effect of startling pedestrians, which may make a collision more likely, not less. Research from Georgia Tech on bicycle bell recognizability notes that an appropriate warning signal "should not cause other road users and pedestrians to startle." Voice signals ("on your left") give more contextual information and allow pedestrians to make a directional judgment.

**The "audible signal" standard already covers the intent.** Section 33-6(f) already requires operators to "give an audible signal before overtaking and passing a pedestrian or human-powered device." This is the substantive safety requirement. The bell mandate in section 33-7(c) is an equipment-specific implementation of that requirement that creates compliance burdens without adding safety. Palm Coast, Florida's 2025 ordinance uses the audible signal standard without mandating a specific device. This is the more defensible approach.

**Enforcement practicality.** A bell-specific equipment requirement creates a citation category for officers to enforce that has no meaningful relationship to actual dangerous behavior. An officer writing a $100 citation for a missing bell on a compliant, slow-moving commuter e-bike diverts enforcement resources from actual safety problems.

Source: AMAC letter (May 5, 2026); TheWashCycle, "Bells and On Your Left" (citing 1887 CBC complaint); Georgia Tech, "Recognizability and perceived urgency of bicycle bells"; Palm Coast, FL e-bike ordinance (October 2025).
