# Renter Protection Package: The Case for It

This package gives Frederick renters three things they don't have today. First, they get to see the full truth about a home before they commit to it. Second, they get a fair shot at renting it and a reasonable chance of staying. Third, when a landlord breaks the rules, the City makes it right, not the tenant, and the family isn't put on the street in the process.

None of this sets the rent. Nothing in it tells a landlord what to charge. It asks landlords to be honest, to use a fair process, and to fix what they are already legally required to fix. Then it gives the City the tools to make that stick. Rockville adopted most of this in July 2026, so a Maryland city has already shown it can be done.

The backup research for every section (peer laws, legal risk, studies, local data, opposition, and the council map) is in the `_Sources` folder. Start at [[Tenant Protection - File Index]].

This note covers what the ordinance would require and who it helps, and it backs each part with evidence. It also prepares the answers to opposition, to staff pushback, and to the "this will make rent more expensive" argument. Ordinance language comes next, drafted together in chat.

---

## The recommendation

Pass this as one ordinance. A working title is the Frederick Renters' Rights Ordinance. Real Rent becomes its first article, and three new articles follow:

- **Know Before You Sign** (disclosure)
- **Fair Screening and a Stable Home**
- **Rights That Hold Up** (enforcement and relocation)

Draft each article so it can stand on its own. If one piece draws an amendment at Second Read, the Council can strike it without unraveling the rest. The severability clause backs that up.

You asked to be convinced either way. One ordinance is the stronger choice for four reasons.

**The parts share the same machinery.** Real Rent already needs a definitions section, a coverage rule that reaches every rental unit, and an enforcement system that works without a private lawsuit. The disclosure and screening rules need exactly the same three things. Passing them separately means building that machinery twice, holding two legal reviews, and possibly ending up with two enforcement systems that don't match. Passing them together means one definition of "covered unit," one restitution process, one set of penalties, and one department in charge.

**The coverage fix only has to happen once.** Frederick's rental licensing chapter exempts buildings whose certificates of occupancy are eight years old or newer (§ 12.5-76(a)(5)). Newer buildings are where fee stacking is most common. They are also where corporate owners using pricing software are most likely to operate. Real Rent's revisions already close that gap. Every protection in this package depends on the same fix.

**One campaign is easier to win than three.** Every ordinance means a workshop, a First Read, a Second Read, public comment, a fiscal note, and a round of meetings with landlords. Running that cycle three times gives the opposition three chances to organize, and it tires out the advocates who need to show up. A single package with a single story ("know the price, get a fair shot, and have rights that work") is easier for the public to follow and for allies to defend.

**It is harder to pick apart.** When each protection stands alone, the easiest one passes and the rest stall. Together, swing members can support the whole and adjust the edges by amendment, which still produces a law.

**The cost of combining** is time. Real Rent was workshopped in July and has been on a Q4 2026 track. Folding the new parts in likely pushes Real Rent's First Read into early 2027. If the timing becomes a problem, there is a fallback. The articles are drafted to stand alone, so Real Rent can be pulled out and introduced first without redrafting anything. Decide that at the point of introduction, not now.

**The other risk** is that the most contested piece drags down the whole. That piece is likely relocation costs or a commission with power to award money. The answer is the severable structure described above, plus sequencing the effective dates (see "Effective dates" under Part 3). Contested provisions can start later, after staffing and rules are in place, without holding up the disclosure and screening rules.

## Who rents in Frederick

The 2020 Census counted 13,724 renter-occupied homes in the City of Frederick, 46 percent of all households.[^census2020] Nearly half the city rents. These protections cover a large share of the city, not a niche group.

The median gross rent in the city, including utilities, was $1,764 a month in the 2020 to 2024 American Community Survey.[^quickfacts] To keep that rent at 30 percent of income, the standard measure of affordability, a household needs about $70,500 a year. United Way's 2026 ALICE report puts 43 percent of City households below what it costs to survive in Frederick County, compared with 32 percent countywide.[^alice] Many renters are one surprise fee, one rent increase, or one forced move from falling behind.

Frederick is also a city of many languages. Just over a quarter of residents age five and older (26.3 percent) speak a language other than English at home, and about one in five was born outside the United States.[^quickfacts] In District 2 the foreign-born share is 1.4 times the county rate, and the poverty rate is 1.5 times the county rate. These households are the most likely to sign a lease they cannot fully read. They are also the least likely to know which rights they have and the most exposed to pressure from a landlord who knows that.

Children live in these rentals. United Way's 2025 report found that 72 percent of Frederick County children in households below the ALICE threshold live in rental housing.[^alice] When a rental household is forced to move, children are usually part of the move.

## Part 1. Know Before You Sign

### What renters face now

Picture a family looking for a two-bedroom apartment in Frederick today. They see an advertised rent. They usually don't see the monthly fees that will be added on top, such as amenity, trash, "technology," or pest fees. They don't know what the unit rented for last year, or how much the owner has raised rents in the building over time. They can't easily learn whether the building has open code violations, whether the owner is in foreclosure, or whether the utilities they will pay run $80 a month or $300.

If the conversation with the leasing office happened in Spanish, the lease probably arrives in English anyway, often at the moment of signing, with a line of people behind them. Once they sign, pay the deposit, and move their things in, they have little leverage left. The cost of moving again keeps them in place even when the deal turns out worse than promised.

This is not just bad manners in the market. It is a well-documented way of extracting money. In a randomized experiment with millions of StubHub customers, people who saw mandatory fees only at checkout spent about 21 percent more than people shown the full price from the start. Even experienced, repeat customers spent 15 percent more.[^drip] A hidden or late-revealed cost reliably moves money from the buyer to the seller. Renting a home is a far bigger purchase than a concert ticket, and the switching costs are far higher.

### What the ordinance would require

**A standard disclosure, before any commitment.** Before taking an application fee, and again before the lease is signed, the landlord gives the applicant a City form of no more than two pages. It lists:

- the rent and any concessions
- every fee, marked mandatory or optional, with its amount and how often it is charged
- which utilities the tenant pays, with an estimate of what they have cost
- the total monthly cost
- the same information at every renewal

Seattle's 2026 fee ordinance uses the same two-page design and translates the City form into the five most common non-English languages.[^seattle] Seattle requires the form in every listing and application. Rockville requires all mandatory fees to appear in every advertised price.[^rockville]

**Three years of rent history.** Before signing, the landlord discloses what the unit rented for over the past three years, the increases it saw, and the average yearly increase across the building. Rockville adopted this rule.[^rockville] It costs the landlord nothing, since the information is already in their ledger, and it gives the renter something real to negotiate with. A renter who learns the building has averaged 9 percent increases a year knows what is coming, and can choose differently before committing.

**The condition and legal status of the building.** The disclosure includes:

- code citations on the unit and common areas in the past 12 months
- pending code enforcement or court actions against the property
- utility shutoff notices
- foreclosure filings

Chicago has required disclosure of code violations and foreclosure filings for years under its Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance (Chapter 5-12).[^chicago] A family deserves to know before moving in that the building has an open mold case or that the bank may take it over in six months.

**Who is responsible.** The disclosure names:

- the owner and the managing agent
- a 24-hour local contact for emergencies (Rockville requires one in buildings without an on-site manager)
- the rental license number

When ownership changes, the new owner notifies every tenant with the same information. Too often, tenants with a broken heater cannot even find out who owns their building.

**Time to read the lease.** The applicant gets the proposed lease at least two days before signing and the signed copy within three days after. Rockville adopted both timelines, shortening the delivery window for the signed copy from ten days to three.[^rockville]

**A lease in the language of the deal.** When a lease was mainly negotiated in a language other than English, the landlord provides a written translation before signing. California has long required this for leases negotiated in Spanish and several other languages. Rockville did not include it. Frederick can be the first Maryland city to make it law, and it matters most to the immigrant households the City Values Act was written to protect. To make compliance easy, the City publishes a model lease and a plain-language renter rights summary in Spanish and the other languages most spoken by Frederick renters. Landlords can use those forms for free.

### Why this is on solid legal ground

The Maryland Tenants' Bill of Rights, which every landlord must attach to every lease, states that local jurisdictions may require additional lease content.[^billrights] State law already requires leases to identify the landlord or agent, to state who pays which utilities, and to disclose prior-year utility totals for landlord-billed utilities. This article builds on those requirements instead of creating a new category of regulation. It is the lowest-risk part of the package.

### Who it helps

It helps every renter who signs a lease in Frederick. The biggest gains go to households with the least room for error, meaning families at or below the ALICE threshold, for whom a $150 surprise fee is a missed bill. It helps renters who speak limited English, who today sign documents they cannot fully read. It also helps renters who don't know how to look up code records, which describes almost everyone.

It helps honest landlords as well. A landlord who already advertises a true price loses business today to a competitor that advertises a lower number and adds fees at signing. A standard disclosure puts everyone on the same footing.

### What it costs and who pays

Almost nothing, and the landlord already holds nearly all of the information. Code citations are records the City itself issues. Rent history sits in the landlord's own accounting system. The landlord already knows the fees, because they set them. The City's cost is a one-time project to create the form and model lease, translate them, and post them online, plus updates when the law changes. A landlord who uses the City's forms has very little added work.

## Part 2. Fair Screening and a Stable Home

### What renters face now

**The screening report.** Most landlords decide on applicants using a report bought from a screening company. Those reports are often wrong. From January 2019 to September 2022 the Consumer Financial Protection Bureau received about 26,700 complaints about tenant screening, and more than 17,200 of them concerned incorrect information on a report.[^cfpb] Renters described being denied because of a twin brother's debt, a felony they never committed, or a misdemeanor listed as a felony. They paid application fee after application fee as landlord after landlord turned them down on the same false information, and they had no practical way to fix the report in time to get the apartment.[^cfpb]

**The eviction filing.** In Maryland, filing a failure-to-pay-rent case is cheap and routine. More than 400,000 such cases were filed statewide in fiscal year 2023.[^mdfilings] Many landlords use these filings to collect rent, not to remove anyone. The tenant pays, the case is dismissed, and the family stays. The filing still lands on screening reports. A study of Pennsylvania tenants whose cases were dismissed, withdrawn, or won found that 8 in 10 said the filing limited their future housing options. Over half of those who searched were told by a landlord that the filing was why they were turned down.[^recordcosts] A tenant who did nothing wrong in court is treated as if they had been evicted.

**The year-to-year gamble.** Most Frederick renters sign one-year leases. Under state law, the rent can go up by any amount at each renewal with 90 days' notice.[^billrights] There is no promise of a second year. A family that wants to keep its children in the same school for three years has no way to plan for it.

**What a forced move does.** The damage from a forced move reaches well beyond the move itself. A study that linked Houston eviction records to school records found that children in households facing eviction filings were more likely to leave the district or switch schools, often to schools with fewer resources and higher turnover. They also had more absences, and those who switched schools had more suspensions.[^houston] Research by Matthew Desmond found that workers who were forced to move were 11 to 22 percent more likely to lose their jobs, and mothers who were evicted reported higher rates of depression two years later.[^desmond] Instability does not stay in one part of a family's life.

### What the ordinance would require

**Clear, fair applications.**

- The landlord posts written screening criteria before collecting any application fee, so applicants know whether they qualify before they pay.
- Landlords may not deny an applicant because of an eviction filing that was dismissed, withdrawn, or decided in the tenant's favor, or because of an eviction older than a set look-back period. Philadelphia adopted this rule in 2021. Rockville took a different route and banned the eviction-history question on applications.[^rockville] Restricting how the information is used, rather than banning the question, is the safer design because a federal appeals court struck down Seattle's ban on asking about criminal history on free-speech grounds. The look-back period is a policy choice for you. See [[Maryland Legal Baseline and Local Authority - Tenant Protection]].
- If an application is denied, the landlord gives the reason in writing and names the screening company used.
- The applicant can get a copy of the screening report and has a short window to dispute an error before the unit goes to someone else.
- When a landlord requires a guarantor, they explain why in writing (Rockville).

**Fair chance screening for small landlords.** Maryland already requires landlords with more than five units who check criminal history to make a conditional offer first and to limit what they can consider. Smaller landlords are not covered.[^billrights] This article can extend the same process to landlords with five or fewer units, so a person's chance at a home doesn't depend on the size of the landlord's portfolio. This will be the most contested item in Part 2, so the package should be able to pass with or without it.

**A real chance to stay.**

- The landlord offers a two-year initial lease. The tenant may choose a shorter term, and the landlord may offer a shorter one for a stated reason that the tenant can challenge. If rent will rise during the two years, the increase is written into the lease from the start. Rockville adopted this structure.[^rockville]
- A continuing tenant's rent can go up only once in any 12-month period (Rockville). This does not cap the amount. It stops mid-lease and back-to-back increases.

**Leaving early without a penalty when life changes.** A tenant may end the lease with 30 days' notice, with any termination charge capped at one month's rent, in these situations:

- an involuntary job relocation
- a unit that has been unfit for 60 days
- a suspended or revoked rental license
- unrepaired health and safety violations
- the death of a primary wage earner
- a change in disability status
- entry into inpatient treatment
- an offer of a unit in subsidized or senior housing

The list follows Rockville's adopted rule, with the subsidized and senior housing item added.[^rockville] The added item matters. A family that finally reaches the top of a waiting list should not have to turn down an affordable home because breaking their current lease would cost three months' rent.

**Lease terms that cut both ways.** A lease may not:

- make the tenant pay the landlord's attorney fees unless a court awards them, finds them reasonable, and the clause applies to both sides
- charge court costs above actual costs
- treat attorney fees as "rent" that must be paid before a tenant can stop an eviction

Rockville and Montgomery County both have similar rules.[^rockville]

**The right to organize.** Tenants may:

- meet, organize, and share information
- use common meeting rooms for tenant meetings without a fee
- authorize a tenant organization to file complaints on their behalf

Harassment, cutting services, or other pressure because of organizing is prohibited. Maryland already treats threatening to report a tenant's immigration status as unlawful retaliation, and as a crime when it is used to extract money or labor.[^billrights] The ordinance names it as prohibited harassment as well, so a local remedy exists.

### Who it helps

It helps people with records, including old eviction filings and old convictions, who have paid what they owed or served their time and still cannot get through the door. It helps families with children who need stability to keep a child in one school. It helps seniors and people with disabilities whose circumstances change and who should not be trapped by a lease. It helps immigrant tenants, who are the most exposed to pressure tactics and the least likely to complain without protection. And it helps every applicant who has paid fee after fee only to be rejected on a false report.

### What it costs and who pays

Most of this costs a landlord nothing. Posting criteria, giving a written reason, and skipping a question on a form are changes in practice, not in spending.

A two-year lease offer can benefit landlords, because tenant turnover is costly for them through vacancy, cleaning, repainting, marketing, and leasing staff time. A two-year tenant is a predictable tenant.

The early-termination rule shifts a limited, capped cost to the landlord in specific hardship cases. It is narrow, and the one-month cap leaves the landlord time to re-rent.

The only provision with a real cost argument is fair chance screening for small landlords. That is why it should be drafted to be severable.

## Part 3. Rights That Hold Up: Enforcement and Relocation

### What renters face now

Maryland gives tenants real rights on paper, including the warranty of habitability, rent escrow for serious defects, protection from retaliation, and a ban on lockouts.[^billrights] Using those rights means going to court. A tenant with a broken furnace or a mold problem must give notice, wait, file in District Court, and often pay rent into a court escrow account while the case moves forward. Working families with hourly jobs and no lawyer often don't do it.

The research shows what happens when repairs don't get made. A Boston study published in *Health Affairs* found that tenant reports of asthma triggers such as mold, pests, and moisture were concentrated in neighborhoods with more Black and Hispanic residents, and that fewer of those cases were repaired. The gap in mold cases was widening. The authors called for stronger enforcement and landlord accountability.[^asthma]

The City's own enforcement tools have a gap too. Real Rent's original private lawsuit provision runs into the *Aleti* decision. In 2022, Maryland's highest court declined to let tenants recover rent paid to an unlicensed Baltimore landlord because the city ordinance did not clearly create that remedy.[^aleti] When the City does act, the punishment can land on the tenant. If a license is revoked or a unit is declared unfit, the family has to leave, and nothing in Frederick's code today says who pays for the hotel room, the moving truck, or the deposit on the next apartment. Rockville's staff reported months-long court delays on contested citations and almost no recent license revocations.[^rvstaff] Weak enforcement and enforcement that displaces people are both failures.

### What the ordinance would require

**The City authorizes the repair.** When a City inspection finds a violation and the landlord misses the deadline to fix it, the City may authorize the tenant to hire a licensed contractor to make the repair. The tenant deducts the cost from rent, up to two months' rent. Rockville adopted this rule.[^rockville] Because the City authorizes the remedy, the tenant doesn't have to win a lawsuit first. That is what makes this approach a strong answer to *Aleti*.

**Repair deadlines matched to the danger.** The ordinance sets correction times by severity. Life-threatening conditions such as no heat in winter, a gas leak, or no water must be fixed within 24 to 48 hours. Ordinary violations get 15 to 30 days, with shorter deadlines for repeat offenders. Montgomery County uses timelines like these.[^tpage] Frederick's property maintenance code may already set some of them, which needs to be checked before drafting.

**Refunds ordered by the City.** When a landlord charges a prohibited fee or violates the disclosure rules, the City orders the money returned to every affected tenant, with interest. This works as a condition of the rental license and through municipal infraction consent orders. A landlord who repays every tenant in the building quickly gets a reduced penalty. Seattle uses this incentive to get money back to tenants fast.[^seattle]

**Records, or a presumption against you.** Landlords keep advertisements, leases, disclosures, and fee records for three years. If the records are missing when the City investigates, the law presumes a violation. Seattle adopted this rule.[^seattle] It puts the burden of proof on the party that holds the records.

**Penalties with teeth.** Violations are municipal infractions. Penalties escalate for repeat violations, up to the $5,000 maximum that state law now allows.[^infraction] A license is denied after repeated uncured violations or a single serious health and safety citation, and buildings with prior failures face more inspections. Rockville uses both measures.[^rockville]

**A place to bring a complaint.** There are two options.

- **A Landlord-Tenant Commission.** Rockville has had one for years. It has two landlords, two tenants, and three public members. After staff investigation and attempted conciliation, it can hold hearings, compel documents, and award up to $5,000 per affected unit.[^rockville] Its balanced membership is a political strength.
- **A hearing officer**, or an expanded role for an existing City board. This is simpler to stand up, but it carries less public legitimacy.

Whether Frederick's Charter supports a body that awards money is the biggest legal question in the package. A hearing officer applying license conditions carries less risk than a commission awarding damages. The risk analysis for both is in [[Maryland Legal Baseline and Local Authority - Tenant Protection]].

**Relocation when the home is lost.** When the City declares a unit unfit for six months or less, the landlord provides temporary housing (a hotel, a per diem, or a comparable unit) plus moving and storage.

A permanent move is triggered when:

- the license is revoked and the owner stops renting
- the displacement lasts six months or more
- redevelopment pushes out a lower-income household before the lease ends

In those cases the landlord pays:

- the full deposit with interest
- prorated rent
- three months of HUD fair market rent
- actual moving costs
- an added amount for households with a senior, a person with a disability, or a child

These payments are owed even if the tenant is behind on rent. Exceptions cover natural disasters, conditions the tenant caused, and lawful evictions. Displaced tenants also get the right to return on similar terms once the unit is fixed.

If the landlord doesn't pay, the City pays the tenant directly, bills the landlord, and records unpaid costs as a lien collected like property taxes. Rockville adopted this full structure.[^rockville] The lien matters. Minneapolis advanced relocation payments without one and recovered only about a quarter of them.[^rvstaff]

**A named department, staff, and a funding source.** The ordinance names the department in charge, sets an annual public report date, and identifies how enforcement is funded (see the cost section below).

**Effective dates.** The articles take effect in stages:

- **First:** the disclosure and fee rules, timed to lease renewal cycles.
- **Next:** data reporting, at each building's next license renewal.
- **Last:** the commission and relocation provisions, once rules and staffing are ready.

Staging lets the City build capacity without delaying everything else.

### Who it helps

It helps tenants in the worst buildings, who are usually the people with the fewest choices. That means families who can't afford to move, seniors on fixed incomes, and immigrant households afraid to complain. It also changes what enforcement means. Today the City's strongest tool, revoking a license, can leave the tenants worse off than the landlord. With relocation in place, the City can enforce the law without making families pay for a landlord's failure.

### What it costs and who pays

**Landlords who follow the law pay nothing under this article.** Repair-and-deduct only happens after a landlord ignores a City deadline. Refund orders only apply to fees that were illegal. Relocation is only owed when a landlord lets a unit become unfit or loses a license. These are costs of breaking the law, and the law-breaker pays them.

The City's costs are staff time to investigate, hold hearings, and track relocation cases, plus the money it advances for relocation before it is recovered through billing or the lien. There is a funding source for this. Rental licensing revenue ran about $1.73 million against a $300,000 projection through the third quarter of FY2026. Seattle funds three enforcement positions with a regulatory fee of about $11 per unit.[^seattle] A small, dedicated share of licensing revenue can fund the work, and the lien protects the City's advances.

## How Real Rent fits

Real Rent is the first promise of the package: **the price is the price.** It requires all mandatory fees to be included in the advertised and charged rent, separates truly optional services from disguised mandatory ones, bans junk fees, prohibits algorithmic rent-setting that uses competitors' private data, and regulates utility billing.

Inside the package, Real Rent gains three things. Its fee disclosures become part of the single Know Before You Sign form. Its enforcement problem (*Aleti*) is solved by Part 3's City-ordered refunds instead of a private lawsuit. And its coverage fix extends to every protection in the package. Real Rent was already the strongest-recognized piece from the July 9 workshop. Putting it first keeps that recognition and gives the rest of the package a familiar starting point.

## Answering "this will make rent more expensive"

This will be the main argument against the package. Answer it head on, because the honest answer is a strong one. It breaks down into three separate claims.

**Claim one: compliance costs will be passed on to renters.** Look at what the package actually requires of a landlord who follows the law: filling in a two-page form with information they already have, posting screening criteria, giving a written reason for a denial, offering a two-year lease, and skipping one question on an application. None of those has meaningful cost. The provisions that do cost money, which are repair-and-deduct, refunds, relocation, and penalties, fall only on landlords who broke the law. Turn the question back to the person raising it. Which requirement costs a law-abiding landlord money? If they name relocation, the answer is that relocation is only owed when a landlord lets a home become unfit to live in.

**Claim two: banning fees will just raise base rent.** Some of that will happen. Rockville's own staff expected some landlords to move banned fee revenue into base rent.[^rvstaff] That is the point of Real Rent, not a flaw in it. When the cost is in the rent, the renter can see it, compare it, and decide before signing. Hidden costs are what make people pay more. The StubHub experiment showed that shoppers who saw the full price up front spent less than those who saw fees at checkout.[^drip] A visible price lets the market do its job. Real Rent doesn't promise lower rent. It promises that what you're quoted is what you pay.

**Claim three: regulation will drive landlords out and reduce housing supply.** The research on tenant protections much stronger than these does not support that claim. A 2025 University of Minnesota study compared new housing permits in counties with good cause eviction laws in California, Oregon, and New Hampshire against nearby counties without them. It found no evidence that the protections reduced new development.[^cura] Research by the Urban Displacement Project found no discernible supply effect from just cause laws in Seattle and New Jersey, which have had them for decades.[^udp] Good cause eviction limits when a landlord can end a tenancy. This package asks for disclosure, fair process, and enforcement of repairs, which is a much lighter touch. If laws restricting eviction don't reduce supply, a two-page disclosure form won't either.

**What not to say.** Don't claim the package will lower rents. It won't, and opponents will hold you to it. The honest claim is better: renters will know the full cost before they sign, have a fair shot at the home, keep it longer, live in safer conditions, and get help when enforcement forces them out. Those outcomes are real, measurable, and worth the vote.

## Other arguments you will hear

**"State law already covers this."** State law covers a lot, and the package doesn't repeat it. Deposits, the 5 percent late fee cap, the basic application fee rule, and eviction court procedure stay with the state. What the package adds is coverage and enforcement where state law stops. State law applies its criminal screening rules only to landlords with more than five units, leaves lease content open to local requirements, and preserves stronger local notice protections in § 8-209.[^billrights] More importantly, state rights depend on a tenant going to court. This package lets the City enforce them.

**"This is anti-landlord."** Rockville built its version over a year of public process. Its Landlord-Tenant Commission gives landlords two seats out of seven. Most of this package rewards responsible landlords by stopping competitors from undercutting them with hidden fees and deferred maintenance. A landlord who already discloses fees, fixes things on time, and treats applicants fairly will barely notice this law.

**"Small landlords can't handle the paperwork."** The City provides the forms, the model lease, and the translations for free. Disclosure takes a few minutes per lease. The heavier rules can include small-landlord exemptions where they make sense. Rockville exempts landlords with only one unit in the city from its algorithmic pricing ban, for example. The fair chance extension is the provision aimed at small landlords, which is why it is drafted to be severable.

**"Wait for the rental licensing rewrite."** Council Member Hempel Irani's licensing rewrite is a separate effort with its own scope and timeline. Renters shouldn't wait for it. The package is drafted to work with whatever licensing structure the Council adopts, and it fixes the coverage gap that licensing currently leaves open. Coordinate on definitions and cross-references, not on timing.

**"Government shouldn't interfere in private contracts."** Every lease in Maryland is already shaped by law. The state bans confession-of-judgment clauses, sets deposit limits, and requires the Tenants' Bill of Rights to be attached. Contracts work best when both sides know what they are agreeing to and can enforce what they were promised. That is all this package does.

**From allies: "Why isn't rent stabilization in here?"** Be direct. Five votes to override a veto on rent stabilization don't exist on this Council. The package does what can pass now and builds toward what comes next. The rent history disclosure, and the unit-level rent and eviction reporting at license renewal (Rockville's Article V model), create the local data that a future stabilization debate will need. Good cause eviction is a 2027 General Assembly ask, because the 2026 bill failed and would only have authorized counties.

## What staff will raise, and how to answer

**Legal staff.** Expect questions on five points. Their review is advisory, and you decide how to proceed. The risk analysis for each point is in [[Maryland Legal Baseline and Local Authority - Tenant Protection]].

1. *Aleti*. The package does not rely on a locally created private lawsuit. Remedies run through City orders, license conditions, municipal infractions, and liens.
2. Charter authority for a body that awards money. This is the biggest open question. Ask for analysis of both the commission and hearing-officer options, and of how Rockville's charter authorizes its commission.
3. Lien authority for relocation costs.
4. Reaching units outside the licensing chapter. This relies on general police power if not on licensing.
5. Any conflict with state screening law for small landlords.

Sharing the specific questions above early, along with the risk analysis, will get more useful feedback than handing over a finished draft.

**Code enforcement and HHS.** Expect concerns about staffing and caseload. The answer is staged effective dates, a named department, a dedicated funding line, and enforcement focused on complaints and repeat violators rather than routine audits of every building. Ask staff what they need, put it in the fiscal note, and fund it. A law that staff can't enforce does renters no good.

**Finance.** Expect questions about the City advancing relocation money before it is repaid. The lien answers most of the risk, and Minneapolis shows the result without one. Ask Finance to model relocation exposure using actual revocation and unfit-unit numbers. Frederick likely sees few cases a year, and the fiscal note should show that.

**Communications and language access.** Translating forms and a renter landing page is a one-time project with small upkeep. Clara Jackson's survey already proposed a renter landing page and handbook, and they can launch with the ordinance.

## What success looks like

Write these measures into the ordinance, with an annual public report from the named department and a date for it.

- **Disclosure compliance.** The share of sampled listings and leases with complete disclosures. The gap between advertised and actual monthly cost.
- **Money returned.** Refunds ordered and paid to tenants, and the number of households receiving them.
- **Repairs.** The time to correct serious violations, the number of repair-and-deduct authorizations, and the number of repeat violations.
- **Displacement.** Households displaced by unfit-unit findings or license actions, relocation payments made, the share recovered from landlords, and the share of tenants who returned.
- **Fair access.** Screening complaints and their outcomes, and denials reversed after an error was corrected.
- **Stability.** The share of new leases offered as two-year terms, the share accepted, and eviction filings for City addresses.
- **Language access.** Translations requested and delivered, and complaints filed in languages other than English.

These measures will show the Council and the public whether the law is working, so they can adjust what isn't.

## What is still open

These items should be settled before or during drafting.

1. **Legal risk decisions**, laid out in [[Maryland Legal Baseline and Local Authority - Tenant Protection]].
2. **Frederick's baseline.** The current text of Chapter 12.5 after the rodent control amendments, and what the property maintenance code already requires for heat, cooling, locks, pests, and repair timelines.
3. **Local data.** Eviction filings and judgments for City addresses from Maryland Judiciary data, code complaint and violation data, rental stock by building age and owner size, and the languages spoken by renter households.
4. **Policy choices for you.**
   - Whether to bar denials based on dismissed eviction filings.
   - Whether to include the fair chance extension for small landlords.
   - The commission versus a hearing officer.
   - Introducing Real Rent with the package or ahead of it.
5. **Stakeholders.**
   - Tenant and immigrant groups: Maryland Legal Aid's Frederick office, the eviction Access to Counsel provider, Centro Hispano de Frederick, the Spanish Speaking Community of Maryland, RISE Coalition, and the Asian American Center of Frederick.
   - Landlord and property-manager associations.
   - The Housing Authority of the City of Frederick.
   - Ryan Trout in Rockville, for implementation lessons once Chapter 18 takes effect January 1, 2027.

Per the project rule, ordinance language is drafted in chat, not here.
