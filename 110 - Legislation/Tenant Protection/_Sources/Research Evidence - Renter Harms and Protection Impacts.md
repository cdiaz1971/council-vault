# Research Evidence - Renter Harms and Protection Impacts

This note gathers the research behind the package. Each entry says what the study found, how it supports the package, and its limits, so nothing gets overstated in public. Use the "limits" lines when preparing for hostile questions.

## Part 1. Know Before You Sign

**Hidden fees make people pay more.** Blake, Moshary, Sweeney, and Tadelis, "Price Salience and Product Choice," *Marketing Science* (2021). StubHub randomly assigned millions of U.S. users to see either all-in prices or prices with fees added at checkout. Buyers who saw fees late spent about 21 percent more and were 14 percent more likely to buy. Even experienced repeat buyers spent 15 percent more.

- *Supports:* all-in pricing and up-front fee disclosure (Real Rent and Part 1).
- *Limits:* the study looked at concert tickets, not housing. It shows how late-revealed fees work on buyers in general. It does not measure rental fees directly.

**Algorithmic rent setting raises rents.** Rockville staff cited a White House Council of Economic Advisers estimate that algorithmic pricing adds about $70 a month to rent nationally and $112 in the D.C. region. RealPage clients make up nearly 90 percent of the national market for buildings of 50 or more units. Rockville found eight properties, nearly 2,000 apartments, managed by companies named in the U.S. Department of Justice suit.

- *Supports:* Real Rent's algorithmic pricing ban.
- *Limits:* the figure is a national and regional estimate, not a Frederick measurement. Frederick's exposure is unknown until someone checks which local properties use these tools.

## Part 2. Fair Screening

**Screening reports are often wrong.** Consumer Financial Protection Bureau, *Consumer Snapshot: Tenant Background Checks* and *Tenant Background Check Market Report* (November 2022). The Bureau received about 26,700 tenant screening complaints from January 2019 to September 2022. More than 17,200 involved incorrect information. Monthly complaints grew from about 300 to almost 700. Renters described being denied over a twin's debt, a felony they never had, or misdemeanors listed as felonies. Some paid hundreds of dollars in fees across repeated denials. About 68 percent of renters pay application fees.

- *Supports:* written criteria, written denial reasons, a copy of the report, and a chance to correct errors.
- *Limits:* complaints are self-reported and don't show an error rate. They show the problem is widespread and growing.

**An eviction filing follows tenants even when they win.** Brantley and Eisenberg, "Record Costs," *Housing Studies* (University of Michigan). The study interviewed 29 Pennsylvania tenants whose cases were dismissed, withdrawn, or won. Eight in 10 said the filing limited their housing options. Among those who moved, 65 percent were asked about eviction records, and over half were told outright that the filing was why they were denied. Many submitted 10 or more applications at about $50 each. Tenants with records also hesitated to ask for repairs.

- *Supports:* the ban on eviction-history questions and limits on using filings that didn't end in an eviction.
- *Limits:* a small, qualitative sample from Pennsylvania. It shows how the harm works, not how often it happens.

**Maryland files a very large number of eviction cases.** More than 400,000 failure-to-pay-rent cases were filed statewide in fiscal year 2023, compared with 674,575 in 2019, according to Eviction Lab figures reported by the *Salisbury Daily Times*. Researchers link the decline to the 2021 right to counsel and 10-day notice laws. Most filings never end in a sheriff carrying out an eviction. Landlords use filing as a collection tool.

- *Supports:* the case that a filing is a poor measure of whether someone will be a good tenant.
- *Limits:* these are statewide numbers. Frederick City filing data still needs to be pulled (see [[Frederick Renter Data Baseline]]).

## Part 2. Stable Tenancy

**Forced moves hurt children in school.** Hepburn, Grubbs-Donovan, Graetz, Jin, and Desmond, "Consequences of Eviction-Led Forced Mobility for School-Age Children in Houston," *Sociology of Education*. The study linked eviction records to Houston school records from 2002 to 2016. At least 13,000 students lived in households with an eviction filing. They were more likely to leave the district or switch schools, often to schools with fewer resources. They also had more absences, and those who switched schools had more suspensions.

- *Supports:* the two-year lease offer, the once-a-year increase limit, and relocation help.
- *Limits:* the study shows an association, not proof of cause, and Houston is not Frederick.

**Forced moves cost jobs and health.** Research by Matthew Desmond found that workers who experienced a forced move were 11 to 22 percent more likely to lose their jobs. Mothers who were evicted reported more depression and material hardship two years later. Desmond also found renters who were forced to move ended up in poorer, higher-crime neighborhoods than those who moved by choice.

- *Supports:* stability provisions and relocation.
- *Limits:* the job-loss figure here comes from a 2017 *Baltimore Sun* summary. Confirm it against the original *Social Problems* article before quoting it publicly.

**Renters want longer leases.** Brookings research cited by Rockville staff found about half of renters stay more than two years and about a third would prefer longer leases. In Rockville, 36 percent of renters have stayed two to four years and 15.5 percent five to 14 years.

- *Supports:* the two-year lease offer.

## Part 3. Repairs and Enforcement

**Poor housing conditions drive asthma, and enforcement falls short.** Lemire, Samuels, Wang, and Haber, "Unequal Housing Conditions and Code Enforcement Contribute to Asthma Disparities in Boston," *Health Affairs* 41(4), 2022. The study used Boston code enforcement data from 2011 to 2021. Tenant reports of asthma triggers such as mold and pests were higher in neighborhoods with more Black and Hispanic residents, even after adjusting for income, and fewer of those cases were repaired. The racial gap in mold reports was widening. A later study in *JAMA Network Open* by the same research group found that tenant reports of in-home asthma triggers were tied to more adult asthma emergency room visits across Boston neighborhoods. Both studies call for stronger code enforcement.

- *Supports:* repair deadlines, City-authorized repair and deduct, and enforcement that tenants can actually use.
- *Limits:* Boston data. The JAMA study is cross-sectional.

**Enforcement without cost recovery leaves the City holding the bill.** Minneapolis advanced $170,523 in relocation payments over three years and recovered only $43,390, about a quarter, with recovery taking two years at best (Rockville staff report).

- *Supports:* the relocation lien.

**Enforcement that depends on court dates stalls.** Rockville's seven inspectors reported no recent license suspensions or revocations, and contested citations waited months for a court date (Rockville staff report).

- *Supports:* administrative remedies (refund orders, license conditions, liens) over court-based ones.

**Legal representation keeps people housed.** In Baltimore's right to counsel program, 82 percent of tenants who received full representation in FY2024 avoided disruptive displacement. Rockville staff cited research that tenants with counsel are two to three times more likely to stay housed.

- *Supports:* referral and Access to Counsel outreach as part of the package's education piece.

## The cost and supply question

**Good cause eviction laws don't reduce new construction.** University of Minnesota Center for Urban and Regional Affairs, "Good Case for Good Cause" (2025). The study compared housing permits in counties with good cause laws in California, Oregon, and New Hampshire against nearby counties without them. It found no evidence that the laws reduced development.

- *Supports:* rebutting the claim that tenant protections reduce supply. This package is lighter than good cause eviction.
- *Limits:* permit data measures new construction, not existing landlords leaving the market.

**Just cause laws show no supply effect.** Tim Thomas of the Urban Displacement Project (UC Berkeley) found no discernible supply effect from just cause laws in Seattle and New Jersey. New Jersey's has been in place since 1974. A literature review rated just cause and right to counsel as high-potential tools against displacement.

- *Limits:* reported in an opinion piece (Cascade PBS, 2021). Use the underlying research where possible.

**Rockville's own economic analysis was neutral overall.** Rockville staff judged the package roughly neutral overall. They saw possible exit by small landlords and some upward rent pressure on one side, and housing stability gains and slight downward pressure from transparency on the other. They expected some landlords to fold banned fees into base rent.

- *Supports:* an honest framing. The package makes prices visible and fair, not lower.

**Rent control evidence, for honesty.** Diamond, McQuade, and Qian (*American Economic Review*, 2019) found that San Francisco's 1994 rent control expansion cut tenant displacement but led affected landlords to reduce their rental supply by 15 percent, partly through condo conversions and sales.

- *Use:* opponents may cite this. It concerns rent control, which this package doesn't include. Don't let it be applied to disclosure and screening rules.
