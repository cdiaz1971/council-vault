# Peer Jurisdiction Tenant Protection Laws

This note collects what other places have actually put into law for each part of the Frederick package. It is organized by protection, not by jurisdiction, so you can see every model for one idea in one place.

**Which peers matter most.** Rockville and Takoma Park are Maryland municipalities, the same kind of government as Frederick. Anything they adopted is the strongest evidence that Frederick can do the same. Montgomery County and Prince George's County are charter counties, which have broader legislative power than municipalities. They are useful as models, but a county having done something doesn't by itself prove a city can. Out-of-state cities show what works in practice, but they operate under different state laws.

## Quick reference

| Jurisdiction | Type | Most relevant to Frederick |
|---|---|---|
| Rockville, MD | Municipality | Nearly the whole package: disclosures, rent history, two-year lease offer, eviction-history question ban, City-authorized repair and deduct, Commission, relocation with lien, data reporting (adopted July 2026, effective January 1, 2027) |
| Takoma Park, MD | Municipality | Commission on Landlord-Tenant Affairs, rent stabilization, relocation on condemnation with lien |
| Montgomery County, MD | Charter county | COLTA with damages awards, fee schedule under rent stabilization, Housing Justice Act (criminal screening), landlords under Consumer Protection law |
| Prince George's County, MD | Charter county | Permanent rent stabilization (2024) |
| Baltimore City, MD | Charter city-county | Universal right to counsel in eviction (2020); the *Aleti* case came from its licensing law |
| Philadelphia, PA | City | Renters' Access Act: written criteria, limits on eviction records, written reasons, reconsideration |
| Minneapolis, MN | City | Inclusive screening criteria, relocation equal to three months' rent, ban on asking immigration status |
| Seattle, WA | City | First-in-Time, Fair Chance Housing, two-page fee disclosure, enforcement with records presumption |
| Chicago, IL | City | Disclosure of code violations and foreclosure, tiered notice by length of tenancy |
| California | State | Lease translation (Civil Code § 1632), repair and deduct (§ 1942) |
| Portland, OR | City | Fixed-dollar relocation assistance |

## 1. Know Before You Sign (disclosure)

**Rockville (Chapter 18, effective January 1, 2027).** Every advertised price must include all mandatory fees and say whether utilities are included. Third-party sites the landlord does not control are exempt. Before signing and at renewal, the landlord must disclose:

- every fee and whether it is optional
- estimated utility costs
- three years of rent history for the unit, plus the building's average yearly increase

The proposed lease goes to the applicant two days before signing, and the signed copy goes back within three days. Owners must post a 24-hour local contact if there is no on-site manager, and new owners must notify tenants of the change. These are §§ 18-117, 18-127, 18-147, and 18-180.

**Seattle (Ordinance 127497, effective July 1, 2027).** Requires a disclosure of no more than two pages, placed before the first page of every lease and included in listings and applications. It shows rent, concessions, utilities, every fee, and the total monthly cost. It must be updated every 12 months and whenever a fee changes. The City publishes a model form translated into the five most common non-English languages.

**Chicago (Residential Landlord and Tenant Ordinance, Chapter 5-12).** Before a tenant signs, landlords must disclose code violations cited in the prior 12 months, pending code enforcement actions, and utility shutoff notices (§ 5-12-100). They must also disclose foreclosure filings (§ 5-12-095).

**California (Civil Code § 1632).** When a residential lease longer than one month is mainly negotiated in Spanish, Chinese, Tagalog, Vietnamese, or Korean, the landlord must give every signer a translated copy before signing. If the translation differs materially from the English lease, the tenant may cancel it. A tenant who brings their own adult interpreter, not provided by the landlord, falls outside the rule.

**What this means for Frederick.** Every disclosure item in Part 1 of the package already exists in adopted law somewhere, and Rockville alone covers most of them. The lease translation rule has no Maryland precedent yet. California's version is the template to adapt. Frederick should set the language list from local census data rather than California's five. Spanish is spoken at home by about 16 percent of City residents.

## 2. Fair screening

**Rockville.** Bans eviction-history questions on rental applications, though landlords may still consider eviction history as otherwise allowed by law (§ 18-149(b)). Landlords must explain in writing why they require a guarantor. Rockville already gets Montgomery County's criminal screening limits, because Rockville's code incorporates the county's Chapter 27.

**Montgomery County Housing Justice Act (2021).** Landlords may not run criminal background checks until after a conditional offer. They may not consider arrests that did not lead to conviction, or a list of minor offenses including trespassing, marijuana possession, and misdemeanor theft. Rockville staff also describe exclusion of misdemeanors older than two years and a seven-day window before an offer can be revoked. The Act does not apply to owner-occupied housing or accessory dwelling units.

**Philadelphia Renters' Access Act (2021).** Landlords must give applicants written screening criteria before taking an application or fee. They may not consider:

- evictions more than four years old
- eviction filings that did not end in a judgment for the landlord, or that were sealed, withdrawn, or settled
- non-payment during the COVID emergency period

Landlords may not reject applicants by blanket rule or solely on a credit or screening score. They must give written reasons and copies of any reports within three business days. Applicants then have time to dispute errors or show mitigating circumstances. Landlords with five or more units must offer the next comparable unit if the applicant succeeds. The Commission on Human Relations enforces the law, with fines up to $2,000 per violation.

**Minneapolis (2019).** Landlords choose between two paths:

- **Inclusive screening criteria.** The landlord follows the City's criteria, which cap how far back they can look at evictions and convictions. For example, a misdemeanor older than three years can't be used, and a minimum credit score is prohibited.
- **Individualized assessment.** A landlord who uses stricter criteria must review each application individually, accept added information, and give specific reasons for any denial.

Landlords also may not ask about immigration status or turn away applicants who use an ITIN.

**Seattle.** The First-in-Time rule requires landlords to publish their screening criteria and offer the unit to the first applicant who qualifies. The Washington Supreme Court upheld it in *Yim v. Seattle* (2019). The Fair Chance Housing Ordinance limits the use of criminal history. In 2023 the Ninth Circuit struck its ban on *asking* about criminal history as a First Amendment violation, but upheld the ban on *denying* housing based on it.

**Maryland baseline.** State law already limits application fees, requires landlords with more than five units to make a conditional offer before checking criminal history, and, since October 2024, shields failure-to-pay-rent records that did not end in possession. See [[Maryland Legal Baseline and Local Authority - Tenant Protection]].

**What this means for Frederick.** Philadelphia is the closest model for going beyond Rockville, because it restricts how landlords may *use* eviction records rather than only what they may *ask*. Because of the *Yim* ruling, the screening article should be drafted mainly as limits on using information and denying applicants, with disclosure duties, rather than bans on asking questions. Minneapolis's two-path design is a useful compromise for small landlords: they can follow City criteria and skip the individualized review.

## 3. A stable home

**Rockville.** The landlord must offer a two-year initial lease. The tenant may decline, and the landlord may offer a shorter term for a stated reasonable cause that the tenant can challenge. Any rent increase during the two-year term must be written into the lease up front. A continuing tenant's rent can rise only once in 12 months.

Rockville also lets tenants end a lease early with 30 days' notice, with any termination charge capped at one month's rent, for these reasons:

- involuntary job relocation
- a unit unfit for 60 days
- a suspended or revoked license
- unrepaired violations
- the death of a wage earner
- a change in disability status
- inpatient treatment
- other causes beyond the tenant's control

Leases may not include one-sided attorney fee clauses or treat those fees as rent. Tenants have the right to organize, to use meeting rooms free of charge, and to have a tenant organization file complaints on their behalf.

Rockville staff cited Brookings research finding that about half of renters stay more than two years and about a third would prefer longer leases.

**Montgomery County.** Requires an offer of a two-year lease. Its rent stabilization law (Bill 15-23, effective July 2024) caps yearly increases at CPI plus 3 percent or 6 percent, whichever is lower. Regulations under that law also cap fees.

**Prince George's County (2024).** Permanent rent stabilization took effect October 17, 2024. Increases are capped at the lower of 6 percent or CPI plus 3 percent, or 4.5 percent for senior housing. Units built since 2000 are exempt.

**Chicago Fair Notice.** Requires longer notice for nonrenewal or a large rent increase the longer the tenant has lived there: 30, 60, or 120 days depending on the length of tenancy.

**What this means for Frederick.** The two-year lease offer, the once-a-year increase limit, and the early termination grounds all have Maryland municipal precedent in Rockville. Rent stabilization exists in Takoma Park and both large counties, but it stays out of this package for vote-count reasons. Tiered notice adds little here, because Maryland already requires 90 days' notice for most lease terms.

## 4. Repairs and habitability

**Rockville.** When a landlord misses a City correction deadline, the City Manager may authorize the tenant to hire a licensed contractor and deduct up to two months' rent, or may revoke the license (§ 18-115(g)). The City also inspects at least 25 percent of multifamily units at each license renewal. An enhanced inspection program raises the sample to 40 percent when serious problems turn up:

- smoke detectors, pests, broken locks, or suspected mold in 25 percent or more of sampled units, or
- heat below 68°F in 10 percent or more

**Montgomery County.** Sets repair deadlines of 24 to 48 hours for life-threatening conditions and 15 to 30 days for others, with 48 hours for repeat violations. Rockville staff used these timelines in designing their rules.

**California (Civil Code § 1942).** Lets tenants repair and deduct up to one month's rent, no more than twice in 12 months, after notice.

**What this means for Frederick.** Rockville's version, where the City authorizes the repair rather than the tenant acting alone, is the one to copy. It keeps the remedy inside City enforcement authority, which avoids the *Aleti* problem with tenant lawsuits.

## 5. Enforcement bodies and remedies

**Rockville Commission on Landlord-Tenant Affairs.** Has two landlords, two tenants, and three public members. After City staff investigate and attempt conciliation, the Commission holds hearings, can compel witnesses and documents, and can award up to $5,000 per affected unit. It can also order lease termination or rent reductions. Tenant organizations have standing to file complaints. Penalties are municipal infractions up to $5,000, the maximum Maryland allows since October 2025.

**Montgomery County COLTA (§ 29-9).** A quasi-judicial body with 15 members (four tenants, four landlords, four public members, and three alternates) that hears complaints staff could not resolve. Possible awards include:

- lease termination
- deposit refunds plus up to three times the deposit
- refunds of rent paid
- up to $2,500 in damages
- the cost of temporary housing
- up to $1,000 in attorney fees

Orders are binding, and the County enforces them. Appeals go to Circuit Court, and the appellant must post a bond to pause a money award. Since Bill 6-25 (effective July 2025), landlord-tenant matters also fall under the County's Consumer Protection law, which lets the County sue landlords with a pattern of deceptive practices.

**Takoma Park COLTA (Chapter 6.24).** Has 7 to 12 members. It decides complaints under the landlord-tenant chapter, mediates disputes, rules on petitions for rent increases above the stabilization limit, and hears appeals of license denials, suspensions, and revocations.

**Seattle.** Its approach to enforcement is administrative. The City runs building-wide and company-wide investigations and requires landlords to keep records for three years, with a presumption of violation when records are missing. It orders restitution plus interest plus double the prohibited fees. Penalties drop 75 or 50 percent if restitution is paid within 10 or 15 days. Three enforcement positions are funded by a rental regulatory fee of about $11 per unit.

**Baltimore City right to counsel (2020).** Every tenant facing eviction is offered a lawyer, with no income test. In FY2024, 82 percent of tenants who received full representation avoided disruptive displacement.

**What this means for Frederick.** Three Maryland jurisdictions run landlord-tenant commissions with balanced membership. Rockville and Takoma Park are municipalities. Montgomery County shows the fuller model with defined damages and bonds on appeal. Seattle supplies the enforcement tools that don't need a commission at all: records rules, City-ordered restitution, and incentives for quick repayment. Frederick can adopt those first and add a commission later if needed.

## 6. Relocation

**Rockville (§ 18-178).** Provides two kinds of relocation help.

- **Temporary.** For a unit unfit for under six months: a hotel, per diem, or comparable housing, plus moving and storage.
- **Permanent.** Triggered by license revocation when the owner stops renting, displacement of six months or more, or redevelopment that pushes out a household under 50 percent of AMI before the lease ends. The landlord pays the deposit with interest, prorated rent, three months of HUD small area fair market rent, moving costs, and an extra amount for seniors, people with disabilities, and families with children.

Payments are owed even if the tenant is behind on rent. If the landlord doesn't pay, the City pays, bills the landlord, and collects unpaid costs as a lien like property taxes. Displaced tenants get a right to return. Rockville staff estimated about $9,782 in total for a five-year tenant paying $2,000 a month.

**Takoma Park (displacement ordinance, 2023).** Adds § 6.16.180. Within 24 hours of a condemnation, the landlord must provide comparable housing. Within seven days, the landlord pays permanently displaced tenants their deposit with interest plus two months' rent. Temporarily displaced tenants get alternative housing plus moving costs both ways, and return on a substantially similar lease. Unpaid City costs become a lien collected like taxes. *The vault has the July 2023 draft; confirm the adopted text before citing.*

**Minneapolis.** Relocation equals three months' rent when a tenant must move because of code violations or license revocation or denial. It also applies in some sales of naturally occurring affordable housing. The City advanced $170,523 over three years and recovered only $43,390, about a quarter, without a lien.

**Portland, OR.** Requires fixed relocation payments of $2,900 to $4,500 by unit size in qualifying no-cause terminations and large increases. **Seattle** splits a relocation payment for low-income tenants (about $5,354) between the landlord and the City.

**What this means for Frederick.** Two Maryland municipalities use the lien approach, and Minneapolis shows what happens without it. Rockville's two-tier design (temporary and permanent) fits Frederick's post-revocation gap described in [[What Happens After License Revocation -- The Missing Piece in Frederick's Tenant Protection Stack]].

## 7. Data reporting

**Rockville (Article V, § 18-195).** At each license issuance or renewal, landlords report a standard lease, owner contacts, building facts, fees, and unit-level rent, rent increases, and evictions. The housing director reports yearly. Rent increase notices must include the City's voluntary guidelines. Staff recommended showing building rent increases as five-year tiers rather than raw percentages, so that published data doesn't help landlords coordinate prices.

**What this means for Frederick.** Reporting at license renewal costs little and builds the record Frederick needs to evaluate the package and any future debate over rent stabilization.
