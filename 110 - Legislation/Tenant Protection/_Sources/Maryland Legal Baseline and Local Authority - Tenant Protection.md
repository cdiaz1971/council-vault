# Maryland Legal Baseline and Local Authority - Tenant Protection

This note covers three things: what state law already does for renters, where Frederick has room to add, and how risky each piece of the package is legally. It is a risk analysis to support your decisions, not a legal opinion. Legal review by the City Attorney's office is advisory and you decide how to proceed.

## 1. What Maryland already guarantees

The package should build on these protections, not repeat them. Every landlord must attach the current Maryland Tenants' Bill of Rights to every lease; the current edition took effect October 1, 2026. It summarizes the following.

**Applications.** Landlords with five or more units may keep no more than $25 of an application fee unless their actual costs are higher. They must refund unused money within 15 days and disclose in writing, before taking a fee, whether they check criminal history. If a landlord accepts a recent screening report the tenant provides, they may not charge an application fee.

**Criminal history.** Screening is optional, but a landlord who screens one applicant must screen all of them. Landlords with more than five units must make a conditional offer before checking criminal history and may consider only specified serious offenses or felonies within five years. They must give written reasons for a withdrawal and must reconsider on new evidence. **Landlords with five or fewer units are not covered.**

**Subsidies.** Applicants who pay with an income-based subsidy can't be refused over income, credit score, or credit history from a period when they had no subsidy. Source of income is a protected class under state fair housing law.

**Eviction records.** Since October 1, 2024, failure-to-pay-rent records that did not end in possession are shielded, and others can be shielded by petition after redemption plus 12 months (per Rockville staff's summary of state law).

**Leases.** Landlords with five or more units must use a written lease. Leases may not include confession of judgment, waivers of legal rights or jury trial, or shortened notice periods. **The Bill of Rights states that local jurisdictions may require additional lease content.**

**Rent increases and notice.** There is no statewide cap. Under § 8-209, rent may rise only at renewal, with 90 days' notice for most terms. § 8-209 **preserves stronger local protections.**

**Deposits and late fees.** Deposits are capped at one month's rent under § 8-203, a topic the state fully covers. Late fees are capped at 5 percent of the unpaid amount.

**Repairs.** The state provides several remedies:

- **Rent escrow (§ 8-211).** A court process for serious defects.
- **Warranty of habitability (§ 8-212).** Remedies include damages, rent reduction, lease termination, and relocation expenses, all ordered by a court.
- **Mold.** Assessment is required within 15 days of notice, and removal within 45 days if possible. The law passed without state funding or a clear local enforcement role.
- **Air conditioning.** Required only in buildings with 10 or more units, and only under certain conditions.
- **Entry.** A landlord must give 24 hours' written notice.

**Utilities.** § 8-212.4 regulates ratio utility billing (RUBS) and expressly addresses stronger local disclosure rules.

**Early termination.** Tenants may end a lease early after domestic abuse, an active-duty military reassignment, or certain medical conditions.

**Retaliation (§ 8-208.1).** Landlords may not retaliate against tenants for complaints, organizing, or calling for help. Threatening to report immigration status counts as retaliation. Under Criminal Law § 3-701, using that threat to get money or labor is a crime.

**Evictions.** A nonpayment case requires 10 days' notice, a judgment, and a warrant executed by the sheriff. Lockouts and utility shutoffs are banned. "Rent" in a nonpayment case means base rent only. Local governments may set the eviction-day notice between 4 and 14 days.

**Right to counsel.** The state's Access to Counsel in Evictions program covers tenants at or below 50 percent of median income.

**Good cause eviction.** The 2026 bills (HB 774 and SB 462) did not pass. They would have authorized counties only.

**Municipal fines.** SB 820 (Chapter 570 of 2025) raised the maximum municipal infraction fine from $1,000 to $5,000.

## 2. Where Frederick has room to act

**General police power.** Maryland municipalities may pass ordinances that protect residents' health, safety, comfort, and convenience under their charters and Local Government Article § 5-202. Frederick also regulates rentals through its licensing chapter (Chapter 12.5). The package can rest on both.

**State law says local rules are allowed in these areas:**

- additional lease content (Bill of Rights)
- additional rent increase notice protections (§ 8-209)
- stronger utility billing disclosure (§ 8-212.4)
- the eviction-day notice window (4 to 14 days)

**The licensing-condition approach.** A landlord who must follow a rule to keep a City rental license is regulated as a business. That is on firmer ground than a rule that rewrites the private landlord-tenant relationship. The vault's [[Real Rent Ordinance - Maryland Peer Jurisdiction Research]] lays out this argument. Its weak point is reach. Units outside licensing aren't covered, and Chapter 12.5 currently exempts buildings whose certificates of occupancy are eight years old or newer (§ 12.5-76(a)(5)). Closing that exemption makes the licensing approach cover everyone.

**Maryland municipalities have already done this.** Rockville adopted almost the entire package in 2026. Takoma Park has run a landlord-tenant commission, rent stabilization, and relocation with a lien for years. They are the same kind of government as Frederick. Montgomery and Prince George's counties are charter counties with broader power, so their laws support the policy but carry less weight on the authority question.

## 3. The three legal doctrines that shape the design

**Preemption.** Maryland courts recognize three kinds of state preemption of local law:

- **Express.** The state says so outright.
- **Implied.** The state has occupied the whole field.
- **Conflict.** Local law prohibits what the state allows, or allows what the state prohibits.

Security deposits are the clearest field the state occupies. That's why deposits stay out of Real Rent and out of this package. Elsewhere, Title 8 sets a floor and in several places says local governments may add to it. For each provision, the question is whether it adds to state law or contradicts it.

***Aleti v. Metropolitan Baltimore* (2022).** Maryland's highest court declined to imply a private right for tenants to recover rent paid to a landlord who lacked a Baltimore license. Assistant City Attorney Iris Annan read *Aleti* as barring local private rights of action unless the General Assembly authorizes them. The vault's [[Real Rent Ordinance - Legal Review Prep]] argues the holding is narrower, limited to *implied* rights.

Either way, the package avoids the question. Remedies run through:

- City-ordered refunds
- conditions on rental licenses
- municipal infractions
- City-authorized repairs
- relocation costs recovered through a lien

None of these depends on a tenant suing.

**First Amendment limits on bans on asking questions.** In *Yim v. Seattle* (2023), the Ninth Circuit struck down Seattle's ban on landlords *asking* about criminal history but upheld the ban on *denying* housing based on it. Maryland is not bound by the Ninth Circuit, but landlords could raise the same argument. Rockville's ban on asking about eviction history is a question ban of this kind. Frederick's screening article is safer drafted as limits on how information may be *used*, together with disclosure duties (the Philadelphia model).

## 4. Provision-by-provision risk

| Provision | Risk | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Two-page disclosure, fees, utility estimates | Low | The state allows added lease content. Rockville and Seattle have it. |
| Three-year rent history | Low | Disclosure of information the landlord already holds. Rockville has it. |
| Code violations, foreclosure, owner and contact disclosure | Low | Disclosure. Code citations are City records. Chicago and Rockville have it. |
| Proposed lease two days ahead, signed copy in three | Low | Rockville has it. |
| Lease translation | Low to moderate | No Maryland precedent. It is a disclosure rule, like California § 1632. The risk is in defining "negotiated in" and the remedy. |
| Written screening criteria, written denial reasons, copy of report | Low | Disclosure and process. Philadelphia and Seattle have it. |
| Ban on asking about eviction history | Moderate | Rockville adopted it, but *Yim* raises a speech argument. Safer as a use restriction. |
| Bar on denials for dismissed or tenant-won eviction filings | Low to moderate | Goes further than state shielding without contradicting it. Philadelphia has it. |
| Fair chance extension to landlords with five or fewer units | Moderate | Could be argued that the state deliberately exempted small landlords. Draft it as adding to state law, not changing it. |
| Offer of a two-year lease | Low to moderate | Rockville and Montgomery County have it. The tenant may decline. |
| One rent increase per 12 months | Low | Regulates timing, not amount. Fits within § 8-209's preserved local protections. |
| Early termination grounds, one-month cap | Low to moderate | Adds grounds the state doesn't cover. Rockville has it. |
| Limits on one-sided attorney fee and court cost clauses | Low to moderate | Rockville and Montgomery County have it. Court costs themselves are set by the state and stay untouched. |
| Right to organize, free meeting rooms | Low | Rockville has it. Builds on § 8-208.1. |
| City-authorized repair and deduct | Low to moderate | Runs through City inspection authority, not a private claim. Rockville has it. Must work alongside rent escrow without replacing it. |
| Repair deadlines by severity | Low | Code enforcement standard. Check what the property maintenance code already sets. |
| City-ordered refunds as a license condition | Low to moderate | The licensing-condition approach. Seattle's model, adapted. |
| Three-year records rule with presumption of violation | Moderate | Seattle has it. A Maryland court has not reviewed a local presumption of this kind. |
| Municipal infractions up to $5,000 | Low | Authorized by SB 820. |
| Commission or hearing officer awarding money | Moderate to high | The biggest authority question. Rockville and Takoma Park do it, so check how their charters authorize it and compare Frederick's Charter. A hearing officer applying license conditions is lower risk than an award of damages. |
| Relocation payments by landlords | Moderate | Rockville and Takoma Park have it. The questions are how far the payment can reach (owed even when the tenant is in arrears) and how the triggers are defined. |
| Relocation costs collected as a lien | Moderate | Rockville and Takoma Park have it. Confirm Frederick's lien authority for code-related costs. |
| Algorithmic pricing ban (in Real Rent) | Moderate | Rockville has it. RealPage has sued other cities. Keep the definition limited to non-public competitor data. |
| Coverage of units outside licensing | Moderate | Relies on general police power. Simplest fix: remove the eight-year certificate of occupancy exemption. |
| Local private right of action | High | *Aleti*. Not proposed. |
| Security deposit rules | High | The state occupies the field. Not proposed. |

## 5. Questions to settle before drafting

1. Does Frederick's Charter allow an administrative body to award money? How does Rockville's charter authorize its Commission?
2. Can Frederick put code-related relocation costs on the tax bill as a lien under existing authority, or does the ordinance need to create it?
3. Should coverage come from removing the § 12.5-76(a)(5) exemption, or from a separate duty under general police power?
4. Should the screening article ban questions (Rockville) or restrict use (Philadelphia)? This note recommends restricting use.
5. Does the City adopt the local eviction-day notice window (4 to 14 days) as part of the package?
