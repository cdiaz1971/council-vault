# Data Center ZTA PC25-626ZTA -- Opposition Master Strategy Note

## Summary

Master strategy note for César's opposition to ZTA PC25-626ZTA, submitted by Trammell Crow attorney Bruce Dean in December 2025, which proposes adding Critical Digital Infrastructure (data centers) as a permitted use in M1 and M2 industrial zones in the City of Frederick. César opposes this ZTA. The vote math currently favors denial. The goal over the coming months is to build a comprehensive public record, sustain community engagement, and position opposition as responsible governance rather than anti-development ideology.

This note is the anchor document for all ZTA opposition work. Other notes in the data centers cluster link here.

---

## Current Status

ZTA PC25-626ZTA is at the Planning Commission. It will not reach the City Council for several months. The legislative record was formally opened at the June 11, 2026 worksession. Three presentations were given: Mike Turner (Loudoun County, cautionary case study), David Lapp (Maryland Office of People's Counsel, ratepayer harm), and Richard Griffin (Department of Economic Development, affirmative fiscal case).

The ZTA was originally drafted by Bruce Dean's firm on behalf of Trammell Crow. A nearly identical draft was submitted by then-Council President Nash in August 2025. The Ethics Commission found Nash violated three provisions of the City Ethics Ordinance (Sections 21-4(b)(1), 21-4(c)(1)(B), and 21-4(f)(1)) in connection with her promotion of data center legislation while employed as a paid lobbyist for energy and data industry clients. Nash is ordered to cease and desist from participation in matters involving data centers or energy, which means she will not vote on the ZTA when it reaches the council.

---

## Process and Timeline

The Planning Commission conducts its own independent hearings on the ZTA and will eventually make a recommendation to the council. Council members do not participate in or testify at Planning Commission hearings. That process runs on its own track.

Once the Planning Commission issues its recommendation, the matter comes to the City Council. The council then holds its own hearings and votes. That vote is not expected until August or September 2026.

In the meantime, the council is holding workshops to build knowledge and prepare for the eventual vote. The first workshop was June 11, 2026. A second workshop is scheduled for June 12, 2026. Additional workshops are expected as the ZTA moves through the Planning Commission process. These workshops are the primary venue for César to develop the record, ask questions of staff and outside experts, and signal his position publicly.

---

## Vote Math

Six voting members when the ZTA reaches council (Nash recused). A tie vote of 3-3 defeats the ZTA. Current alignment:

- César: No
- Brehm: No
- Taylor: No

Three votes is sufficient to deny. The strategy is to hold those three and build a clean, well-documented record that makes approval politically costly for the three unknowns: Shackelford, Adkins, and Hempel Irani.

## The Core Frame

Three arguments anchor all public statements and record-building. They are used in combination depending on the audience.

**The fiscal argument.** The city cannot levy a business personal property tax. In Loudoun County, that tax accounts for two-thirds of data center revenue. Without it, Frederick's fiscal return is a fraction of what proponents claim. The developer's own economic analysis (Sage Policy Group, June 2026) was commissioned and paid for by Trammell Crow. The city has no independent number. The New Jobs Tax Credit would reduce real property tax liability for six years. Staff has provided no revised projection accounting for this.

**The ratepayer argument.** OPC's June 11 testimony documented that data centers are already raising Maryland residential utility bills by $10 to $21 per month in PJM capacity costs. Frederick residents are already paying that. Approving this ZTA means more of the same, permanently baked into the grid.

**The process argument.** This ZTA originated through a lobbying channel. The language was written by the developer's own counsel. The Ethics Commission found violations. The city has no independent analysis. The Maryland SB 116 state study is due September 1, 2026. Staff's own memo recommends following the county's two-year CDI Overlay process. LMC Section 306(b) provides for scheduling hearings "as soon as practicable" -- language the city's own code uses intentionally to allow a context-specific timeline that balances developer urgency against the city's need for fact-finding and thorough review. Mobilize Frederick identified this provision explicitly in their February 23, 2026 letter as a legal basis for a deliberate, unhurried process. There is no responsible basis for moving forward before the state study is complete and independent city analysis exists.

## Fiscal Weaknesses in the Pro-ZTA Case

These are the specific gaps that need to be on the record at council workshops and hearings.

No business personal property tax. The city lacks authority to levy this tax. In Loudoun County, it accounts for approximately two-thirds of data center tax revenue. The fiscal case for Frederick depends on real property taxes alone, which is a materially weaker position than the staff presentation implied. The ULI Local Guidelines for Data Center Development (2024) confirms this dynamic explicitly, noting that in Loudoun personal property tax revenue from data centers surged 170% in 2023, accounting for two and a half times the real property tax revenue.

New Jobs Tax Credit exposure. Data centers would likely qualify for this credit, reducing real property tax liability for six years. Staff has not provided a revised fiscal projection accounting for this. This question needs to be raised at a workshop.

Developer-commissioned analysis only. The Sage Policy Group report was paid for by Trammell Crow. No independent city analysis exists. This is not a minor procedural gap. It is the entire evidentiary foundation for the economic case.

## Grid and Power Constraints

The Fellowship of Scientists and Engineers documented these constraints in their December 19, 2025 summary of a meeting with Shackelford and Hempel Irani.

Maryland imports 40% of its power. That percentage will increase as the Adamstown complex develops. The existing transmission capacity from Doubs Substation to the Adamstown site has already been fully allocated by Bauxite I, II, III, and the initial Aligned Data Center. Additional transmission infrastructure would require new MPRP-scale transmission lines.

PJM has allocated power to as many as 100 approved data centers in Northern Virginia. It could take 10 years for the Adamstown site to receive power for all intended data centers. Any Frederick city data center would be at the end of this queue.

FERC has issued a preliminary order that data centers should provide their own generation -- meaning they must buy or build large generators such as 400 MW gas/steam combined cycle plants or nuclear facilities. Maryland is not a generating state.

The city does not control electricity in the area. However, residents and businesses will realize the impacts of data center electricity usage through higher rates. Electricity costs are already elevated due to data center demand, per the city's own electricity supplier. This is documented in the Mobilize Frederick/Willoughby February 2026 presentation.

Key question for staff at a workshop: has any grid capacity analysis been done confirming that power is actually available for a Frederick city data center, and on what basis is the fiscal impact analysis projecting tax revenue from facilities that may not be able to receive power for a decade?

## Environmental Concerns

**Water -- Potomac River Basin.** The proposed Trammell Crow sites are near the Monocacy River, a Potomac tributary. The city acknowledges it has limited water resources and is currently updating its water resources study. The Mobilize Frederick/Willoughby February 2026 presentation explicitly recommends waiting for that study to be complete before making decisions about large new water consumers. ICPRB's March 2026 analysis projects that data center water demand in the Potomac Basin could reach 22 MGD on average and over 80 MGD at peak by 2050. A single mid-sized data center can consume roughly 300,000 gallons of water per day -- equivalent to 1,000 households. Currently 70-80% of water drawn into evaporative cooling systems is permanently lost. Data centers in the basin are served by public water utilities and hold no direct withdrawal permits, placing them outside existing consumptive use regulations. The city is also currently experiencing a serious drought, making this timing especially significant.

Key question: has any hydrological analysis been done regarding consumptive water use impacts on the Monocacy River and downstream Potomac supply? When will the city's water resources study be complete, and how will it inform this ZTA decision?

**Karst topography and vibration risk.** This is a Frederick-specific environmental risk with no parallel in general data center debates. The proposed sites sit on karst topography. Generator vibrations from data center operations could cause ground instability in karst geology. The Mobilize Frederick/Willoughby February 2026 presentation identifies this as a specific concern and recommends a thorough vibration analysis to determine what mitigation would be needed for each site. The ZTA contains no requirement for such an analysis.

**Wastewater treatment risks.** The Fellowship of Scientists and Engineers raised the wastewater treatment contamination risk in detail. Cooling water biocides, glycol additives, and brine buildup from repeated cooling cycles all pose threats to municipal wastewater treatment operations if discharged. The ZTA contains no provisions addressing this.

**Impervious surfaces and stormwater.** Data center campuses create large impervious surfaces, reducing groundwater recharge and creating flashy runoff that can flood and contaminate neighboring properties, wells, riparian buffers, floodplains, and streams. The Monocacy River location makes this especially significant.

**Noise, light, and air quality.** Residents near data centers report a constant hum from cooling equipment. Noise from operations including backup generators can impact wildlife. The Mobilize Frederick/Willoughby presentation recommends noise be kept below 55 dB(A) at 300 feet from the facility, with physical barriers reducing noise levels by 70%. Betty Law's June 9 letter notes that MDE is not permitted to impose requirements beyond minimum approval standards for diesel generators. The city can require more. The ZTA does not. The Mobilize Frederick/Willoughby presentation recommends Tier IV backup generators and, where possible, battery energy storage or solar for backup power.

**Piecemeal aggregation risk.** The ZTA would permit data centers on any site of at least 75 acres zoned M1 or M2. Four sites near the Monocacy River currently qualify. Adjacent properties, if separately developed, could aggregate into a de facto hyperscale facility. The ZTA contains no aggregation cap.

## Conflict with City Climate and Sustainability Goals

This is a significant argument that has not been fully developed in prior opposition framing.

The City of Frederick adopted a Climate Emergency Resolution in 2020 committing to reduce emissions 50% from 2010 levels by 2030 and 100% by no later than 2050. The city's own Climate and Energy Action Plan concludes that this goal will be impossible with data centers unless stricter state regulations are in place. Data centers appear as a distinct emissions category in the Action Plan's emissions trajectory modeling.

The city is actively working to reduce electricity usage in government facilities through solar, renewable energy certificates, battery backup storage, and LEED-equivalent build-outs. Approving a ZTA that would bring massive new electricity load to the city directly undermines that work.

The Mobilize Frederick/Willoughby February 2026 presentation recommends requiring large-impact facilities to bring their own power (BYOP) -- a requirement other communities have imposed. The ZTA contains no such requirement.

Key question for staff at a workshop: how does the proposed ZTA interact with the city's Climate Emergency Resolution and Climate and Energy Action Plan? Has staff assessed whether approving this use is consistent with those adopted goals?

## The ULI Standard -- Measuring the ZTA Against Industry Guidelines

The Urban Land Institute's Local Guidelines for Data Center Development (2024), published by the ULI Americas Data Center Product Council, is the data center industry's own guidance document for municipalities. It is useful as a neutral, industry-recognized benchmark against which to measure the ZTA. Several of its recommendations directly support denial or delay.

ULI recommends that municipalities perform comprehensive analyses of power availability, infrastructure, and environmental impact before permitting data centers. The city has done none of that.

ULI warns against reactive, developer-initiated zoning by exception, and recommends instead that municipalities establish clear rules in advance. The ZTA is precisely the kind of reactive, single-developer-initiated text amendment that ULI cautions against.

ULI's model ordinance for industrial areas adjacent to residential recommends a minimum 200-foot setback from residential or noise-sensitive uses. Betty Law's critique of the ZTA's defective setback language is validated by ULI's own standard -- and the ZTA falls short of it.

ULI confirms that the personal property tax is the dominant revenue source from data centers, reinforcing the fiscal argument about Frederick's inability to levy it.

ULI explicitly recommends that municipalities identify strategic locations based on power availability, infrastructure, and environmental impact before attracting data center investment. Frederick has not done this analysis.

This document can be cited to counter any argument that opposition to the ZTA is anti-technology or anti-development. The data center industry's own professional organization recommends more process than has occurred here.

## Process Failures in the ZTA

Betty Law, P.E. (retired), submitted detailed comments on the third submittal of the ZTA on June 9, 2026, identifying specific drafting defects.

The setback language reads "at least 500 feet from any existing structure devoted to residential use." Law identifies that this means a 90-foot building could be constructed 50 feet from a property line if the house itself is 500 feet away from that line. This is a specific, documentable drafting defect. ULI's own model ordinance recommends a minimum 200-foot setback from residential uses -- which the ZTA's language does not reliably achieve.

The ZTA was written from the perspective of one builder. It covers his needs but omits the language needed to protect residents. It lacks provisions that the county's CDI Ordinance includes as baseline requirements.

Amazon has already purchased property on Gas House Pike from Trammell Crow described as a "warehouse." Industry practice of calling data centers "data warehouses" or "AI factories" is documented. The ZTA contains no protection against this terminological arbitrage.

Both Loudoun County and Frederick County have moved away from by-right zoning for data centers. The ZTA proposes by-right permitting in M1 and M2 zones.

## LMC Section 306(b) -- The Process Argument in City Code

This is the key legal point identified by Mobilize Frederick in their February 23, 2026 letter to the council, and it belongs prominently in any public discussion of timeline.

LMC Section 306(b) provides that hearings by both the Planning Commission and the City Council on petitions for zoning text amendments shall be scheduled "as soon as practicable." Mobilize Frederick correctly identified that this language is intentional. It contemplates a context-specific timeline that balances the developer's desire for prompt action against the city's need for fact-finding, thorough review of cumulative and other impacts, and time to consider the merits of a floating zone with appropriate performance standards.

This is not a delay tactic. It is the city's own code recognizing that some text amendments are consequential enough to require deliberate, methodical process. A ZTA that would open 230 acres near the Monocacy River to heavy industrial data center development, in the absence of a state impact study, independent city analysis, grid capacity verification, water assessment, karst vibration study, or climate impact analysis, is precisely the context for which that language exists.

## Ethics Commission Ruling -- Context and Use

The Ethics Commission unanimously found that Nash violated City Ethics Ordinance Sections 21-4(b)(1), 21-4(c)(1)(B), and 21-4(f)(1) in connection with her promotion of data center legislation while employed as a paid lobbyist for Vistra, Amazon Web Services, NTT Data, and other energy and data industry clients through Fiastro Consulting and Energy Advocacy, LLC.

The ruling establishes on the public record that the ZTA originated through an industry lobbying channel, not through independent staff or council deliberation. The draft language Nash submitted in August 2025 was prepared by Bruce Dean's firm -- the same firm that submitted the identical ZTA on behalf of Trammell Crow in December 2025.

How to use this: The ruling is a public document. It is relevant as public input on the ZTA's origins. It is not a basis for denying the ZTA on land use grounds. Do not lead with it. When asked, the response is: the Ethics Commission has issued its ruling and it is a public document. The focus is on the substance of the ZTA and why it is wrong for Frederick.

The practical consequence of the ruling: Nash is ordered to cease and desist from participation in matters involving data centers or energy. She will not vote on the ZTA. That changes the vote math to six members, where 3-3 is a denial.

The Willoughby/Cannon February 2026 presentation documents conflict with city climate goals, karst vibration risks, noise standards, and the city's own pending water resources study.

**Interstate Commission on the Potomac River Basin (ICPRB).** Their March 2026 analysis on data center water use in the Potomac Basin is the authoritative source for water impact projections. External expert testimony, not community advocacy.

Council vote on ZTA: expected August or September 2026, after the Planning Commission issues its recommendation.

## Questions to Raise at Council Workshops

These are the substantive gaps that need to be surfaced during council workshops before the vote. Workshops are the venue for César to ask staff for analysis, request outside expert testimony, and build the factual record that will support denial.

On fiscal impact: Has the city commissioned any independent economic analysis, or is the only analysis the Sage Policy Group report commissioned by Trammell Crow? Has staff modeled revenue projections accounting for the New Jobs Tax Credit?

On grid capacity: Has any grid capacity analysis confirmed that power is actually available for a Frederick city data center? Has staff consulted with First Energy or PJM regarding queue position for new city load?

On water: Has any hydrological analysis been done regarding consumptive water use impacts on the Monocacy River and downstream Potomac supply? Has the city assessed its wastewater treatment system's capacity to handle cooling water discharges including biocides, glycol, and brine? When will the city's water resources study be complete, and will the council wait for it before acting?

On karst geology: Has any vibration analysis been conducted for the proposed sites given their karst topography? What mitigation would be required, and who bears that cost?

On climate goals: How does this ZTA interact with the city's Climate Emergency Resolution and Climate and Energy Action Plan? Has staff assessed whether approving this use is consistent with those adopted commitments?

On process: Has the SB 116 state study been completed and reviewed? Staff's own memo recommends following the county's two-year CDI Overlay model. What is the basis for proceeding before that process is complete?

On the Loudoun precedent: What specific safeguards in this ZTA prevent Frederick from reaching the same grid oversubscription outcome Loudoun experienced after 16 years?

On piecemeal aggregation: Multiple adjacent qualifying sites exist near the Monocacy River. What prevents incremental applications from aggregating into a de facto hyperscale campus?

On Amazon: Amazon purchased property on Gas House Pike from Trammell Crow described as a warehouse. Is that property currently permitted for warehouse use? What in the ZTA prevents conversion to data center use if the amendment is approved?
