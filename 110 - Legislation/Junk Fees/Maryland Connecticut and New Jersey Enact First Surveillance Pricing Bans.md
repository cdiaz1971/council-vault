# Maryland Connecticut and New Jersey Enact First Surveillance Pricing Bans

## Summary

Maryland, Connecticut, and New Jersey became the first states to ban surveillance pricing -- the practice of using personal data like browsing history or shopping habits to set individualized prices on goods and services. Maryland's law, the first in the country, takes effect October 1 and targets grocery stores and food delivery services. At least 11 states considered similar bills this year.

## Key Points

Surveillance pricing is difficult for consumers to detect because they cannot see what other customers are being charged for the same product. The practice uses personal data -- browsing history, shopping patterns, location, demographics -- to generate individualized price points, meaning two people buying the same item may pay different amounts based on what the algorithm knows about them.

Maryland's law applies to grocery stores of at least 15,000 square feet and third-party food delivery services. It prohibits using personal data to set a price for a specific shopper for most groceries. Loyalty programs, subscription prices, and differences based on supply, location, or operating costs are permitted. Businesses get 45 days to correct a violation before enforcement action. Takes effect October 1, 2026.

New Jersey's law, signed by Gov. Mikie Sherrill in July, prohibits retailers from using personal data to set individualized grocery prices and places a one-year moratorium on new electronic shelf label installations while the state studies their effects. Retailers with existing shelf labels can continue using them.

Connecticut's law, signed by Gov. Ned Lamont in June, broadly prohibits retailers and delivery services from surveillance pricing with exceptions for discounts and price differences unrelated to personal data.

Retail and technology groups opposed the legislation as potentially interfering with legitimate discount and rewards programs, and as expensive to comply with.

## Relevance

Maryland's law is already in effect and applies to stores in Frederick. The Golden Mile's concentration of grocery stores and the district's high ALICE rate make this directly relevant to the affordability work -- surveillance pricing is a regressive practice that extracts more from cost-burdened households whose shopping patterns signal financial stress.

The algorithmic pricing ban in the Real Rent Ordinance and the Rockville Chapter 18 peer model (which bans algorithmic rent pricing tools) sit alongside this as part of the same emerging policy category: prohibiting the use of personal data and algorithmic tools to extract more money from vulnerable people. The grocery pricing ban and the rent pricing ban are structurally identical policies operating in different markets. That framing could be useful if building a broader consumer protection narrative connecting housing and food affordability.
