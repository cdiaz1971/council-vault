# Real Rent Ordinance - Workshop Talking Points

## Opening: Name the Problem

What this ordinance does is simple. It says that when a tenant sees a price, that is the price they pay.

Right now that is not how it works. Tenants in Frederick find a unit they can afford, sign the paperwork, and then discover they owe $200 more a month for technology fees, common area maintenance fees, payment processing fees, administrative fees. Fees with names designed to obscure what they are, which is rent.

That is not a disclosure problem. That is a deception problem. This ordinance fixes it.

---

## Why It Is Needed Here

- Frederick's ALICE rate is 43 percent. More than four in ten City households cannot consistently cover basic costs.
  - For those residents, a $150 monthly fee is not an inconvenience. It is the difference between making rent and not.
- Tenants often only learn about mandatory fees after they have paid application fees and been handed a lease.
  - Renters choose a home that appears within their budget, then discover their actual monthly cost is significantly higher.
- District 2 has a foreign-born population 1.4 times the county rate and a poverty rate 1.5 times the county rate.
  - These residents are most likely to be renting, least likely to absorb surprise charges, and least likely to have leverage to push back on a landlord.
  - They are the people this ordinance is built for.

---

## Who It Disproportionately Harms Without It

- Junk fees disproportionately harm people of color and lower-income renters who already struggle with housing affordability. (National Consumer Law Center, 2024)
- Renters of color are more likely to pay application fees than white renters, and pay a higher median application fee.
  - Renters of color are also more likely to submit five or more applications during a housing search, spending hundreds of dollars with no guarantee of housing.
- About 58 percent of Black households and 52 percent of Hispanic households rent their housing, compared to about 28 percent of white households.
  - 54 percent of Black renter households and 52 percent of Hispanic renter households are cost-burdened, meaning more than 30 percent of income goes to rent and utilities.
- When colleagues say they want to make housing more affordable and more equitable, this is a concrete mechanism for doing that without spending a dollar of city money.

---

## What Other Jurisdictions Have Done

- **Maryland (HB 1257, 2025 and re-introduced 2026):** Prohibits landlords from advertising rent without disclosing mandatory fees. Bans landlords from imposing any fee not previously disclosed. Authorizes tenants to sue for violations up to two years after tenancy ends.
  - Maryland's own Department of Housing and Community Development testified favorably on this bill.
  - The bill explicitly authorizes local jurisdictions to enact stronger protections. Frederick does not need to wait for Annapolis.

- **Takoma Park, Maryland:** Has had a comprehensive fee ordinance covering permitted, optional, and prohibited fees on the books since 2003, substantially updated in 2013 and 2021. That is more than two decades of Maryland precedent.
  - This is not a legal novelty in this state. Another Maryland city has been doing it for over twenty years.

- **Colorado (effective January 1, 2026):** Requires landlords to advertise a single total price incorporating all mandatory costs. Bans passing common area maintenance through as a separate charge.
  - This is structurally identical to what Frederick's ordinance requires.

- **Bellingham, Washington (effective August 2025):** Requires disclosure of all mandatory fees in any advertisement or rental application. Prohibits fees for in-unit appliance use, payment processing, and mail handling.

- **The national trend:** More than 20 states have now regulated rental housing junk fees. Colorado, Shoreline, Ann Arbor, New York City, and San Diego have all taken action using different models.
  - Evidence from early regulations shows that even when some costs shift to base rent, tenants benefit from reduced surprise charges and a better ability to budget and compare units across the market.

---

## Answering the Main Objection: "Landlords Will Just Raise the Base Rent"

Some landlords argue that if separate fees are banned, costs will move into base rent. Advocates counter that this outcome is preferable because a single, all-in price is more transparent and easier to compare across units.

The objection actually proves the point. If a landlord rolls the fees into rent, the tenant sees the real cost upfront, can compare it accurately across units, and can make an informed decision. That is the goal. Transparency is the policy. Whether it takes the form of a fee or rent does not change what the tenant owes. It changes whether they know about it before signing.

---

## Other Potential Obstacles

**Preemption**
- Opponents may argue that landlord-tenant regulation is a state matter and the city lacks authority to act.
- Counter: HB 1257 explicitly authorizes local jurisdictions to enact stronger protections. Rachel Gray has completed legal review. This is tied to the city's existing rental licensing authority, which is well-established.

**Enforcement capacity**
- Council members may ask who enforces this and whether the city has staff for it.
- Counter: Enforcement rides on the existing rental licensing infrastructure. Violations are municipal infractions with fines, and license denial or revocation is an additional lever. No new department or significant staffing is required.

**Definition of "mandatory fee"**
- Landlords or their advocates may argue the 25 percent threshold in the mandatory fee definition is arbitrary or unworkable.
- Counter: The 25 percent presumption is a floor, not a ceiling. A fee is also presumed mandatory if refusal results in lease denial or less favorable terms. The dual trigger is intentional and defensible.

**Small landlord hardship**
- Expect claims that mom-and-pop landlords will be disproportionately burdened by compliance.
- Counter: Compliant landlords have nothing to change. The burden falls entirely on landlords currently hiding costs. The ordinance does not cap what landlords can charge.

**January 2027 effective date**
- Someone may push for a longer lead time or a phased rollout.
- Counter: The date applies only to new leases and renewals executed after January 1, 2027. Landlords have the remainder of 2026 to adjust advertising and lease templates. That is adequate runway.

**"This will chill rental housing investment"**
- A broader economic argument that fee restrictions reduce landlord returns and discourage investment.
- Counter: Colorado, Bellingham, and Takoma Park have not produced evidence of market exit as a result of fee transparency requirements. This conflates fee bans with rent control, which this ordinance is not.

**Brehm on rental licensing scope**
- Peter Brehm has previously argued that the rental licensing regime is already overburdened. He may use this as another regulatory layer objection.
- Counter: The ordinance is additive to licensing but does not change inspection or compliance workflows. It adds a pricing condition to an existing license, not a new enforcement apparatus.

---

## Connecting to What Council Members Have Said

Every member of this council has said publicly that housing affordability is a priority. This ordinance delivers on that directly, without a subsidy, without a tax increase, without a zoning fight.

It does not cap rent. It does not dictate what landlords can charge. It says only that whatever you charge, you must charge it honestly and upfront. That is a principle most residents across the political spectrum support.

The city already licenses rental housing. This ordinance makes the licensing regime more meaningful by requiring honest pricing as a condition of that license. Enforcement rides on a system that already exists.

## Political Watch

Derek Shackelford is the swing vote most worth watching. His questions will likely center on enforceability and landlord impact rather than principle. Frame answers around the simplicity of compliance for honest landlords.

## One-Line Closer

The ordinance does not tell landlords what to charge. It tells them to be honest about it. That is all.

---

## Hostile Question Prep

**"Doesn't this just add regulatory burden on small landlords?"**
The compliance obligation is simple. Include your mandatory costs in the advertised price. If a small landlord is already pricing transparently, they have nothing to change. The burden falls on landlords who are currently hiding costs. That is a feature, not a bug.
