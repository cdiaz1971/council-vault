# Real Rent Ordinance - Legal Review Prep

## Summary

Internal prep memo comparing the original Real Rent Ordinance draft to the City Attorney's revised version. Identifies which removals are legally grounded vs. editorial, analyzes the Aleti citation, and builds counter-arguments for restoring key provisions.

## What Legal Changed

The original draft was a standalone 14-section ordinance. The City Attorney's office converted it to a code amendment adding Chapter 12.5, Article VI (sections 12.5-89 through 12.5-96). Iris Annan (Assistant City Attorney) provided a summary email characterizing most changes as organizational.

**Structural change:** Standalone ordinance converted to code amendment. WHEREAS recitals removed. Optional Service definition relocated from definitions to exceptions section.

**Key provisions removed:**
- Anti-circumvention section
- Private right of action (treble damages and fee-shifting)
- Mandatory rental licensing consequences as a standalone provision (consolidated into penalties)
- Per-unit penalty multipliers
- Security deposit protections
- General burden-shifting presumption
- All WHEREAS recitals / Findings section

**Added by legal:** Pet deposit section not in original draft.

## Which Removals Are Legally Grounded vs. Editorial

Legal's email draws a clear line, though it is not stated that way explicitly.

**Legally grounded removals (two):**

Private right of action: Legal cited Aleti v. Metropolitan Baltimore LLC, 479 Md. 696 (2022). This is a real legal argument, not just a drafting preference. See Aleti analysis below.

Security deposit provisions: Legal cited state preemption. Maryland Real Property Article governs security deposits, and local ordinances cannot override state law here. This removal is correct and should not be contested.

**Editorial/drafting-preference removals (everything else):**

Anti-circumvention: Legal's stated reason is that "the ordinance's intent, purpose and application are clearly articulated." This is a drafting judgment, not a legal conclusion. It is contestable.

Findings/WHEREAS recitals: Legal said they were "unnecessary since the purpose is clearly articulated." Again a drafting preference, not a legal requirement.

Licensing compliance as standalone provision: Characterized as a consolidation move for coherence. Substantively neutral if the penalties section captures the same content.

Severability: Legal noted it is covered by Code section 1-6. This is correct and the removal is not a problem.

## Aleti Analysis

Aleti v. Metropolitan Baltimore LLC, 479 Md. 696 (2022) arose from Baltimore City's rental registration ordinance. The Court of Appeals held that Baltimore's ordinance did not create an implied private right of action because the legislative history and text did not express clear intent to benefit a specific class of persons (tenants) with a private enforcement remedy.

The key word is implied. The Aleti holding forecloses implied private rights of action where legislative intent is ambiguous. It does not categorically bar explicit private enforcement authority tied to a licensing regime.

The distinction matters. If the ordinance expressly states that tenants may bring a civil action for violations, the Aleti concern is addressed. The problem is that Frederick's charter authority to create explicit private rights of action in housing matters has not been confirmed. That is the open question, not Aleti itself.

Iris's email characterizes Aleti as suggesting "municipalities cannot establish a private right of action in landlord-tenant matters unless expressly authorized by the General Assembly." That reading is broader than what Aleti held. Aleti says implied rights do not exist without clear intent. It does not say express rights require General Assembly authorization. That distinction is worth pressing in any conversation with Rachel Gray.

## Counter-Argument on Anti-Circumvention

Legal's position: the purpose is clearly articulated, making anti-circumvention unnecessary.

**First weakness: clarity of purpose does not equal enforceability against evasion.**

A clearly stated purpose tells a court what the ordinance means. It does not tell a landlord what conduct is prohibited when they structure around the literal text. Anti-circumvention provisions exist because bad actors do not argue the ordinance is unclear. They argue their specific conduct falls outside it. The third-party vendor scenario is the clearest example. A landlord routing a $150/month "resident benefits package" through a subsidiary can argue in good faith that the fee is not charged by the landlord, that the tenant is contracting with a third party, and that the ordinance does not reach that relationship. The purpose clause does not answer that argument. An explicit anti-circumvention provision does.

**Second weakness: legal's own revision created the gap.**

The original draft defined "optional service" in the definitions section. Legal moved it to the exceptions section. That move is actually an argument for anti-circumvention language, not against it. The exceptions section now defines what landlords can charge, and any fee a landlord can characterize as an "optional service" potentially falls into that exception. Without anti-circumvention language, a mandatory fee bundled as an "optional" enrollment becomes a factual dispute rather than a clear violation.

**The constructive ask:**

Rather than arguing to restore the full anti-circumvention section, the narrower and more defensible ask is a single sentence added to the scope or enforcement section: the substance and effect of a charge controls, not its label or the identity of the party collecting it.

This is not a new provision. It is a drafting clarification that closes the gap legal's own reorganization created.
