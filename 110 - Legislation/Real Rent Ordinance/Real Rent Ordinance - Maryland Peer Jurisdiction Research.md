# Real Rent Ordinance - Maryland Peer Jurisdiction Research

## Summary

Maryland peer jurisdiction research for the Real Rent Ordinance. Covers Montgomery County Bill 6-25 and Baltimore City's rental licensing framework, with analysis of the licensing-condition theory and its application to Frederick.

## Montgomery County Bill 6-25

Montgomery County introduced Bill 6-25 as a rental junk fee ordinance in 2025. It uses a permitted-fees framework, meaning landlords may only charge fees explicitly authorized by ordinance rather than prohibiting specific fees. This is the inverse of Frederick's approach.

Key features:
- Covers "regulated rental units" tied to Montgomery County's existing rent stabilization regime
- Explicitly enumerates permissible charges; all other charges are prohibited by default
- Enforcement through the county's existing rental licensing and registration infrastructure

The Montgomery County model is the strongest structural peer because it is in-state, uses licensing authority as the enforcement hook, and has been through local legal review. The limitation is that its scope is tied to rent stabilization, which Frederick does not have. Adapting the permitted-fees framework to Frederick would require an independent definition of covered units.

## Baltimore City Framework

Baltimore City's rental licensing ordinance ties licensing compliance to habitability and registration requirements. The Aleti case arose from this framework. Baltimore did not expressly authorize a private right of action in its licensing ordinance, which is how the implied-right question got to the Court of Appeals.

The lesson from Baltimore is structural: the enforcement gap Aleti identified can be closed by express language, not by assuming a private right exists. Baltimore's ordinance also illustrates the value of clear legislative intent language in the text itself, rather than relying on recitals or purpose statements alone.

## The Licensing-Condition Theory

The core doctrinal argument for Frederick's ordinance surviving preemption challenge is that it is a condition of licensure, not a regulation of the landlord-tenant relationship.

Maryland's landlord-tenant law (Real Property Article) governs the rights and obligations of landlords and tenants in their lease relationship. Local ordinances that try to alter those rights face preemption risk. But local governments have broad authority to set conditions on business licenses, including rental licenses. A landlord operating a rental unit without a license is not in a landlord-tenant relationship the state is protecting; they are operating an unlicensed business a locality can regulate.

Frederick already requires rental licenses under its code. An ordinance that says "you may not charge prohibited fees as a condition of maintaining your rental license" is a licensing ordinance, not a landlord-tenant ordinance. That framing does real legal work. It is the same theory Baltimore should have used more explicitly before Aleti.

## Application to Frederick

Frederick's Real Rent Ordinance is structured as a code amendment to Chapter 12.5, the same chapter governing local licensing and business regulation. That placement supports the licensing-condition theory.

The remaining preemption risk is security deposits, which the City Attorney correctly flagged. Maryland Real Property Article section 8-203 et seq. governs security deposits comprehensively and the state has occupied that field. The removal of security deposit provisions from the ordinance is legally correct.

The anti-circumvention gap matters here too. If third-party vendors can route fees outside the ordinance's reach, the licensing-condition hook weakens. A landlord can argue their license is compliant while a subsidiary charges prohibited fees. The single-sentence clarification proposed in the Legal Review Prep note (substance and effect of a charge controls, not its label or the identity of the party collecting it) closes this gap within the licensing-condition framework.

## Research Gaps

- No confirmed search result on whether Bill 6-25 passed or was amended before enactment. Confirm current status before citing as a peer model.
- No analysis yet of whether any other Maryland jurisdiction has enacted a junk fee ordinance using the licensing-condition theory explicitly. Worth checking Prince George's County and Takoma Park.
- Frederick's charter authority to create express private rights of action in housing/licensing matters has not been confirmed. This is the threshold question that drives whether a private right of action can be restored.
