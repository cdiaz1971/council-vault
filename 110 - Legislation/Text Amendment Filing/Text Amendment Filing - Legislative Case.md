# Text Amendment Filing - Legislative Case

## Bottom line

This amendment moves the power to propose citywide zoning rules from whoever can pay a filing fee and a law firm to the people who answer to voters. It keeps every public hearing, leaves zoning map amendments alone, gives residents a free route through their Council member, and puts Frederick in line with Rockville, Montgomery County, and Frederick County. The legal risk is low. The political risk sits in three places: the line "residents can no longer file," the Council President's scheduling role, and the question of whether developers will simply route the same amendments through friendly officials. All three have strong answers, and none requires changing the text, which is final as submitted.

Being the applicant, you and Brehm present the case at the Planning Commission hearing. That is a different role from the data center ZTA, where Council members stay out of the Commission's process. Here you are the applicant, so presenting is expected.

## What the text does

Quoted from [[Text Amendment Filing - Submitted Text 2026-10-26]].

1. **Who may file.** "An elected City official; or The Division." City residents and City property owners are struck.
2. **Council review of an official's application.** The Division sends it "to the City Council for review at a public meeting scheduled by the Council President." The sponsor gives "a broad overview of the text amendment concept, including its alignment with the City Comprehensive Plan, as well as potential benefits and areas of concern." Members "will discuss the proposal and offer recommendations but will not take a formal vote."
3. **Division applications.** The Division gives Council notice and the same broad overview.
4. **Commission hearing timing.** Scheduled "upon completion of the application review," then "as soon as practicable."
5. **Recommendation and Council hearing.** The recommendation goes to "the City Council President," who "will schedule a public hearing by the City Council to be held as soon as practicable." "Board of Aldermen" becomes "City Council." The Council decides "in the same manner as it takes action on any other ordinance."

What it does not do: change zoning map amendments, remove either public hearing, give Council a vote before the Commission, or change the standard for the final decision.

## The problem it solves

**Citywide rules are being written one site at a time, by applicants.** Recent private filings came from Trammell Crow (data centers in M1 and M2), Wormald (gas pumps at convenience stores), GG Frederick Holdings (ground-floor residential in MU districts), and Frederick Health (hospitals in mixed-use districts). Most were prepared by one land use firm. Each changes the code for the whole city to fit one company's plans. Brehm's constituents called them "one-off wonders based upon specific development proposals" (FNP, October 23, 2026). See [[Frederick Text Amendment Data Baseline]].

**Residents already cannot use the route the code nominally gives them.** The fee is $4,400 (confirm against the current fee schedule), plus counsel and consultants. The one resident-originated amendment in the record, Kevin Sellner's brownfield amendment, went to Council Members Kuzemchak and Nash instead of being filed, because the fee "would have been a showstopper for us as residents" (FNP, December 30, 2025). Hempel Irani said the same at the workshop: text amendments are mainly brought by developers and the cost keeps ordinary residents out. The amendment does not take a working tool from residents. It turns the route they actually use into the official one, and that route is free.

**Council learns about code changes late.** Under the current process an application goes from staff to the Commission with no public moment where the elected body hears the concept. Members get asked about amendments they have never seen. The new review meeting is a public, on-the-record first look.

**Staff time follows applicants, not priorities.** Planning staff process every complete private application, including ones that will not survive, while the Council's own priorities (missing middle, parking, ADUs, the LMC review) wait. PC25-626ZTA alone has gone through at least three submittals.

## Evidence and peer practice

- **Rockville** adopted a new zoning ordinance effective August 1, 2026, in which "The Mayor and Council may make an application for an amendment to the text of this Chapter" (Sec. 25.1.4.2(a)). No private filing route is in the section. Frederick's amendment is broader, since any elected official and the Division may file.
- **Montgomery County.** The District Council introduces ZTAs; the Planning Board and others request introduction.
- **Frederick County.** "The County Council may initiate a zoning text amendment with no particular form required" (§ 1-19-3.100.2). Whether the County also takes private applications under another section is not yet confirmed, so do not claim it bars them.
- **Gaithersburg.** The text amendment application requires a "Requested Sponsor," the Planning Commission or the Mayor and City Council. That is the sponsorship model this amendment uses.

## Legal authority and risk

Overall risk: **low.** Detail in [[Maryland Legal Baseline and Local Authority - Text Amendment Filing]].

- Land Use § 4-203 makes the legislative body "provide for the manner in which its zoning regulations ... shall be established, enforced, and amended." It does not say who initiates.
- The same section guarantees a public hearing "at which parties in interest and citizens have an opportunity to be heard." Both hearings stay. Residents keep their voice.
- Map amendments (§ 4-204) are untouched, which is the answer to any property rights claim.
- The only moderate item is the text's silence on applications already on file. Treat it as a question for Rachel Gray, advisory, and keep the data center question on the moratorium track.

## Fiscal impact

The executive summary left this blank, so expect the question at the Commission and at First Read.

- **Revenue.** Text amendment fees from private applicants go away, because Council members filing officially pay no fee under the 2026 Rules. The amount is private filings per year times the fee. Get the real count from Planning before using any figure.
- **Costs avoided.** Staff hours on private applications that do not fit the Comprehensive Plan, and repeat review rounds.
- **The Graditor argument.** Applicants paid for studies, renderings, and engineering. Nothing in the text stops a person who asks a Council member to sponsor from preparing and paying for that supporting work. What changes is who signs the application and answers for it in public.
- **Spoken answer.** "The fiscal effect is small and it runs both ways: some fee revenue goes away, and staff stop spending time on applications that were never going to fit the Comprehensive Plan. We have asked Planning for the five-year numbers."

## Neighborhood Advisory Council impact

Also blank in the executive summary. Spoken answer: NACs gain an earlier public look at every proposed text amendment through the Council review meeting, before the Commission hearing, and any NAC can bring an idea to its Council member at no cost.

## Equity impact

Also blank in the executive summary. The honest picture:

- **Gains.** Residents generally get earlier notice of code changes and a free route to propose them. Code changes shift from site-driven to policy-driven, which matters most in neighborhoods like District 2 that absorb the effects of citywide rules without paid representation at the table.
- **Losses.** Residents and small property owners lose a direct filing right they could rarely afford to use. Developers lose direct access and the ability to set the timing of a code change.
- **The real equity risk.** If sponsorship becomes a favor for the well connected, access moves from a public application to private conversations, and residents without relationships lose ground. The answer is a public, documented way to ask any member to sponsor, and your own commitment to disclose who asked for any amendment you file. Neither needs a text change (see the next section).
- **Housing.** Some private amendments have been pro-housing, like GG Frederick's ground-floor residential change. The Council carried that idea too: you and Nash had drafts in July. A good idea finds a sponsor.

## Gaps in the text and how to handle each without amending it

The text is final. Each gap below has an answer that lives in testimony, the Council Rules, or your own practice.

| Gap | Opposition line | Handle it with |
|---|---|---|
| No deadline for the Council President to schedule the review meeting | "One person can bury any amendment" | Public commitment: every complete application goes on the next available workshop agenda. Offer to put a deadline in the Council Rules of Procedure, which Council controls by majority (Charter § 11). Remind them the Council can compel agenda inclusion (§ 15(a)) and that the Council elects its President |
| Council President receives the recommendation and schedules the Council hearing | "Power grab from the Mayor" | Conforming change. The old language dates from the Board of Aldermen; the Mayor is no longer a member of Council. Council already routes Planning items through Leadership with the Planning Department. Text amendments become ordinances like any other |
| No transition rule for pending applications | "Retroactive; aimed at the data center developer" | "This bill is about how future amendments begin. Pending applications are a legal question for the City Attorney. The data center question is being handled through the moratorium, on its own merits." Do not let the bill become a proxy for PC25-626ZTA |
| No sponsorship process or disclosure | "Developers will launder amendments through friendly members" | Your practice: you will disclose in the application and at the review meeting who requested or drafted any amendment you file. Propose the same in the Rules for all members. The review meeting itself forces every sponsor to present in public and answer for Comprehensive Plan fit |
| Division filings get notice only | "Staff gets a pass the Council does not" | Staff applications already reflect administration policy and still go through both public hearings. The notice and overview requirement is new transparency that does not exist today |
| "Elected City official" includes the Mayor | "So the Mayor can still file" or "you cut out the Mayor" | Yes, the Mayor can file, and the Mayor's applications go to the same public Council review. That is accountability applied evenly |
| Executive summary justification mentions Council approval; the text has no vote | "Which is it?" | Say it plainly: the workshop version had a vote, it was dropped deliberately so four members cannot kill an idea before the Commission weighs in, and the review is discussion only |
| Exec summary fiscal, NAC, and equity fields blank | "Did anyone analyze this?" | Use the spoken answers above, and bring the data request to Planning |

## Who benefits, who pays

| Group | Effect |
|---|---|
| Residents and NACs | Free route to propose; earlier public look; hearings unchanged |
| Planning staff | Work aligned to public priorities; fewer non-viable applications; new duty to give Council notice of staff applications |
| Developers and land use counsel | Lose direct filing and timing control; must persuade an elected sponsor in public |
| Council members | Gain responsibility; must field and answer for sponsorship requests |
| Mayor | Loses scheduling of the Council hearing on text amendments; keeps filing authority and the veto |
