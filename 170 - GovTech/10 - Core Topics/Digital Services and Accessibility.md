# Digital Services and Accessibility

## Purpose

This page supports Council decisions on how residents reach City services online: legal accessibility duties, service design, data transparency, and connectivity.

## ADA web and mobile accessibility

- Under the Justice Department's 2024 Title II rule, state and local government websites and mobile apps must meet WCAG 2.1 Level AA. After an April 20, 2026 interim final rule, entities serving 50,000 or more people, including the City, must comply by April 26, 2027; smaller entities by April 26, 2028 ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2026/04/20/2026-07663/extension-of-compliance-dates-for-nondiscrimination-on-the-basis-of-disability-accessibility-of-web)).
- The extension does not remove existing liability; governments can be sued over inaccessible services now ([[Digital ADA Compliance for Local Governments -- Extended Deadline Same Liability]]; [[ADA Web Accessibility Deadline -- One-Year Extension for State and Local Governments]]).
- State CIOs rank accessibility a top priority, and collaboration between IT and accessibility staff is the main success factor.
- The City's compliance status, including documents, forms, meeting video, and third-party portals: verify.

## Service design

- **One door.** Maryland's single application for five benefit programs reached 500,000 users in its first year and cut delivery times.
- **Small fixes.** North Carolina cut SNAP payment errors with one caseworker prompt used over 20,000 times.
- **Procedural loss.** About 320,000 of Maryland's 1.47 million Medicaid enrollees face new federal work reporting and six-month redetermination rules; most coverage losses are expected among eligible people who miss a notice. Maryland is redesigning notices and caseworker tools with Code for America. City services that help residents through these systems face the same risk.
- **Small teams.** Broward County, Florida, runs its public website with a four-person team that is using AI to rebuild the site on a commercial platform, with the stated goal of organizing official content once so it stays accurate when search engines and AI tools summarize it ([Sitecore announcement](https://www.sitecore.com/company/newsroom/press-releases/2026/03/broward-county-selects-sitecoreai-to-help-ensure-residents-find-clear-trusted-information-online); [[Broward County Four-Person Web Team Uses AI to Rebuild County Website]]). The relevant lessons for the City are the staffing ratio and the AI-search goal; the platform choice is a long vendor commitment that belongs in [[AI Governance and Procurement]] review.
- **Modernization.** Kansas replaced 1970s unemployment systems in 29 months, moving availability from about 65% to around the clock and cutting training from eight weeks to two.
- **Federal systems.** Federal tools do not always fit local needs; most states declined CMS's free Medicaid verification tools.
- **Identity.** States are building unified digital ID to cut fraud and simplify access; any City use must respect immigrant residents' privacy under the [[City Values Act]].
- **Operations data.** Connecting IT with operational systems lets cities move from reactive to proactive service. Baltimore uses drones to inspect vacant properties on public land under community notice rules.

## Language access

- Real-time AI-assisted translation and captioning at public meetings is now low-cost and technically simple enough for under-resourced local governments to pilot, letting non-English-speaking residents follow and participate without waiting on a scheduled human interpreter ([[Real-Time AI Translation at Public Meetings -- Government Accessibility Tool]]). Frederick's Spanish-speaking population, served locally by Centro Hispano de Frederick, is the most direct beneficiary; a Spanish-captioning pilot for Council meetings is the lowest-cost starting point.
- Minnesota's Department of Human Services built a four-question framework for deciding when AI translation is appropriate for government content (does the content warrant it, what resources exist, which product, how to prepare content for quality) and pairs AI generation with mandatory human quality assurance; the hybrid model cut translation time roughly in half for Spanish, Somali, and Hmong content ([[AI Translation in Government Services -- Minnesota DHS Framework]]). Procurement-level detail on evaluating and contracting for AI translation tools is covered in [[AI Governance and Procurement]].
- Which City forms, notices, and web content are available in languages other than English: verify.

## Transparency tools

Philadelphia's Kensington dashboard gives residents a neighborhood-level view of conditions and progress. Harvard's Allen Lab offers free tools to small governments, starting with an open-source budget transparency platform.

## Connectivity

Maryland is expanding fiber to rural western counties with federal funds. Treating broadband and data as public infrastructure is one way cities reduce dependence on private platforms. City-level gaps in home internet access: verify with ACS data.

## Policy options

| Option | Addresses | Tradeoffs |
|---|---|---|
| Fund an accessibility audit and remediation plan ahead of April 2027 | Legal duty and access | Cost; ongoing maintenance |
| Accessibility review in all technology purchases | Inaccessible new tools | Narrows vendor choice |
| Plain-language and multilingual standards for notices and forms | Lost access through confusing notices | Staff time |
| AI-assisted translation pilot for public meetings and notices, with mandatory human QA | Language barriers to participation | Accuracy risk; interpreter costs remain for high-stakes matters |
| Public service dashboards | Transparency | Data quality work |
| Digital navigator support for residents | Residents left behind by online-only services | Staffing |
