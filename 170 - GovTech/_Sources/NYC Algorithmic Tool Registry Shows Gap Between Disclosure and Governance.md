# NYC Algorithmic Tool Registry Shows Gap Between Disclosure and Governance

## Summary

NYC's mandatory algorithmic tool disclosure list -- required since 2020 -- shows that transparency requirements alone don't produce governance: the registry is incomplete, critics call it imperfect, and tools range from decades-old EMS routing models to current-year ChatGPT deployments with little consistency in how use is described or evaluated.

## Key Points

Since 2020, NYC agencies have been required to publish an annual list of "algorithmic tools" in use, with brief descriptions, procurement details, and notes on whether the tool collects identifying information. The most recent report covers tools used in 2025.

The range of what the list includes is striking:

- **ChatGPT**: used by five city offices for brainstorming social media content, drafting emails, writing alt text, and interview questions. Also used in the now-abandoned MyCity chatbot.
- **Google Gemini**: used by the Department of Environmental Protection to generate an icon image for a city app.
- **Idling Complaints Program**: AI tool by contractor Acuvate analyzes citizen-submitted photos and videos of vehicle idling and makes enforcement recommendations. Staff retain final decision authority.
- **311 Mobile App AI Smart Select**: photo-matching tool that routes 311 complaint photos to relevant service request categories.
- **Virtual Assistant Chatbot (DYCD)**: Microsoft Azure Language-powered chatbot for afterschool and family program questions, averaging 2,100 requests per day.
- **HELIX (Dept of Health)**: generative tool allowing staff to ask questions of GPT-4o, optionally grounded on approved agency documents.
- **M365 Copilot**: drafting social media, translating content, simplifying emergency preparedness language for public audiences.
- **Voice to Vision**: MIT CCC research project using GPT models and Claude to geolocate, group, and summarize public planning comments for two neighborhood plan processes. Results published on public-facing websites.
- **Mufflers Noise Violations**: camera-based noise monitoring that analyzes recordings and reads license plates to assist DEP enforcement staff.
- **Fusus CORE Elite AI (Axon)**: AI cameras that detect objects, people, or motion events and issue real-time alerts to the city's Technology Operations Center.
- **OpusClip**: AI video tool that analyzes DYCD podcast footage to identify shareable moments for social media.
- **Algebra Teaching Assistant**: DOE tool generating responses to algebra-related teacher and student prompts, trained on the specific curriculum.

Critics describe the list as imperfect -- not all tools are captured, descriptions vary widely in specificity, and there is no consistent framework for assessing risk level or governance requirements across the range of deployments.

The governance gap is visible in the tool categories: a Gemini use to generate an icon image and a Fusus AI surveillance system generating real-time alerts to a security operations center appear on the same list with similar description formats, as if they present comparable governance challenges. They don't.

## Relevance

This is directly useful for building Frederick's AI procurement governance case. The NYC example shows what a disclosure-only approach produces: a list that demonstrates breadth of AI use but provides no governance framework for distinguishing high-risk from low-risk tools, no accountability mechanism beyond publication, and no way for residents or council members to understand what oversight exists.

The [[When AI Governs vs When AI Assists Procurement Distinctions for Local Government]] framework provides exactly what the NYC list lacks: a principled distinction between tools that assist human decision-making and tools that make or drive decisions. The Fusus surveillance system and the Idling Complaints Program both warrant scrutiny under that framework -- Fusus because it generates real-time alerts that drive operational response, the idling tool because even with staff retaining "final authority," the AI recommendation shapes what gets reviewed.

Voice to Vision is worth noting separately: using Claude and GPT to synthesize and geolocate public planning comments is a genuinely assistive use -- AI making the input more usable for human planners, not substituting for planning judgment. That's the positive model.
