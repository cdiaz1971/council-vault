# Government Modernization Fails When Treated as an IT Project

## Summary
An EY public sector leader argues state and local modernization stalls on three assumptions: that it is an IT project leadership can delegate, that people will adopt whatever gets built, and that change management is training at the end.

## Key Points
The first myth is that modernization belongs to IT and leadership can hand it off. Fishman argues the work starts with leadership alignment on the problem, the outcomes, and the operating changes needed, before any platform choice. The diagnostic questions are where delays occur, what blocks the agency's objectives, and which processes burden employees or residents. The answers usually point to process, unclear ownership, policy constraints, or competing priorities rather than software. He cites one effort where IT-led framing produced requirement gaps, weak business prioritization, and disconnected users, and later rollouts that improved once business leaders were involved from the start and staff took part in design and testing. The conclusion is shared ownership across program, operations, policy, and technology leaders.

The second myth is that if you build it, people will use it. Launch is not the finish line. Staff need practical training and clear direction, leaders need visibility into operational effects, and feedback has to be gathered and acted on as problems surface. The people closest to the work know where friction will appear. Residents differ in needs and digital access, so a system that works administratively can still create barriers for the public. Portal redesigns benefit from up-front research with staff, residents, and outside stakeholders to find where government language, complex journeys, or information overload get in the way.

The third myth is that change management means communication and training at the end. Government faces leadership turnover, policy shifts, and funding changes, so modernization needs governance that lets leaders reprioritize without losing momentum, and phased delivery that shows value early. A motor vehicle agency example combined workflow automation with executive sponsorship, governance, and agile delivery and now processes more than one million transactions a quarter while saving over 60,000 hours.

On AI and automation, the same rule applies. Tie the tool to a defined objective rather than treating modernization as a series of technology purchases.

The closing principles are to define the problem before the solution, build adoption into the strategy, plan for changing priorities and leadership transitions, and measure outcomes that matter to the public.

## Relevance
This is a consulting-firm op-ed, so it is advocacy for a service model as much as analysis, but the framing is useful for Council oversight of City technology spending. The diagnostic questions translate directly into questions Council can ask the CTO's office when a system purchase comes forward: what problem does it solve, who owns the business process, how will adoption be measured, and what happens when priorities change.

It reinforces the incremental approach in [[Incremental GovTech Modernization -- The Single Pain Point Strategy for Small Municipalities]] and the staffing argument in [[Digital Service Teams as Government Modernization Infrastructure]]. The resident-access point also connects to the procurement governance thread, since a tool that serves staff can still exclude residents with limited digital access or English proficiency.
