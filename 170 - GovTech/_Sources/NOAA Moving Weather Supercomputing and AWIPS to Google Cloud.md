# NOAA Moving Weather Supercomputing and AWIPS to Google Cloud

## Summary
NOAA is moving its operational weather supercomputing to Google Cloud, targeting a full-scale system by end of 2026 and go-live by end of 2027, and moving forecasters' AWIPS workstation system to the cloud by early 2028 to stop discarding data and tying forecasters to office hardware.

## Key Points
NOAA runs two physical supercomputers for the Weather and Climate Operational Supercomputing System (WCOSS), each at 14.5 petaflops, which produce the models used across NOAA and shared publicly. They have roughly five-year service lives, and David Michaud, director of NCEP Central Operations, says NOAA is pushing the limits of the physical infrastructure and that the systems are hard to modify.

Moving model output to NOAA's 122 local forecast offices often means cutting variables and atmospheric layers to shrink the data. Administrator Neil Jacobs said at the Google Public Sector Summit that his bigger problem is not trapped data but data that has been deleted and can never be recovered.

The plan shifts high-performance computing from on-premises hardware to the cloud, with Google Cloud as primary provider for the WCOSS modernization. A full-scale system is targeted for end of 2026 and go-live for end of 2027. NOAA is still learning how its models behave in the cloud and how to configure it.

Separately, National Weather Service forecasters currently download data to Linux workstations in their offices and process it locally. If an office evacuates, others must cover, and resolution upgrades reach forecasters late. NOAA will move the Advanced Weather Interactive Processing System (AWIPS) to the cloud by early 2028 so forecasters can work from anywhere.

## Relevance
A large federal example of the cloud trade-off cities face at much smaller scale: flexibility, resilience, and remote continuity of operations against long-term vendor dependence on a single provider. Useful as a reference case when Frederick's CTO (Matt Bowman) brings infrastructure or continuity-of-operations proposals, and for the question of what happens to public data and public capability when it lives on a private platform. Weather data also feeds local emergency management, so NWS reliability and continuity matter to the city directly.
