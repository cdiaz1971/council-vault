# AI Translation in Government Requires Procurement Discipline, Not Blanket Adoption

## Summary

AI translation tools are increasingly deployed by state and local governments to expand language access for public meetings, benefits communications, and service delivery. The technology offers real cost and reach advantages over human interpreters, but requires deliberate procurement discipline, local calibration, and ongoing human oversight -- particularly for interactions where accuracy failures could harm residents.

## Key Points

Washington state passed a law requiring the state Office of Equity to develop standardized language access guidelines. California passed legislation requiring multilingual translation of public meeting agendas starting July 1, 2026. These legislative moves reflect a genuine priority to expand language access -- and a recognition that AI offers a more scalable path than human interpreter hiring alone.

The cost case for AI is strong: a survey of 200+ event and meeting professionals found that 95% agreed AI translation tools are easier and more affordable than human interpreters, and 66% now prefer AI-powered solutions. But the cost argument can lead governments toward blanket adoption without adequate vetting.

Suresh Venkatasubramanian of Brown University's Center for Tech Responsibility offers the key caution: "Serving a population is not just about the cheapest way to do it, but the most efficacious way to do it." The recommended approach:

Start with temporary pilot contracts rather than permanent vendor agreements. This preserves leverage to customize, evaluate, and exit if the tool underperforms.

Jurisdictions have highly specific vocabulary -- department names, document titles, geographic references, local program names -- that generic AI translation models may not handle correctly. A tool that worked in one city may fail in another for this reason. Local calibration is required.

Human interpreters are most valuable not as a full replacement for AI but as validators of AI outputs. Interpreters can assess nuance, catch meaning errors, and handle regional dialect variation that AI models miss.

Wisconsin's legislature attempted to allow AI translators to replace human interpreters in courtrooms -- the bill died after sustained opposition from translation professionals who argued AI is not suitable for contexts where accuracy is a legal matter.

Boston's generative AI guidelines (2023) explicitly limit municipal staff reliance on AI for translations and require review by language-proficient staff before using AI-generated content in other languages.

The Minnesota DHS framework for assessing whether AI is the right translation solution is a useful procurement reference tool.

## Relevance

Frederick's immigrant community -- concentrated in District 2, with foreign-born population 1.4x the county rate -- depends on language access for public participation, benefits enrollment, and safety communications. The City Values Act and Frederick Rapid Response Network both operate in the same language access environment.

If Frederick explores AI translation for council meetings, city communications, or HHS service delivery, the due diligence framework here is the right standard: pilot contracts, local vocabulary calibration, and human interpreter validation for high-stakes interactions. This is also directly relevant to the surveillance procurement governance thread -- the same procurement discipline that applies to surveillance technology applies to AI translation tools: pilot before permanent, contract language controls data use, audit mechanisms built in.
