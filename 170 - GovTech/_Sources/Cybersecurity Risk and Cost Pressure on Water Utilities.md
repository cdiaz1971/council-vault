# Cybersecurity Risk and Cost Pressure on Water Utilities

## Summary
Cyberattacks on water and wastewater utilities are rising nationally and carry real fiscal costs, and Maryland's Modernize Maryland Act of 2022 already requires cybersecurity assessments and plans for larger systems.

## Key Points
- The FBI received reports of cyber incidents affecting water systems in at least seven states over a few days in July 2026. A coordinated cyberattack disrupted water utilities in more than 30 Minnesota communities in July 2026, forcing some systems offline or onto manual operation.
- Most incidents have not contaminated water or caused sustained outages, but costs are rising as utilities adopt internet-connected technologies, often without adequate safeguards.
- Fiscal exposure is real and documented: a 2019 ransomware attack on Baltimore's municipal systems halted water billing for months and cost the city an estimated $18 million in IT repairs and delayed/lost penalty revenue. An October 2024 cyberattack on American Water, the nation's largest water utility, forced a shutdown of customer billing systems.
- Credit rating agencies and municipal bond analysts are starting to weigh cyber resilience as a factor in a utility's financial position, per Fitch Ratings and Municipal Market Analytics commentary.
- **Maryland's regulatory baseline:** the Modernize Maryland Act of 2022 requires water and wastewater systems serving more than 10,000 users and receiving state funding to assess cyber vulnerability, develop cybersecurity plans, and report findings to the General Assembly. The state's 2024 Cybersecurity Action Plan for Water and Wastewater Systems built on this, and 2025 legislation expanded the requirement to systems serving more than 3,300 customers. As of 2024, over 85% of Maryland residents were served by systems that had completed cybersecurity assessments.
- The Maryland Association of Counties warned the 2025 expansion would place an "untenable fiscal burden" on counties already facing workforce shortages and hiring freezes.
- State estimates suggest compliance could cost up to $150,000/year for utilities serving 3,300-50,000 people, and up to $5 million/year for larger systems.
- Funding sources cited: the federal State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program (set to expire in September, renewal uncertain), Maryland's Local Cybersecurity Support Fund (covers the state match), EPA/CISA's joint water and wastewater cybersecurity toolkit, and State Revolving Funds that can finance vulnerability assessments and upgrades but don't fully cover costs.
- New York's approach (cited as a peer model): a 2025 coordinated multiagency framework aligning Department of Health and Department of Environmental Conservation rules, paired with a $2.5 million state grant program that had awarded over $9 million to 153 local projects by August 2026.

## Relevance
Frederick's own water, sewer, and stormwater infrastructure falls under the same Maryland regulatory framework (Modernize Maryland Act of 2022 and its 2025 expansion) if the city's system crosses the 3,300-customer threshold, which it almost certainly does. This is a fiscal and procurement question as much as a technical one: Frederick should confirm with CTO Matt Bowman and Public Works whether the City's water/wastewater cybersecurity assessments and plans are current and budgeted, and whether the City is positioned to draw on the federal State and Local Cybersecurity Grant Program before it potentially lapses. The Baltimore and American Water examples are useful cautionary citations for any future municipal cybersecurity budget or procurement discussion.
