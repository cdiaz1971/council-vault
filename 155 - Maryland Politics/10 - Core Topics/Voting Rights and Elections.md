# Voting Rights and Elections

## Purpose

This page supports Council decisions and positions on voting rights, redistricting, and election methods, from federal law down to how the City elects its own Council. City voter eligibility and referendum rules are in [[Civic Participation and Co-Governance]].

## Federal voting rights after Callais

- In *Louisiana v. Callais* (April 2026), the Supreme Court struck down Louisiana's congressional map with two majority-Black districts and narrowed Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act to require a strong inference of intentional discrimination. Justice Kagan's dissent called Section 2 "all but a dead letter" ([SCOTUSblog](https://www.scotusblog.com/2026/04/in-major-voting-rights-act-case-supreme-court-strikes-down-redistricting-map-challenged-as-racia/); [[SCOTUS Louisiana Redistricting -- Voting Rights Act Narrowed]]).
- Fair Fight projected up to 19 additional safe Republican House seats from new Southern maps ([[Louisiana v Callais SCOTUS Guts Voting Rights Act Section 2]]). Federal courts are no longer the main arena for challenging minority vote dilution ([[Callais Ruling Guts Section 2 and Ends the Courts as the Primary Arena]]).
- A federal court ruled the administration's SAVE voter database illegal under the Privacy Act and ordered it dismantled; States had used it to purge rolls, wrongly flagging citizens as noncitizens ([[SAVE Voter Database Ruled Illegal -- Federal Court Orders Dismantling]]).

## Maryland Voting Rights Act (2026)

The Maryland Voting Rights Act of 2026 (SB 255, Chapter 157, effective April 28, 2026) prohibits county and municipal election methods that impair a protected class's ability to elect candidates of its choice or influence elections by diluting or abridging their votes. The Attorney General or private parties may sue, with injunctive relief and damages available (Election Law §§ 15.7-101 to 15.7-107; [SB 255](https://mgaleg.maryland.gov/mgawebsite/Legislation/Details/sb0255?ys=2026RS)). A companion bill covering voter intimidation, deception, and suppression of protected-class voters (HB 219) did not pass in the 2026 session, so those protections are not yet part of Maryland law ([[Maryland Voting Rights Act of 2026 -- Passage and Political Context]]).

**For the City:** Council has five district seats and two at-large seats (Charter Art. II, § 1.1). At-large seats are a common target of vote-dilution claims. Frederick's growing Latino and Black communities make the question live ([[Callais VRA Ruling and Frederick Minority Voting Power]]). Counsel should assess exposure before the next redistricting.

## Maryland redistricting

- A 2022 State court ruling applied compactness standards to congressional maps. After *Callais*, the General Assembly held a special session August 3 to 5, 2026 and passed HB 2100, a constitutional amendment providing that nothing in the State Constitution sets criteria for congressional districts (House 96-38, Senate 32-13) ([[Maryland Redistricting Constitutional Amendment Passes Special Session with Legal Challenge Looming]]).
- Lower courts blocked it from the ballot; on September 3, 2026 the Maryland Supreme Court reversed and kept it on the ballot with revised wording ([WYPR](https://www.wypr.org/wypr-news/2026-09-03/maryland-supreme-court-rules-redistricting-question-can-stay-on-november-ballot-with-small-tweaks)). It appears as Question 3 on November 3, 2026 ([State Board of Elections](https://www.elections.maryland.gov/elections/2026/2026_Ballot_Question_SBE_Letter.pdf)).

## Election methods

- **Ranked choice voting.** 49 U.S. jurisdictions used RCV as of March 2026, reaching about 14 million voters; FairVote reports turnout gains, including 9.6 points in Twin Cities mayoral races, and majority winners without runoffs ([[Ranked Choice Voting Increases Voter Turnout -- FairVote Research Summary]]). Washington, D.C. used RCV for the first time in June 2026; Honolulu, Irvine, and Longmont put it on November ballots ([[FairVote -- RCV Results in Washington DC and Maine 2026 Primaries]]; [[Ranked Choice Voting Momentum July 2026]]). Maryland's crowded 2026 primaries produced plurality winners, including a 32% winner in the Fifth District and a 45% gubernatorial nominee ([[Maryland's 2026 Primary Results as Evidence for Ranked Choice Voting]]).
- **Primaries.** Maryland's primaries are closed to about one million unaffiliated voters; a lawsuit challenges public funding of closed primaries, and the Governor called the system outdated before his office walked it back ([[Governor Moore on Open Primaries -- Signal Without Commitment]]).

## Election administration

- The Board of Public Works approved a $109 million voting system contract with ES&S over objections that certifications were incomplete ([[Maryland Board of Public Works Approves $109M ES&S Voting Machine Contract]]).
- A vendor sent some voters the wrong party's mail-in primary ballot; the State resent ballots to all 500,000-plus mail-in voters, and the error was used to attack confidence in Maryland elections ([[Maryland Mail-In Ballot Vendor Error -- June 2026 Primary Resend]]; [[Trump Election Integrity Attacks as Democratic Resilience Pattern -- Maryland Case]]).
