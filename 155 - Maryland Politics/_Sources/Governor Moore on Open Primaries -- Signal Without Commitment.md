# Governor Moore on Open Primaries -- Signal Without Commitment

## Summary

In an HBO interview with Bill Maher, Gov. Wes Moore expressed the view that Maryland's closed primary system should be "reevaluated" because it disenfranchises the state's roughly 1 million unaffiliated voters. His spokesperson immediately walked the comment back, characterizing it as a theoretical musing rather than a policy position. The episode highlights a real tension in Maryland's electoral structure as both unaffiliated registration and political dissatisfaction with party primaries grow.

## Key Points

The context: Maryland has over 2.2 million registered Democrats, just over 1 million Republicans, and nearly 1 million unaffiliated voters -- none of whom can vote in a primary under the current closed system. Of 22,000 new March registrations, 7,847 were unaffiliated -- nearly as many as Democrats. Moore described independents as "the fastest-growing political party in this country."

The lawsuit: Five unaffiliated voters (Bryson, et al v. Moore, et al) are not asking to open primaries outright -- they're asking the courts to prevent the state from spending public money on an election process that excludes them. An Anne Arundel County Circuit Court dismissed the case in November; the appeal is now before the Maryland Appellate Court with briefs due in May.

The legislative record: Sen. Cheryl Kagan (D-Montgomery) sponsored SB 132, which would have allowed unaffiliated voters to re-register with a party up to primary election day. The House passed its version; both died in the Senate Education, Energy and Environment Committee. Kagan believes the bill has a better chance next session, particularly given Moore's comments.

The irony: Moore pushed this session for mid-cycle redistricting to make the state's only Republican congressional seat more competitive -- a maneuver that would further entrench the value of the primary for incumbents of the controlling party.

## Relevance

Maryland electoral structure matters for Frederick's civic participation agenda. If unaffiliated voters -- nearly a million statewide -- continue to feel their voices don't count until November, that structural alienation shapes turnout, civic engagement, and the political coalition available to progressive municipal officials. The Kagan bill's framework (allow re-registration through election day) is the most plausible incremental reform. Worth tracking whether the appellate case creates any new leverage.
