# Maryland SEIF Diversion and Renewable Compliance Payments Drive Electricity Costs

## Summary
A Maryland Matters op-ed argues the state's energy affordability crisis is self-inflicted: renewable mandates outpacing in-state supply force Alternative Compliance Payments that ratepayers fund, and Annapolis has diverted over $500 million of that money from the Strategic Energy Investment Fund to plug budget gaps.

## Key Points
- Maryland residential electricity prices rose nearly 40% from January 2023 to June 2026, one of the steepest increases nationally and the highest among neighboring states. Marylanders now pay the third-highest residential rates in the country. Even after the Utility RELIEF Act passed this year, legislators concede the work is unfinished.
- **Mechanism.** The Renewable Portfolio Standard requires suppliers to buy growing quantities of renewable energy credits. When not enough qualifying credits exist, suppliers pay Alternative Compliance Payments instead, and those costs pass straight through to ratepayers.
- **SEIF numbers.** ACPs flow into the Strategic Energy Investment Fund, created in 2008 for efficiency, renewable generation, and ratepayer relief. SEIF payments went from $86.6 million in 2022 to $362.3 million in 2024, and budget officials project $336.9 million in each of FY2026 and FY2027. DLS expects them to stay elevated until more qualifying renewable capacity comes online.
- **Diversion.** Over the past two years, more than $500 million has been moved out of SEIF as what state budget analysts call budget relief, funding deficits instead of renewable generation or rate relief.
- **Author's prescription.** An all-of-the-above in-state generation strategy, renewable requirements pegged to the renewable capacity actually coming online, and keeping energy-policy revenue dedicated to generation, reliability, and ratepayer relief.

## Relevance
The author, Randy Altschuler, is a businessman and Republican political figure, and the piece is a partisan framing of state energy policy rather than neutral analysis. It should be read as the opposition's affordability message heading into 2026 and 2027. Two parts hold up regardless of politics and are useful to César: the SEIF diversion figure is a budget-integrity argument a progressive can make too (ratepayer money collected for relief should go to relief), and the price trend matters directly to the ALICE affordability baseline, where utility costs compound housing burden for the same households. Also relevant context if data center load growth comes up in the city moratorium debate, since new demand is one of the pressures on regional prices.
