# Maryland Special Session Needed for Redistricting and Democracy

## Summary

Op-ed arguing Maryland must call a special session to address redistricting in the wake of the Callais decision, and should bundle special elections reform onto the November ballot simultaneously. Senate leadership now appears open to a session focused on redistricting, but the author urges legislators not to miss the window for the companion special elections reform that failed only on timing in the regular session.

## Key Points

The Callais ruling gutted Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, and Republican-controlled Southern states are already moving to erase Black majority districts. Maryland needs to respond.

Two redistricting reform proposals advanced by House leadership in the regular session are the targets for the special session: (1) moving redistricting litigation to the Maryland Supreme Court to prevent judge-shopping and allow faster resolution, and (2) giving legislators more legal latitude in drawing new congressional districts. If voters approve these constitutional changes in November, the legislature could confidently draw a new map for 2028 and 2030.

The second thread is special elections reform. Both chambers passed their own versions in the regular session but ran out of time to reconcile. The Maryland proposal addresses the undemocratic nature of typical special elections by tying primaries to the presidential primary date, ensuring high turnout and a campaign of at least four months. The Senate passed its version unanimously the last two years. If the legislature waits until 2027, anyone appointed in the next four-year term faces no serious special election challenge until 2032.

The author frames this as a "single subject" session on elections: put both questions to voters in November, let voters decide.

## Relevance

The Callais decision is directly named in the June 8 Gov Ops brief -- the ACLU and NAACP presentation explicitly names Caesar's election as part of the Frederick minority voting power context. A Maryland special session on redistricting is therefore directly relevant to district composition and political representation in Frederick, particularly in District 2.

The special elections reform thread is worth tracking separately: if it passes, it changes the political calculus for mid-term vacancies on the Frederick City Council and potentially on the County Commission. Worth monitoring whether this surfaces as a charter amendment candidate at the June 8 meeting.
