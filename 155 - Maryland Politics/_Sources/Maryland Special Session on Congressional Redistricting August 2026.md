# Maryland Special Session on Congressional Redistricting August 2026

## Summary

Senate President Bill Ferguson and House Speaker Joseline Pena-Melnyk announced a special session for August 3-5 to consider a constitutional amendment clarifying how congressional districts can be drawn. The session responds to a 2022 ruling by Judge Lynne Battaglia that applied compactness standards previously limited to state legislative districts to congressional maps, as well as post-VRA uncertainty created by recent Supreme Court rulings.

## Key Points

The immediate trigger is a combination of two pressures. First, Ferguson had previously blocked mid-cycle redistricting but changed position after the Supreme Court weakened Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act in May 2026 and after Trump successfully primaryed Indiana Republicans who had blocked redistricting in that state. Second, the Battaglia ruling remains technically non-precedential but Ferguson and allies worried the Moore-appointed Maryland Supreme Court might adopt it, threatening the current 7-1 Democratic map.

The session scope is narrow. The most likely outcome is not new maps but a constitutional amendment for voters to ratify that would undo the Battaglia interpretation and establish clearer standards for future redistricting. New maps would not be enacted in time for the current cycle. Gov. Moore was publicly supportive but walked back earlier demands that new maps be included.

Republican opposition framed the session as a national power play for Moore and an effort to erase the one Republican congressional seat (Justin Ready's district in Frederick and Carroll counties). Senate Minority Whip Justin Ready specifically cited his own district.

Sen. Clarence Lam, an early proponent of mid-cycle redistricting, called the session "an important first step" and framed the constitutional amendment as correcting a judicial overreach by Battaglia rather than partisan gerrymandering.

## Relevance

Frederick is in a Republican congressional district. The redistricting debate touches directly on Frederick community representation at the federal level. The Frederick County sheriff is already in a federal lawsuit against the state over the Community Trust Act, and the county's Republican congressional delegation has been a persistent obstacle to the state's immigrant protection agenda. A redrawn map that placed Frederick in a more competitive or Democratic-leaning district would shift the federal representation environment for the community organizations in Frederick's coalition.

The session is also a test of Ferguson's willingness to act on democratic resilience issues. His initial obstruction of redistricting despite the House majority supporting it is a useful data point on state Senate dynamics going into the 2027 session, which will matter for Real Rent Ordinance preemption questions and other Frederick legislative priorities.

The Battaglia ruling created the same kind of novel judicial risk that the Community Trust Act sheriffs lawsuit represents at the state level: lower-court judges using unsettled precedent to limit legislative authority. Both are worth tracking together.
