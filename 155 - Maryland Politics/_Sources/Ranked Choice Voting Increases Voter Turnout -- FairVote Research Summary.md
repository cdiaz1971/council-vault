## Summary

FairVote's research on ranked choice voting (RCV) and voter turnout shows consistent positive effects: a 9.6-percentage-point increase in turnout for mayoral elections in the Minneapolis-St. Paul metro, higher turnout in NYC's 2025 primaries than any election since 1989, and majority winners without the steep turnout drop that characterizes traditional runoffs. As of March 2026, 49 American jurisdictions use RCV, reaching nearly 14 million voters. César asked this question directly at the June 8 Gov Ops meeting after the ACLU's presentation on voting reform as a post-Callais remedy.

## Key Points

**Turnout Effects**
Research from Minneapolis-St. Paul found RCV produced a 9.6 percentage point increase in mayoral election turnout. NYC's 2025 primaries using RCV saw the highest turnout since 1989. Federal primary runoffs over the last 20 years have seen a median 37% turnout decline between first and final rounds -- RCV eliminates that drop by delivering a majority winner in a single election.

**Outcomes for Candidates of Color**
FairVote research consistently finds that candidates of color are more likely to win RCV elections, particularly in multi-candidate fields. Voters of color rank more candidates on average than white voters, making fuller use of the ballot. RCV reduces the spoiler dynamic that pressures communities of color to vote strategically rather than expressively. This is the dimension most directly relevant to the post-Callais context -- if district lines can no longer reliably protect minority voting power, procedural reforms like RCV become a structural alternative lever.

**Majority Winners Without Runoffs**
In NYC's 2025 Democratic mayoral primary, 95% of voters expressed a preference between the top two candidates. An average of 74% of voters ranked the winning candidates in their top three choices. 76% of NYC voters said they would like to keep or expand RCV. This is the "instant runoff" function: majority legitimacy without a second election that almost always draws a fraction of the original turnout.

**No Evidence of Voter Confusion**
Research by Donovan, Tolbert, and Gracey found no significant racial or ethnic differences in understanding of RCV ballot instructions. Ballot error rates do not increase under RCV. The concern that RCV is too confusing for voters -- especially low-income or minority voters -- is not supported by evidence.

**Scale as of 2026**
49 jurisdictions as of March 2026. Maine has used RCV statewide since 2018. Washington DC used RCV for the first time in its June 16, 2026 primary. The 2026 cycle has multiple high-profile RCV primaries running simultaneously.

## Relevance to Frederick

This note was triggered by César's question at the June 8 Gov Ops meeting during the ACLU's presentation. The ACLU recommended RCV as a post-Callais remedy -- after the Supreme Court gutted Section 2 of the Voting Rights Act, structural electoral reforms become the alternative path for protecting minority voting power that can no longer be secured through district map litigation alone.

The connection to Frederick specifically: District 2 has a majority-minority composition by combined demographics. The at-large and ward structure of Frederick's council elections shapes who can run and who can win. If Maryland pursues electoral reform at the state or local level in response to Callais, RCV is the most evidence-supported option on the table. The ACLU's naming of César's 2025 election explicitly in their slides is relevant context -- his election is being read as evidence of what structural reform makes possible.
