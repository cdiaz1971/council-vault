# Maryland Board of Public Works Approves $109M ES&S Voting Machine Contract

## Summary

The Board of Public Works approved a $109M contract with Election Systems and Software for a new Maryland voting system, with Comptroller Lierman absent and advocates objecting that key certifications are not yet in place -- a risk compounded by Trump's gutting of the federal Elections Assistance Commission.

## Key Points

The Board of Public Works voted 2-0 (Comptroller Brooke Lierman absent) on July 23, 2026 to approve a contract with Omaha-based Election Systems and Software for a new statewide voting system ahead of the 2028 election. The base contract is nearly $85 million, with optional renewals bringing the total to nine years and more than $109 million.

State Elections Administrator Jared DeMarinis cited equipment age as the driver: the current system is more than a decade old, replacement parts are no longer manufactured, and the state is losing machines it cannot replace.

Advocates from Free Speech for People, joined by Common Cause Maryland and Save Our Votes Maryland, pushed for a delay. Their argument: the system is still under development, lacks some certifications, and commits Maryland at a premium price to unproven technology.

The certification structure is the key vulnerability. The system procured must comply with 1.0 standards through the 2028 cycle; compliance with updated 2.0 standards is required only for 2030. That 2.0 certification must come from the four-member federal Elections Assistance Commission -- which Trump has effectively dismantled by firing two Democrats (one Republican resigned, another had already left). Sen. Cheryl Kagan put it plainly: "We are now dependent on the EAC and it's not clear they will be able to certify 2.0 after being decapitated by Donald Trump."

Payments are structured around deliverables, with no prepayments. DeMarinis argued ES&S's reputation in the nationwide vendor market is itself an enforcement mechanism.

## Relevance

Maryland's election infrastructure is increasingly exposed on two fronts: aging equipment forcing rushed procurement, and a decapitated federal certification body creating downstream legal and operational uncertainty for the 2030 election cycle. This is worth tracking as a democratic resilience issue. The 2-0 vote with Lierman absent also illustrates how Board of Public Works dynamics can shape major state decisions with reduced deliberation.
