# Moore Names Will Tilburg Chief Legislative Officer

## Summary

Governor Moore replaced his chief legislative officer ahead of a likely second term, bringing in Will Tilburg (former cannabis regulator) as his new Annapolis liaison amid a documented rift with Senate President Ferguson and looming budget and tax pressures.

## Key Points

Will Tilburg, former director of the Maryland Cannabis Administration and most recently VP of government and regulatory affairs at the University of Maryland Medical System, will become Moore's chief legislative officer on August 19. He replaces Jeremy Baker, who moves to become acting director of the Maryland Lottery and Gaming Control Agency.

Both transitions happen after the upcoming special General Assembly session (called for August 2026 on congressional redistricting).

**The context for this hire matters:**
- A documented Moore-legislature rift, most visibly with Senate President Bill Ferguson, stemming from the midcycle redistricting fight and Moore's failure to endorse Ferguson in the primary
- Looming second-term agenda challenges: Blueprint for Maryland's Future funding pressures, possible sales tax increase debate, and questions about Moore's national political ambitions distracting from state governance
- Lottery agency complications Baker inherits: a disputed 10-year lottery management contract still in legal limbo, and pressure to expand gaming revenue for Blueprint funding

Tilburg's reputation is for good lawmaker relationships and for bringing technical expertise rather than political theater. Del. C.T. Wilson called him "a phenomenal addition" specifically because of the current legislative-executive tension.

Baker's record: known for making legislators look good rather than taking credit himself; built the transportation project scoring rubric with Sen. Beidle in 2016.

## Relevance

This personnel move is worth tracking because Tilburg's success or failure at bridging the Moore-legislature rift will shape the second-term legislative environment for priorities that intersect with Frederick's interests: Blueprint education funding (which directly affects city budget dynamics), tax structure, and any state housing or tenant protection legislation César might pursue through state allies.

The framing of Moore as potentially distracted by 2028 presidential ambitions is now on the record from multiple legislators -- that shapes how much bandwidth Moore's office will have for local government partnerships.
