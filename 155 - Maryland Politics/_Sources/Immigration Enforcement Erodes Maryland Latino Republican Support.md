# Immigration Enforcement Erodes Maryland Latino Republican Support

## Summary

Maryland Latino Republican leaders say aggressive ICE enforcement is pushing some Hispanic conservatives away from Trump, with the likelier outcome being that they stay home rather than vote Democratic. Nationally, Hispanic GOP support has fallen from 48% in 2024 to 26% in an October 2026 poll, while Maryland ICE arrests more than doubled early this year.

## Key Points

Pew found Trump won 48% of Hispanic voters in 2024. A Reuters/Ipsos poll completed October 5, 2026 found only 26% of Hispanic registered voters planning to vote Republican in November, with 52% planning to back Democrats.

In Maryland, a CNS analysis of Deportation Data Project figures found ICE arrests more than doubled between January 20 and March 10, 2026 compared with the same period a year earlier.

Juan Leo Salazar, president of the Latin Republican Organization of Maryland, says the administration is overdoing it and that many Latinos are moving away from the party. Gabriela Lemus of the nonpartisan Maryland Latinos Unidos says some Latinos who voted for Trump on the economy now regret it after what she calls punitive actions, and that conservative Latinos uncomfortable with Democrats may simply not vote.

Not everyone agrees. Alfonso Talavera, who chairs the Maryland GOP Hispanic and Latino Outreach Committee, defends enforcement as applying the law, comparing it to a DUI stop. Some Latino Republican candidates are dodging the issue entirely. Josephine Salazar, running for Montgomery County Council District 5, left immigration off her platform, though she privately supports a legal path for long-settled, tax-paying immigrants.

Latino Republicans describe the original draw to the GOP as family values and security, and cite Reagan's 1986 legalization as part of the party's appeal. The economy is the other major issue. A Maryland Latinos Unidos survey last fall found families choosing between food and utility bills, and leaders across parties agree families are stretched.

Maryland's Hispanic and Latino population exceeds 830,000, with roughly 39% eligible to vote per Pew.

## Relevance

The demobilization story is the important part. The leaders interviewed expect disaffected Latino conservatives to sit out rather than switch, which means the enforcement climate depresses Latino civic participation across the board, not just Republican turnout. That matters for Frederick's Latino residents and for any candidate or organization counting on Latino turnout in 2026.

The Latino Republicans' own framing, that long-settled, working, tax-paying immigrants deserve a legal path, is common ground César can use when making the case for the [[City Values Act]] to moderate and conservative audiences.

Maryland Latinos Unidos and its economic survey are worth knowing as a potential data partner on affordability and immigrant community needs.
