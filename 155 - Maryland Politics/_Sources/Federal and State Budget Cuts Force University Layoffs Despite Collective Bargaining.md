# Federal and State Budget Cuts Force University Layoffs Despite Collective Bargaining

## Summary

AFSCME Council 3 rallied more than 100 members outside the University System of Maryland Board of Regents meeting in June 2026 to challenge layoffs at UMD College Park (73 members), Bowie State (21 members), and UMBC. USM leadership attributed the cuts to a 10% reduction in state base funding over two budget cycles, federal research funding cuts, and inflation. Senate President Bill Ferguson tied the moment to a November ballot referendum on binding arbitration for state employees.

## Key Points

The layoffs at UMD College Park included an integrated care specialist whose job was to help students prevent suicide -- a position that illustrates the human cost of treating staff reductions as purely budgetary rather than service delivery decisions. "I had no opportunity to transition services, very unethical from a licensed perspective," she said.

AFSCME President Patrick Moran challenged USM's reasoning by pointing to large endowments and asking why vendor contracts were not scrutinized before cutting union workers: "Would you cut the basketball coaches or the football coaches pay after you have a contract?"

USM's position: a 1.5% cost-of-living increase was provided system-wide, but no additional merit pay was funded by the state. The system acknowledged it is "facing budget challenges" from lower state base funding, reduced federal research funding, and inflation.

The Arbitration Reform for State Employees Act (SB 28, 2026), sponsored by Sen. Cory McCray, would require binding neutral arbitration in labor-management disputes with state workers. Senate President Ferguson said voters will decide in November and the law would prevent layoffs like this "in the near future." This is a significant expansion of collective bargaining protections if it passes.

## Relevance

This story connects directly to the Collective Bargaining with Binding Arbitration thread (the Nash-authored legislation circulating for feedback). The Maryland Arbitration Reform Act going to voters in November is the state-level analog to what Nash's bill would establish locally. If the state measure passes, it creates political momentum and a legal framework that could strengthen the case for the city's own binding arbitration legislation.

The AFSCME challenge also illustrates a limit of collective bargaining without strong anti-layoff provisions: even with union contracts in place, budget-driven mass layoffs can proceed. Binding arbitration is one protection, but the USM case suggests that the deeper problem is the structure of state funding itself. For Frederick, this is relevant context for the Priority-Driven Budgeting thread -- how budget decisions about staffing levels interact with labor relations and what the council's leverage points actually are.
